Two measurements in this paper constrain how a rule-grounded metabolite predictor should be built,
and neither of them is a statement about the model that produced them. This appendix states the
design they point at and marks the boundary between what is measured here and what is proposed.

\paragraph{What is measured.} Emitting a set sized to the candidate pool beats the best global
constant on frozen scores by $+0.0147$ $[+0.0059,+0.0237]$ macro F1, which is four fifths of what an
oracle count would take over the same constant (Appendix~\ref{app:emission}). Against the deployed
budget the margin is $+0.074$, but that is the arm \S\ref{sec:metric} discredits and it is not what
this design rests on. Forecasting the count from the substrate's own descriptors is worse than the
constant it replaces (Appendix~\ref{app:negative}). So the number of metabolites a substrate has is
not recoverable from the substrate; it is recoverable from the distribution of scores over the
candidates the substrate generates.

\paragraph{What that rules out.} A scorer that maps a (substrate, product) pair to an independent
probability cannot express it. Its output for one candidate does not change when a better sibling
appears, so the size of the emitted set has to come from a threshold or a budget, and both are
global objects for a quantity that is not global. The deployed filter here is such a scorer, and the
$+0.0147$ over the best constant it cannot express is the price.

\paragraph{What it points at.} Score the candidates of one substrate against each other. Encode the
substrate once, score each candidate from the atom it fires at and the rule that fires, and take a
softmax over the pool rather than a sigmoid over each pair, with one extra logit for emitting
nothing further. Training is then a listwise loss over the pool: the negative log of the normalised
score, averaged over the annotated products, which asks the model to rank a substrate's true
products above its own alternatives rather than to calibrate an absolute probability for each. That
matches what this corpus can supply. Labels here are positive-unlabelled, so an absolute probability
has no ground truth to be calibrated against, while a relative order does.

\paragraph{Why the flow machinery is not needed.} Metabolism is a directed acyclic graph and the
obvious tool for generating one is a flow network trained by trajectory balance, which exists to
solve credit assignment when only the terminal state is rewarded. That is not this problem. The
annotations here are edges: a triple names a substrate and one of its direct products, and a
metabolite that is itself metabolised appears again as a substrate, which is how $8.4\%$ of the
products in the training split do appear. Supervision is therefore dense at every node of the graph,
and the objective can be a per-node quantity. The graph follows by recursion: apply the pool softmax
at the root, expand the candidates that carry mass, and stop where the extra logit does, which makes
depth a learned quantity rather than the fixed one every system here uses.

\paragraph{What is not claimed.} None of this is trained or evaluated in this paper. The emission
rule is measured, the listwise objective is not; a model trained that way could fail for reasons the
frozen-score experiment cannot see, and the honest status of the paragraph above is a design that
two measurements make plausible and no measurement here confirms.
