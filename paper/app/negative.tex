The main text locates the dominant recall shortfall at rule selection. A natural question is
whether the granularity of the rule bank itself is responsible: the bank is assembled from several
published sources and by automated mining, so it may contain functionally redundant rules that
dilute the selection signal, and rules that never contribute and merely enlarge the label space.
We tested three hypotheses of this kind. Each was specified together with the observation that
would refute it, before the measurement was run, and none survived. They are reported here because
the question arises naturally from the decomposition, and because two of the three outcomes
constrain what this architecture can be expected to achieve.

\subsection{Redundancy in the rule bank}

Redundancy is present and measurable. Applying the full bank to a sample of 1{,}000 substrates,
2{,}747 rules fire at least once, and these fall into 2{,}056 distinct classes when rules are
grouped by the exact set of substrate-product pairs they generate. A quarter of the firing rules
are therefore functional duplicates of another rule on this chemistry. Splitting that sample in
half and re-deriving the grouping on each half preserves 97.5\% of the equivalences, so the
grouping is not an artefact of the particular substrates sampled. A second, distribution-free
procedure that canonicalises each reaction template and removes exact duplicates eliminates 7.5\% of the whole bank, with permutation variants of a single template accounting for roughly
43\% of that redundancy and non-structural equivalences for the remainder.

Whether removing this redundancy would improve selection is not established. Training marks every
rule that produces an annotated metabolite as positive, and each rule carries its own embedding,
so duplicated rules divide the positive signal for one transformation among several parameters.
Against this, the noisy-or aggregation used at inference pools the duplicated scores again, which
partially restores what training divided. The ablation reported below shows in addition that the
per-rule embedding component is not load-bearing, which removes the mechanism by which the divided
signal would matter. We therefore treat deduplication as a housekeeping measure affecting startup
cost and memory, not as a route to better accuracy.

\subsection{Rules that never contribute}

Across the 4{,}787 training substrates, 56.3\% of the bank is never a positive label, and a
further 20.1\% is positive for exactly one substrate. Roughly three quarters of the
7{,}581-dimensional target is therefore either inert or supported by a single example, which is
consistent with the extreme multi-label characterisation in the main text and suggests that the
effective sparsity of the learning problem is reducible.

It does not follow that the corresponding rules can be removed. We tested removal directly, by
discarding the 4{,}271 rules that are never a positive label in training, which reduces the bank
from 7{,}581 to 3{,}310, and re-ranking on the validation split. Performance falls on both axes.
Recall@15 declines from 0.388 to 0.352 (paired difference $-0.036$, 95\% confidence
interval $[-0.050,-0.024]$), and the fraction of references present anywhere in the generated pool
declines from 0.439 to 0.378 ($-0.061$, $[-0.078,-0.046]$); both intervals exclude zero. The mean
pool size falls from 46.9 to 40.0 candidates.

Rules carrying no positive label in training therefore still generate correct metabolites for
substrates outside the training distribution. What appears as dead weight in the training target
is in part generalisation capacity held in the tail of the bank. The distinction governs how the
earlier statistic should be read: three quarters of the \emph{target} is inert during training,
but the bank is not correspondingly prunable, and treating the two as equivalent removes coverage
on which the deployed system depends.

\subsection{The per-rule embedding component}

Each rule's representation is the sum of a graph encoding of its reaction template, a learned
per-rule embedding indexed by position in the bank, and a small vector of structural features.
Decomposing the variance across rules attributes 82\% to the per-rule embedding and 17 per
cent to the graph encoding, which suggests that the model treats the bank largely as a lookup
table. On that reading the rare rules, whose embeddings receive almost no gradient, would
contribute noise to the selection score, and the extreme multi-label pathology would have an
architectural explanation.

That reading does not hold. Setting the per-rule embedding to zero at inference, which removes the
component carrying 82\% of the variance, changes recall@15 by $-0.007$ on the test split.
Repeating the ablation on the validation split with paired bootstrap intervals over 994 substrates
gives no significant change at any cut-off: $+0.0011$ $[-0.0086,+0.0107]$ at $k=1$, $+0.0055$
$[-0.0074,+0.0181]$ at $k=5$, $-0.0116$ $[-0.0266,+0.0036]$ at $k=15$, and $-0.0119$
$[-0.0282,+0.0044]$ at $k=30$. Dominance of a component in the variance of a representation does
not imply that the component carries the decision. A single test-split run on 291 substrates had
indicated that removing the embedding improved recall at small $k$ ($+0.037$ at $k=1$,
$+0.027$ at $k=5$); that effect did not replicate on validation and is not reported as a result.

\subsection{Dispatching the hydrogen convention per template}
\label{app:dispatch}

\S\ref{app:enginereach} shows that what a bank reaches depends on a convention its engine applies
globally, and that banks disagree about which convention they want. The obvious repair is to stop
having a global one and apply each template under the convention it was written in. The policy was
fixed before anything was measured (a template gets the expanded substrate if and only if its
reactant side carries a bracket-hydrogen atom, blind to the product side, to the hydrogen-count
primitive and to negations, with the $238$ templates whose recursive \textsc{smarts} that test
cannot see inside taking their own bank's majority convention) because a policy chosen after
seeing which setting wins would beat any global setting by construction and would measure nothing.

The quantity that decides the claim is the residual rather than the win: reach under dispatch minus
the better of the two global settings. A residual at zero says the bank is single-convention and
choosing correctly is the whole story. Above zero says no global setting expresses the bank.

\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
bank & rules & dispatched & dispatch & best global & residual \\
\midrule
SyGMa          & $175$    & $0$   & 0.535 $[0.510,0.561]$ & 0.535 & $+0.000$ $[0.000,0.000]$ \\
ours           & $7{,}581$ & $675$ & 0.8229 $[0.8009,0.8445]$ & 0.8194 & $+0.0035$ $[0.0015,0.0058]$ \\
BioTransformer & $994$    & $726$ & 0.4925 $[0.4669,0.5182]$ & 0.4963 & $-0.0038$ $[-0.0067,-0.0015]$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

One prediction was structural and two hold, and what the residual is measured against decides the
second of those. SyGMa is the structural null: none of its templates is dispatched, so dispatch is
the identity map and has to reproduce its implicit-hydrogen arm to four decimals, which it does,
and that is the check that the classifier is not sending templates somewhere it should not.
BioTransformer was predicted to clear its better global setting, because $726$ of its $994$
templates want the convention a single global arm would deny them: the $690$ that name a hydrogen
atom on their reactant side, and the $36$ recursive patterns the classifier cannot see inside, which
take the bank's majority convention by the rule registered before the run. It does not clear it. It
loses by $-0.0038$ $[-0.0067,-0.0015]$, a paired difference measured in one pass over the same
substrates rather than a gap between marginals, which is the wrong test for a within-bank contrast.
Those templates do add products, and the products are real; almost none of them recovers a reference
these substrates carry. Our own bank does clear it, by $+0.0035$ $[+0.0015,+0.0058]$. Every bank in
the table records the revision that measured it and a released check refuses a citation of one whose
revision does not match the others, because an entry produced by an earlier revision of the loop is
exactly the mixture this appendix is about.

What ``better global setting'' means decides the second result, and getting it wrong is how this
prediction was first reported as holding. An arm that expands the substrate and never contracts the
product is not a setting anyone would choose; \S\ref{sec:enginereach} calls it an unfinished loop.
Two defects followed from it. Measured against that arm, the residual is a comparison with a program
that has a bug in it. And the dispatch arm itself was built from the uncontracted half of its own
enumeration, so a repaired control was being compared against an unrepaired treatment. With both
sides completed the prediction fails, which is what the appendix reported before either defect was
found, though not for the reason it gave. A comparison in which one side has been repaired and the
other has not measures the repair.

The conclusion is close to the recommendation \S\ref{sec:decomp} already makes: declare the
convention. Dispatching it per template is not worth having on either published bank tested here,
being the identity on the single-convention one and a loss on the mixed one, and it pays only on
ours. The failure is the useful half: a bank can be mixed in its syntax and single-convention in
what it reaches, and the one bank where dispatch pays is the one whose $675$ dispatched templates
were written by hand for it. For a bank written in one convention there is
nothing for a cleverer engine to recover, and SyGMa is the demonstration that the classifier knows
the difference. Where our own residual comes from was registered as a further prediction and that
one failed: it is not created by the bank being mixed but by the $675$ hand-written templates that
want an expansion a global setting denies them, and it is \emph{larger} on those templates alone
than on the bank containing them (Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}).

\subsection{Evaluation caveat}

The exploratory measurements in this appendix were scored on the test split before the discipline
described in the main text was tightened; the pruning and ablation results were subsequently
repeated on validation, and those are the values reported above. They support no claim in the main
text.
\paragraph{Estimating a leaderboard's fragility from the published table alone.} The instrument of
\S\ref{sec:robust} needs released prediction files, and most tables come with none. A pair reorders
when a choice moves the two systems apart by more than the margin between them, and the margins are
printed, so the share of pairs whose margin falls below a domain-level movement should predict the
share that reorder. It does not. On the two retrosynthesis leaderboards the threshold rule predicts
$21$ fragile pairs against $16$ observed, and replacing the single movement with its own
distribution and summing the expected count gives $20.7$: both over-predict by about a third, and by
the same third on each, because a movement large enough to cross a margin need not be in the
direction that flips it. A calibration factor fitted on two leaderboards would be an overfit, so
none is offered. The instrument needs the predictions, which is one more reason for releasing them.

\paragraph{Forecasting how many metabolites a substrate has, from the substrate.} If the emitted
set should be sized per substrate, the obvious way is to predict the size. Least squares on heavy
atoms, rings and rotatable bonds, fitted on the training split and applied to test, reaches $0.181$
macro F1 against the deployed policy's $0.136$ -- and against $0.195$ for simply emitting one
candidate to every substrate. The forecast is worse than the constant it was meant to beat, so the
count is not recoverable from the substrate's own descriptors. What works is relative: reading the
candidate pool rather than the molecule (\S\ref{sec:metric}).
