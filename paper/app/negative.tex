The main text locates the dominant recall shortfall at rule selection. A natural question is
whether the granularity of the rule bank itself is responsible: the bank is assembled from several
published sources and by automated mining, so it may contain functionally redundant rules that
dilute the selection signal, and rules that never contribute and merely enlarge the label space.
We tested three hypotheses of this kind. Each was specified together with the observation that
would refute it, before the measurement was run, and none survived. They are reported here because
the question arises naturally from the decomposition, and because two of the three outcomes
constrain what future work on this architecture can expect.

\subsection{Redundancy in the rule bank}

Redundancy is present and measurable. Applying the full bank to a sample of 1{,}000 substrates,
2{,}747 rules fire at least once, and these fall into 2{,}056 distinct classes when rules are
grouped by the exact set of substrate-product pairs they generate. A quarter of the firing rules
are therefore functional duplicates of another rule on this chemistry. Splitting that sample in
half and re-deriving the grouping on each half preserves 97.5\% of the equivalences, so the
grouping is not an artefact of the particular substrates sampled. A second, distribution-free
procedure that canonicalises each reaction template and removes exact duplicates eliminates 7.5\% of the whole bank, with permutation variants of a single template accounting for roughly
43\% of that redundancy and non-structural equivalences for the remainder.

Whether removing this redundancy would improve selection is not established. Training marks every
rule that produces an annotated metabolite as positive, and each rule carries its own embedding,
so duplicated rules divide the positive signal for one transformation among several parameters.
Against this, the noisy-or aggregation used at inference pools the duplicated scores again, which
partially restores what training divided. The ablation reported below shows in addition that the
per-rule embedding component is not load-bearing, which removes the mechanism by which the divided
signal would matter. We therefore treat deduplication as a housekeeping measure affecting startup
cost and memory rather than as a route to better accuracy.

\subsection{Rules that never contribute}

Across the 4{,}787 training substrates, 56.3\% of the bank is never a positive label, and a
further 20.1\% is positive for exactly one substrate. Roughly three quarters of the
7{,}581-dimensional target is therefore either inert or supported by a single example, which is
consistent with the extreme multi-label characterisation in the main text and suggests that the
effective sparsity of the learning problem is reducible.

It does not follow that the corresponding rules can be removed. We tested removal directly, by
discarding the 4{,}271 rules that are never a positive label in training, which reduces the bank
from 7{,}581 to 3{,}310, and re-ranking on the validation split. Performance falls on both axes.
Recall@15 declines from 0.388 to 0.352 (paired difference $-0.036$, 95\% confidence
interval $[-0.050,-0.024]$), and the fraction of references present anywhere in the generated pool
declines from 0.439 to 0.378 ($-0.061$, $[-0.078,-0.046]$); both intervals exclude zero. The mean
pool size falls from 46.9 to 40.0 candidates.

Rules carrying no positive label in training therefore still generate correct metabolites for
substrates outside the training distribution. What appears as dead weight in the training target
is in part generalisation capacity held in the tail of the bank. The distinction governs how the
earlier statistic should be read: three quarters of the \emph{target} is inert during training,
but the bank is not correspondingly prunable, and treating the two as equivalent removes coverage
on which the deployed system depends.

\subsection{The per-rule embedding component}

Each rule's representation is the sum of a graph encoding of its reaction template, a learned
per-rule embedding indexed by position in the bank, and a small vector of structural features.
Decomposing the variance across rules attributes 82\% to the per-rule embedding and 17 per
cent to the graph encoding, which suggests that the model treats the bank largely as a lookup
table. On that reading the rare rules, whose embeddings receive almost no gradient, would
contribute noise to the selection score, and the extreme multi-label pathology would have an
architectural explanation.

That reading does not hold. Setting the per-rule embedding to zero at inference, which removes the
component carrying 82\% of the variance, changes recall@15 by $-0.007$ on the test split.
Repeating the ablation on the validation split with paired bootstrap intervals over 994 substrates
gives no significant change at any cut-off: $+0.0011$ $[-0.0086,+0.0107]$ at $k=1$, $+0.0055$
$[-0.0074,+0.0181]$ at $k=5$, $-0.0116$ $[-0.0266,+0.0036]$ at $k=15$, and $-0.0119$
$[-0.0282,+0.0044]$ at $k=30$. Dominance of a component in the variance of a representation does
not imply that the component carries the decision. A single test-split run on 291 substrates had
indicated that removing the embedding improved precision at small $k$ ($+0.037$ at $k=1$,
$+0.027$ at $k=5$); that effect did not
replicate on validation, and we do not report it as a result.

\subsection{Evaluation caveat}

The exploratory measurements in this appendix were scored on the test split before the discipline
described in the main text was tightened; the pruning and ablation results were subsequently
repeated on validation, and those are the values reported above. They support no claim in the main
text.
