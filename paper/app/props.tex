\subsection{Coverage composition, data scale, and regioselectivity}

Three of the nine measurements are reported here; the remaining six are expanded in the
subsections that follow and in Propositions 2 and 3.

\paragraph{Composition of the uncovered set.}
On the full clean test split under the tautomer-aware default, $18.3\%$ of reference metabolites
lie outside what the bank reaches in one step: $475$ of $2{,}597$, the complement of \ceiling.
The misses are a diverse tail rather than one absent rule family. Binning them by the
substrate-to-metabolite mass difference, on a $500$-substrate subset under strict \textsc{inchikey}
matching where that binning was run, the largest bin holds $6.0\%$ of the uncovered set. We
report the distribution, not named reaction classes, because a mass difference does not
identify a transformation: the same $+14$ is produced by an N-methylation and by an oxidation of a
methylene to a carbonyl. Proposition 3 types
the gap by reaction type and bounds how much of it a more general template could close.

\paragraph{Training-set size.}
Recall moves from 0.330 to 0.334 between 2{,}418 and 4{,}787 training substrates. Both are ablation
runs rather than the deployed checkpoint, so neither is $\grailmacro$ and neither should be read
against it. They are also two single runs, not a paired comparison, so no interval is quoted; the change is smaller than the
$\pm0.010$ spread the same configuration shows across three seeds, which is the sense in which it
is flat. Doubling the number of substrates does not move selection, which rules out under-training as the
explanation for the shortfall. Proposition 2 accounts for that: with one to three rules firing per
substrate over a label space of $\nbank$, more substrates add little to the per-rule positive counts
a learned selector would need.

\paragraph{Site-of-metabolism prior.}
Weighting candidates by a learned prior over which site of the substrate is metabolised lifts
recall@15 from 0.147 to 0.158 on 245 test substrates. The weight was the best of five values tried
on that same split, and the response to it is not monotone, so $+0.011$ is an upper bound on what
this signal is worth rather than an estimate of it. Ordering candidates regioselectively within the
bank remains hard, and we did not isolate why.

The number of rules the generator selects is the pipeline's most direct control over the
recall--precision trade. Sweeping it on the deployed pipeline, with everything else held fixed,
separates selection breadth from the two neighbouring factors.

Widening the selector from 30 to 300 rules lifts recall@15 by 0.061 and pool coverage by 0.126,
at 8.6 times the pool size. This is recall bought with precision, the same trade SyGMa makes by
overproducing. The gain saturates: even when essentially every applicable rule is
fired, the fraction of substrates with at least one reference somewhere in the pool plateaus at
$0.608$, and recall@15 at $0.413$ remains far below SyGMa's $0.572$. Those two are not a ratio (the first counts substrates, the second references) so what the pair shows is that widening the
selector stops helping well before either is exhausted.
That is not the deployed ranking factor, which is measured on the narrower pool the deployed
selector produces and is close to one: widening the selector fills the pool faster than the
fifteen output slots can carry, so what selection recovers, the cut-off gives back.

Abstention moves in the opposite direction and gains little. Dropping candidates whose filter
score falls below a threshold, then ranking the survivors as usual, traces a frontier along which
precision barely moves. It is measured on the broad pool, not the deployed one (all $300$
applicable rules, as in the last row of Table~\ref{tab:selection-breadth}) which is why its
no-threshold baseline sits above the deployed macro recall of $\grailmacro$ at a larger output size.
With no threshold, recall@15 is 0.388 at precision 0.098 and 9.96 outputs
per substrate; at a threshold of 0.5, recall is 0.146 at precision 0.114 and 1.55 outputs; at 0.9,
recall is 0.041 at precision 0.040 and 0.30 outputs. Precision never exceeds about 0.123. Raising the
threshold removes true and apparently false candidates in near-equal proportion, because an
unannotated candidate is scored a false positive whether or not it is a real metabolite. The best
threshold selected on
validation lifts F1 from 0.129 to 0.131 by trimming the output tail. Precision here is bounded
by the annotation, not by the threshold, which is why the deployed system ranks rather than
abstains.

(Table~\ref{tab:ranker-vs-prior}). At narrow breadth the two are indistinguishable; as the pool
broadens the learned filter pulls ahead while the prior plateaus. Substituting the prior for the
learned score at the ranking stage would cost recall, not gain it.

\subsection{The decomposition's analogue for a sequence-to-sequence method}
\label{app:seq2seq}

The factorisation runs only where the candidate set can be enumerated, which excludes the strongest
systems in the comparison. The structural analogue is available anyway, and the three gates are the
same three with the bank replaced by what a wide decode can reach: coverage becomes the references
appearing anywhere in a wide beam, selection becomes what survives narrowing to the deployed beam,
and ranking is the same truncation to $k$. The sets nest for the same reason, so the identity is
exact rather than approximate.

MetaPredictor decodes in two stages, $8\times8$ then $2\times8$, giving $16$ ranked candidates.
Re-running it at $16\times16$ then $4\times16$ on the same checkpoints, pipeline and seed changes
nothing but the beam and yields $64$, of which $34.3$ are distinct under the matching criterion
against the deployed $10.9$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
& reach & selection & ranking & recall@15 & what the first factor is \\
\midrule
MetaPredictor  & 0.612 & 0.804 & 1.000 & 0.492 & a beam budget \\
GRAIL          & 0.701 & \multicolumn{2}{c}{0.454}   & 0.318 & a rule bank \\
SyGMa          & 0.520 & \multicolumn{2}{c}{0.947}   & 0.493 & a rule bank \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Read across the row the arithmetic holds exactly, $0.612\times0.804\times1.000 = 0.492$, which is
the realised micro recall to four places. Read down the first column it does not mean the same
thing twice, and that is the caveat the analogy needs, not a detail. A beam is a budget, so $0.612$ bounds \emph{this
decode} and rises with a wider one; a rule bank is a fixed object, but its reach is not, and
\S\ref{app:enginereach} measures how much of it belongs to the engine rather than the rules. The
two columns are structurally parallel and semantically different, and putting them in one column of
one table without saying so would be the confusion this paper is about.

Two readings survive that caveat. MetaPredictor's narrowing costs $0.20$ where GRAIL's rule
selection costs $0.55$, so a beam is a far less lossy budget than a top-$k$ over $7{,}581$ rules, which is what one would expect of a budget the model itself was trained against. And its ranking
factor is $1.000$ exactly, for the reason GRAIL's is near one on this subset: the deployed decode
leaves $10.9$ distinct candidates against a budget of $15$, so the field's $k$ binds neither of
them. At $k=15$ SyGMa is truncated on $92.7\%$ of substrates on the full split against MetaPredictor's $1.4\%$ and GRAIL's none, which is the
population that fraction is measured on. The budget parameter of
\S\ref{sec:metric} is therefore acting on one method out of three even here.

All three rows are the $\nshared$ shared substrates; the full-split figures for GRAIL and SyGMa in
\S\ref{sec:decomp} are a different population and are not comparable with these.

\subsection{A third rule-grounded method}
\label{app:third}

The decomposition runs on two methods and one of them is degenerate in the factor the paper is
about, SyGMa's selection being $1$ by construction. BioTransformer supplies a third rule-grounded
row, and a non-degenerate one: it selects, conditioning reactions on biosystem and enzyme, not firing
everything applicable, and its templates ship enumerably: $994$ distinct SMIRKS across its
human, environmental-microbial and standardisation reaction files, of which $16$ occur only inside
records the shipped database comments out. Every reach figure here is computed over all $994$ and is
therefore an upper bound on what the $978$ active ones express, since a template can only add
products.

\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule

\midrule
GRAIL          & $7{,}581$ & 0.701 & 0.318 & 0.45 \\
SyGMa          &: & 0.520 & 0.493 & 0.95 \\
BioTransformer & $994$     & 0.479 & 0.444 & 0.93 \\
\bottomrule

The row is a bound on what this template set expresses through \emph{our} engine, and it is not a
third point on a trend. Its reach carries an interval, $0.479$ $[0.414,0.550]$, and
\S\ref{app:enginereach} measures the engine term at fixed rules at $+0.188$ $[+0.170,+0.205]$: a
term the size of the $0.479$-to-$0.701$ spread among the three reach figures above, though measured
on a different population, and larger than this row's own interval. The direction of that bias is
not known for this row, and the reason is instructive. Our engine under-realises SyGMa's rules by
about a factor of two, but that is a mismatch of hydrogen convention rather than a property of the
engine: $69.4\%$ of BioTransformer's templates carry a hydrogen \emph{atom} on the reactant side
against not one of SyGMa's $175$, and our loop supplies exactly the hydrogens theirs are written to

neither direction. No monotone reading across the three rows is available, and we
state the mechanism as the two-system contrast \S\ref{sec:decomp} draws until a third measurement of
comparable provenance exists. The three reach figures here are of mixed provenance in any case: two
are through their own engines and one through ours.

The two properties that follow are the row's actual claim, not caveats on it. Applying
BioTransformer's templates uniformly with RDKit is not its own Java implementation, which layers
enzyme predicates, cofactor logic and depth control on the same SMIRKS; this is therefore what the
template set expresses under a stated application procedure, not what its pipeline enumerates. And
its budgeted pool is not observable from frozen predictions, so selection and ranking stay merged in
the conversion column rather than separating as they do for GRAIL, which is why the row cannot
speak to the selection factor at all. The check that makes it credible is the inequality: realised
recall cannot exceed what the bank reaches. Parsing only the first of the three reaction files gave
a reach of $0.399$ against a realised $0.444$, which is impossible and is how the missing files were
found. The margin at $994$ templates is $0.035$, and it holds at the point estimate only: the lower
end of the reach interval, $0.414$, falls below the realised $0.444$. If further templates ship
elsewhere in that distribution the reach is higher and the conversion lower still: in the
direction the table already reports.

\subsection{How much of a bank's reach belongs to the engine}
\label{app:enginereach}

\S\ref{sec:decomp} compares $\ceiling$ against $0.539$ and the natural reading is bank breadth. Three
measurements say that reading is wrong, and the third is the one that matters. Every reach in this
subsection is measured with the substrate expanded, which is not the convention $\ceiling$ is quoted
under; the comparison here is between two \emph{applications} at fixed rules, and holding the
expansion fixed across both arms is what makes it one.

\paragraph{The two banks are not separate objects.} SyGMa's published rule set is one of the curated
banks our own is a deduplicated union of, so containment is testable by verbatim string membership, not inference: $152$ of its $175$ rules are present in the bank, all in the
curated portion and none among the mined templates. The comparison is a superset against a subset.

\paragraph{Depth is not the confound.} The expanded-convention ceiling used here is one uncapped
depth-1 pass. SyGMa's deployed
pool is built by a scenario in which phase II fires on phase I products, which is composed rather
than single-step, so the two were not measured the same way. Matching them costs little and moves
the number the wrong way for us: SyGMa reaches $0.5325$ $[0.507,0.558]$ at one step against $0.5391$
$[0.514,0.565]$ deployed, composition worth $+0.0065$ $[+0.0038,+0.0096]$, and the gap against that
same expanded ceiling widens from $0.1964$ to $0.2029$ $[0.185,0.222]$. The re-run reproduces the frozen pool on every
substrate, which is the gate this was run behind.

\paragraph{Reach is a property of the system, not of the rule base.} Hold the rules fixed at the
$152$ both systems have and swap only the engine:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llc}
\toprule
& rules, engine & reach \\
\midrule
A & $152$ shared, expanded      & 0.189 $[0.151,0.230]$ \\
B & $152$ shared, SyGMa's       & 0.381 $[0.333,0.433]$ \\
C & all $175$, SyGMa's          & 0.524 $[0.469,0.579]$ \\
D & all $175$, SyGMa's composed & 0.527 $[0.473,0.582]$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The engine at fixed rules is worth $+0.188$ $[+0.170,+0.205]$, the $23$ rules absent from our bank $+0.143$ $[+0.107,+0.180]$, and composition $+0.004$. The engine term alone is the size of the
entire reach gap the main text reports.

\paragraph{The term is a single decision.} The application loop makes three discretionary choices
before it reports a product: whether the substrate is expanded with explicit hydrogens before the
templates fire, whether products are normalised by tautomer canonicalisation or as plain canonical
SMILES, and what counts as a valid fragment. Toggling them one at a time over the same $152$ rules,
with the default configuration gated to reproduce arm A to four decimals and the comparator arm
gated against arm B, puts the whole term on the first. The gate binds the rule set, the substrates,
the matcher and the hydrogen decision, because moving any of those moves the number it checks; it
cannot bind the other two, since every configuration with hydrogens explicit returns that number
exactly. That is the second and third rows of the table rather than a gap in the check.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llcc}
\toprule
choice & from $\to$ to & reach & against the default \\
\midrule
explicit hydrogens    & yes $\to$ no             & 0.390 & $+0.190$ $[+0.173,+0.208]$ \\
product normalisation & tautomer $\to$ canonical & 0.200 & $0.000$ \\
validity floor        & on $\to$ off             & 0.200 & $0.000$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The engine term is $+0.188$, so that one call carries all of it, and what remains against SyGMa's own
engine is $+0.002$ $[0.000,+0.005]$: at that setting the two programs are no longer separable on
these rules. SyGMa's source calls neither \texttt{AddHs} nor \texttt{RemoveHs} anywhere, so the
contrast is between what the two programs do and not an inference from what they emit. Every figure
in this paragraph is micro, a difference of ratio-of-sums, which is the estimator the engine term
itself uses. The macro counterparts sit beside them in the artifact because they are a different
quantity and not a rounding of the same one: the switch is worth $+0.159$ under it, not $+0.190$, and the residual $+0.006$ $[+0.002,+0.010]$ rather than $+0.002$: an interval that
excludes zero where the micro one does not. The separability claim is the micro one, and the two estimators should not
be quoted against each other.

That the other two choices move nothing is a statement about these $152$ rules under one setting of
the first choice, and it generalises in neither direction on its own. Across the whole $\nbank$,
under the convention the pipeline fires rules in, the normalisation knob is inert exactly:
\textsc{canonical} and \textsc{standardize} both recover $454$ of $567$, the same references, and
reproduce the committed ceiling to four decimals, which is also what certifies this loop at the
scale of the bank, not of the shared subset. Expand the hydrogens first and the same two
modes separate by one recovered reference. Whether a discretionary choice matters is therefore
conditional on the other discretionary choices, and ``this one does not matter'' is a claim about a
configuration rather than about a knob.

\paragraph{What the switch does.} Not matching, which is the natural guess and is wrong. Over the
corpus the $152$ templates fire $4{,}259$ times with hydrogens explicit against $4{,}124$ without,
and every firing writes a SMILES either way. The loss is one step later and it is a valence
violation: of the $4{,}588$ fragments those products separate into, $2{,}891$ are structures RDKit
will not read back, against $23$ of $4{,}453$: $63\%$ against $0.5\%$, and $99.9\%$ of the excess
is that, not the heavy-atom floor. A
substitution template written against implicit hydrogens does not mention the hydrogen it replaces;
it attaches the new group and lets RDKit decrement a count held as a property rather than an atom.
Expand the substrate first and that hydrogen is an atom, nothing removes it, and the product carries
an aromatic carbon holding both its hydrogen and the new substituent: benzoic acid under aromatic
hydroxylation gives \texttt{[H]OC(=O)c1c([H])c([H])c([H])(O)c([H])c1[H]}, which does not parse. The
template is not wrong and the engine is not wrong. They disagree about how a hydrogen is spelled.

\paragraph{The decision belongs to the bank as much as to the engine.} It does not follow that the
expansion is a mistake. Which setting a bank wants is a property of how its templates were written,
and no rule file records it. Running the same switch with our engine throughout, over three banks
assembled by three groups, against the same substrates and their $567$ references:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
bank & rules & hydrogen atom & recovered, explicit & change \\
     &       & on reactants  & $\to$ implicit      & \\
\midrule
SyGMa          & $175$    & none  & $125 \to 299$ & $+0.307$ $[+0.260,+0.355]$ \\
ours           & $\nbank$ & $675$ & $414 \to 453$ & $+0.069$ $[+0.041,+0.098]$ \\
BioTransformer & $994$    & $690$ & $268 \to 74$  & $-0.342$ $[-0.388,-0.298]$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Not one of SyGMa's $175$ templates carries a hydrogen \emph{atom} on its reactant side, and its
reach more than doubles once the hydrogens are gone. $690$ of BioTransformer's $994$ do, and its
reach falls to a quarter of itself. Ours lies between them on both counts, and that row was written
down before it was measured: at $675$ templates in $\nbank$ our bank is in SyGMa's convention and
should move the same way by much less, which is what it does. Three banks cannot establish the share
as a predictor and it is not offered as one. What they establish is that the direction is not a
property of our engine, so a reported coverage figure identifies a bank together with a convention,
and the second half of that pair is recorded nowhere: including, until this measurement, by us.
Our own bank is where it costs us: on these substrates it recovers $414$ references with hydrogens
expanded and $453$ without. \ceiling\ is therefore quoted without the expansion, matching the
convention the deployed generator fires rules in; the figures this appendix reports for the shared
$152$ are the expanded ones, and each is labelled where it appears.

\paragraph{What the decomposition survives, and what it does not license.} Each method's three
factors are measured through its own application and multiply to its own realised recall, so the
decomposition itself is unaffected. What it does not license is reading the first factor
\emph{across} methods as a property of their rule sets: it is a property of rule set and engine
together, which is what \S\ref{sec:decomp} says. The direction of the effect is opposite to the one that would
suit the present system rhetorically: our engine under-realises
SyGMa's rules by a factor of two under the expansion, and the same expansion applied to our own bank
costs it $0.082$ of reach on the clean test split, which is why $\ceiling$ is quoted under the
convention the pipeline fires rules in, not under that one, and why the headline that the
bank is not the binding constraint is strengthened rather than weakened. The $152$ are curated, and
the expansion is what the curated half gains from and the mined majority loses to
(\S\ref{app:provenance}), so the factor of two is the size of an effect our own hand-written rules
do not suffer: the asymmetry runs against us here, not for us. The mined majority was itself
kept only if applying it through this engine regenerated its own annotated product
(Appendix~\ref{app:arch}), so it was validated against this engine, not a foreign one and
supplies no offsetting overstatement.

\subsection{The same choice outside metabolism, failing the other way}
\label{app:retroconvention}

Everything above is measured on metabolite rule banks, which alone would establish a fact about
one small literature. The USPTO retrosynthesis templates are a library from another field,
extracted by other people from other reactions for another task, and the instrument applies to them
unchanged. What it finds is not a repetition, because the mechanism is the other one.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
& metabolite banks & retrosynthesis templates \\
\midrule
patterns                                  & $175$ / $\nbank$ / $994$ & $42{,}554$ \\
carrying a hydrogen atom                  & none / $675$ / $690$     & none \\
constraining an atom by degree            & rare                     & all $42{,}554$ \\
what the expansion breaks                 & the product              & the match \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Applied to $500$ products of the USPTO-50k test split this repository holds (the
three-system group's, of the three that circulate under that name (\S\ref{app:xdomain})) the library makes $146{,}346$
template--product matches with hydrogens implicit and $26{,}788$ with them explicit, and the number
of products with any applicable template falls from $500$ to $489$. The cause is one construct and
the attribution is exact rather than approximate: of the $119{,}558$ matches the expansion costs,
$119{,}558$ come back when the degree constraint alone is deleted from the very patterns that lost
them. The check is a gate, the measurement is refused below $90\%$, and it returns $100\%$.

So a degree primitive counts explicit connections, expanding a molecule changes every degree in it,
and a library built on them stops matching before it can build anything. That is a different failure
from the metabolite one, where the patterns fire just as often and the product is what will not
parse, and the two together give the claim its general form. What is convention-dependent is a class
of \textsc{smarts} construct, not a domain: a primitive that counts explicit structure breaks
matching, a template that rewrites without naming the hydrogen it consumes breaks the product. Both
are readable from the rule file without running anything, and neither is declared by any library we
have looked at.

\paragraph{Three ways the choice bites, and one of them can be repaired.} A third mechanism turned
up inside our own measurement rather than in someone else's library, and the three together are
what makes this a class, not three defects. Each has one measured instance:

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
what breaks & the construct responsible & measured on \\
\midrule
the match, before any product & a primitive counting explicit structure & $42{,}554$ retro templates \\
the product, once built       & a rewrite not naming its hydrogen        & three metabolite banks \\
which answer the bank gives   & a union of two written conventions       & our own $\nbank$ rules \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The first two are read off a rule file and avoided by declaring the convention. The third is not
avoidable by any declaration, because no single setting is right for an object assembled from two:
one collection is mismeasured whichever is chosen, and the choice does not shift the answer so much
as exchange it, which is what \S\ref{app:provenance} shows it doing to the two halves of our bank.
It is also the only one of the three that can be \emph{recovered} rather than merely avoided, by
dispatching the convention per template (Appendix~\ref{app:negative}).

Two things this does not claim. It is applicability, not coverage against references, which
is the first step of reach and not the whole of it. And no retrosynthesis system expands its target
by convention, so this is a latent hazard in that field rather than an error in it: the point is
that the dependence is a property of the patterns, and the patterns are not ours.

\subsection{The bank without a selector, at a matched budget}
\label{app:noselect}

Everything above localises the loss at selection by observation. The counterfactual states it
directly: apply the bank with no selector at all, rank the whole pool with the deployed scorer, and
truncate at the number of candidates SyGMa emits. The breadth sweep does not answer this. It ranks
the pool the deployed 30-rule selector produces, $12.5$ candidates on average of which $8.0$ are
emitted, which has nothing to give at $k=64$; and the widest row of Table~\ref{tab:selection-breadth} is not the whole bank
either, since the generator's calibrated threshold is itself a selector and prunes rules however
large $\mathit{top\text{-}k}$ is.

\subsection{The decomposition applied to the comparator, and what it bounds}

\paragraph{Why the dispatch recovers anything, registered in advance and decided against us.} Two
accounts fit a bank whose halves want opposite conventions. On the first, \emph{mixing} creates the
loss: one collection is mismeasured whichever global setting is chosen, and the repair returns what
the losing half forfeits. On the second and duller, $675$ hand-written templates simply want the
expansion, and the mined majority bears on neither the loss nor its repair. They differ in exactly
one measurable way, and that difference was registered in advance of any measurement: if mixing creates the

\mathrm{res}(\text{curated}) + \mathrm{res}(\text{mined})$.

It is subadditive. On the full split the whole bank's residual is $+0.0062$ $[+0.0032,+0.0095]$ and

so the second account is the one that survives. Mixing does not create the loss, it \emph{dilutes}
it: the mined majority independently reaches part of what the expanded curated templates would have
recovered, so their repair is partly redundant once both are present. What the dispatch recovers is
therefore the expansion those $675$ templates require and the bank's global setting denies them: a smaller claim than a mixing account would have supported, and the one the measurement carries.

The per-template dispatch of Appendix~\ref{app:negative} still beats the better of the two global
settings, not tying it, and by more on the half that wants the expansion than on the bank
that contains it.

\subsection{Selection as measured against selection as deployed}

The learned-versus-prior comparison in Table~\ref{tab:levers} is a controlled comparison of rule
selection, in which each mode picks its own thirty rules and shares the downstream product loop
and filter. It is not the deployed recall. The deployed pipeline applies its own top thirty
rules, and the breadth sweep confirms the correspondence: the 30-rule baseline of 0.352 on this
subset sits close to the deployed macro recall of $\grailmacro$ on the full test, above it by the
subset inflation noted above rather than reproducing it exactly.

At that operating point the deployed system is close to prior-independent. Two checkpoints
differing only in whether a trained prior buffer was present gave 0.335 and 0.334 on a
291-substrate evaluation. Deployed recall is therefore limited by coverage, not by the
prior term, even though the selection experiment shows the learned selector losing to the prior
when selection is what is measured. The experiment exposes a weakness that deployment conceals
by not depending on the selector's ordering.

Neither multi-step application nor any single rule family moves coverage much, and training-set
size is saturated. The dominant addressable loss remains selection, at $\selret$: a learned rule
selector that performs worse than counting. The three propositions below explain why.

\subsection{Three propositions}

\paragraph{Proposition 1: the training objective is mismatched to the metric.}
A filter trained under a strictly proper scoring rule learns a globally calibrated posterior,
which is Bayes-optimal for AUC and for calibration. Ranking each substrate's pool by that
posterior and taking the top $k$ is optimal for recall@$k$ only when pools are homogeneous.
GRAIL's pools are not: they range from 17 to 150 candidates and from 1 to 18 positives. A
pointwise-calibrated scorer can therefore be suboptimal for recall@$k$ while a listwise
surrogate dominates it. A two-substrate counterexample makes this concrete: the reordering that
improves recall is verifiably not globally calibrated, so a proper scoring rule rejects it.

A controlled swap of the objective, holding everything else fixed, confirms the prediction. The
same two auxiliary heads, the same frozen generator and filter, and the same candidate pool are
used twice, differing only in how the heads were trained. Trained pointwise and multiplied into
the rank, they add nothing: $+0.0002$, 95\% CI $[-0.0073,+0.0077]$. Fine-tuned with a listwise
ranking loss against the frozen generator and filter context, the identical heads gain $+0.0089$
$[+0.0032,+0.0153]$ over their pointwise counterpart and $+0.0091$ $[+0.0027,+0.0160]$ over the
baseline, on a paired bootstrap over the full test set of $\ntest$ substrates. Capacity, features
and pool are constant, so the margin is attributable to the objective. A second re-ranking arm
reproduces the direction at the same sample size: adding type and site terms lifts recall@15
from $0.388$ to $0.404$ on the same broad pool, a paired $+0.0165$ $[+0.006,+0.027]$, while the
factorised signal on its own is not significant ($+0.001$, $[-0.010,+0.013]$).

\paragraph{Proposition 2: rule selection is extreme multi-label classification, where counting is
a strong baseline.}
Selecting which of $\nbank$ rules apply to a substrate is an extreme multi-label classification
problem with a nearly empty positive set: a median of five rules carries a positive label per
substrate, $0.07$ per cent of the label space, and a mean of $11.3$, $0.15$ per cent. In this regime the classification literature has long established that
frequency and propensity baselines are hard to beat and that naive losses are dominated by label
sparsity; propensity-scored losses were introduced for precisely this failure mode
\citep{Jain_2016}. Metabolism rule selection inherits the pathology, which the domain literature
does not name. Identifying it here, and testing it, is the contribution.

The mechanism is specific. Under positive-unlabelled annotation with an approximately constant
labelling propensity, a learner with constant unlabelled weighting recovers a propensity-distorted
score whose dominant component is the marginal rule-firing rate. That rate is the frequency
prior, so the prior is competitive by construction at the selection stage, and the learned
selector improves on it only when substrate-conditional variation in prevalence exceeds
estimation noise. The saturation with training-set size indicates that it does not at present
scale.

The supporting measurement is the learned-only recall of 0.266 against the prior's 0.410
($\Delta=-0.144$, $[-0.196,-0.095]$, $n=245$). The advantage is specific to selection and does
not generalise to ranking: once rules have been applied broadly, the learned filter is the
better scorer, at 0.413 against the prior's 0.374 on the same 245 substrates, a paired $+0.039$
$[+0.007,+0.072]$. That interval clears zero and not by much, and where it comes from is worth
saying. The two arms order one identical pool, so they can differ only where the pool exceeds the
fifteen-candidate budget by enough for order to matter. That is $32$ substrates: the filter ahead on
$22$, behind on $10$, tied with the prior on the remaining $213$. The proposition makes a refutable prediction,
that reweighting the labelled loss by the inverse estimated propensity should shrink the prior's
edge; this is an open test. The propensity model itself is an unmeasured modelling assumption.

\paragraph{Proposition 3: single-step rule application has a ceiling below one.}
Single-step recall cannot exceed single-step coverage, and coverage is bounded away from one
because a non-vanishing fraction of references are multi-generation products, for instance
oxidation followed by conjugation, unreachable by any single rule application. No improvement in
ranking recovers that residual.

Two measurements witness the bound. Depth-2 application raises the ceiling by 0.012 at 8.5 times
the candidate cost. On the external GLORYx set the uncapped single-step ceiling is 0.709, and
its references include multi-generation metabolites that no single step can reach.

\begin{figure}[h]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.52\textwidth]{fig_gap}
\end{center}
\caption{What the bank fails to reach in one step, typed by element-aware changed-bond multiset.
Only the first group is reachable by generalising an existing template, which is what bounds the
in-bank ceiling at $0.855$.}
\label{fig:gap}
\end{figure}

The composition of the gap determines how much of it template generalisation could close. Typing
each uncovered transformation by its element-aware changed-bond multiset, and comparing against
the 4{,}417 types the bank already contains, splits the $475$ uncovered test transformations into
$98$ ($21\%$) whose reaction type the bank already has and $337$ ($71\%$) whose type is
absent from it; the remaining $40$ admit no radius-0 type and stay unclassified. The first group is reachable in principle by a more general template, which
bounds an in-bank ceiling at $(2122+98)/2597 = 0.855$; closing both groups would bound it at
$0.985$. Template generalisation is therefore a real but tightly bounded gain of $0.038$ in ceiling
headroom, and seven-tenths of the residual is genuinely out-of-bank chemistry rather than an
artefact of template specificity. The bound is conditional on single-step application: it
constrains the paradigm, not the problem.

\subsection{Two interventions}

\paragraph{A factorised generator tests Propositions 2 and 3.}
Rebuilding the generator as a dense factorised model, $P(\text{type}\mid s)\cdot
P(\text{site}\mid \text{type},s)$ over a coarse reaction-type vocabulary, turns the observational
decomposition into a causal test.

Selection proves fixable. The learned type head beats the frequency prior by roughly threefold at
$k=5$ (recall@5 0.086 against 0.030), and converges to the prior by $k=10$ (0.298 against 0.309). Site localisation is close to, but below, its reference: 0.78 hit@3 against 0.81. The positive-unlabelled degeneracy of Proposition 2 is
therefore an artefact of the target's support, not a fundamental limit, which confirms that
proposition's prediction.

Coverage does not bind it. As a replacement generator the pipeline reaches recall@15 $0.489$
$[0.464,0.514]$ on the full test against $\grailmacro$ for the deployed pipeline, but it emits
$14.0$ candidates per substrate against $8.4$, so that pair is not budget-matched and supports no claim. The conservative comparison is: truncated to five candidates it reaches $0.337$, matching
the deployed pipeline's $\grailmacro$ while emitting fewer than half as many, and at ten it reaches
$0.432$. Both runs were scored separately, not paired, so no interval is quoted on a
difference. The convention this is measured in is the one the deployed generator fires rules in;
under the expanding one the same model reaches $0.256$ and emits $8.0$, with $46$ substrates
receiving no prediction at all against $2$ here, which is the size of the artefact rather than a
property of the architecture. That the coverage term binds is consistent with Proposition 3 but does not test it: the
proposition concerns what one application step can reach, and a type vocabulary is not application
depth.

Kept at broad coverage and used only to re-rank, the same signal is worth a small significant
margin: recall@15 rises from $0.388$ to $0.404$, a paired $+0.0165$ $[+0.006,+0.027]$ over the full
test set. Both are broad-pool figures, not deployed ones. How the heads are trained decides whether that margin appears. Multiplying
independently trained type and site probabilities into the rank adds nothing on a matched pool
($+0.0002$, not significant). Fine-tuning the same two heads with a listwise ranking loss against
a frozen generator and filter beats that bolt-on by $+0.0089$ $[+0.003,+0.015]$ and the baseline
by $+0.0091$ $[+0.003,+0.016]$. Rank-aware training, not the type and site heads themselves,
recovers the margin.

Selection is addressable and ranking yields a small margin, but no \emph{generator redesign} tested
here matches SyGMa without a broader bank: for this redesign, coverage is what binds. That is not
in tension with the summary above or with \S\ref{sec:decomp}, which put the dominant addressable
loss at the deployed operating point in selection. The two statements are about different
quantities (where the deployed pipeline is losing recoverable recall, and what caps a generator
rebuilt to fix selection) and \S\ref{sec:decomp} gives the reconciled verdict, that selection
binds jointly with the cut-off, not alone.

\paragraph{Set generation with a GFlowNet does not win here, and the reason is cardinality.}
We formulated the generator as a set-terminal GFlowNet trained by Trajectory Balance against a
positive-unlabelled set-coverage reward $\log R(S) = \beta(\mathrm{TP} - \lambda|S|)$ with an
analytic backward policy; the formulation is new for this domain and is released for reuse. Across
three seeds on 40 validation substrates its single-set recall@15 is $0.292\pm0.032$, indistinguishable
from beam search at $0.311\pm0.039$ and below a reranker at $0.411\pm0.044$. It is reported as a negative result, because the endpoint is one its objective works against:
the reward pays a size penalty and recall@$k$ does not charge one. The diagnosis is cardinality
rather than mis-specification: $\beta$ cancels in the argmax, so the reward is the \emph{more}
conservative of the two, and what happened is that the policy emitted $14.2$ against a configured
maximum of $15$, leaving the size term almost no room to act. Set-level training without an explicit
cardinality estimate is therefore untested here, not refuted, and predicting set size is the
component this architecture was missing.

\subsection{The output budget is mis-set, and adapting it per substrate does not help}

Section~\ref{sec:metric} reports that the truncation is the aggregate's second dial. This gives the
measurement behind that, and the negative result that follows from it.

Every method here emits a number of candidates fixed by convention rather than by the substrate, and
no budget is selected in what follows: the F1-versus-$k$ curve is swept for each method, the
orderings are read off the curves, and the one certified comparison is made at a $k$ fixed in
advance. A budget chosen against the numbers it is reported on would be optimistically biased, and
a reordering could be an artifact of the choice, so the choice is removed rinstead ofcorrected.

Three orderings occur across $k \in [1,32]$. At $k=1$ it is MetaPredictor, SyGMa, GRAIL; from
$k=2$ to $5$ SyGMa leads; from $6$ to $13$ it is MetaPredictor, SyGMa, GRAIL again; and from $14$ on,
where the field's $15$ sits, it is MetaPredictor, GRAIL, SyGMa. GRAIL is last at every $k \le 13$.
At $k=5$, fixed before these curves were computed, two of the three pairs separate: SyGMa above
GRAIL by $0.071$ $[0.057,0.086]$ and MetaPredictor above GRAIL by $0.067$
$[0.051,0.083]$; SyGMa and MetaPredictor do not, SyGMa nominally above by $0.005$
$[-0.006,0.015]$.

The mechanism is in the same sweep, and it is stronger than a statement about GRAIL. A budget only
binds a method when the method emits more than the budget, so at $k=15$ the comparison is not
budget-matched at all: SyGMa is cut from $80.7$ candidates to $15$ on $92.7\%$ of substrates, while
re-truncating GRAIL's \emph{emitted} list at $k=15$ removes nothing from any of them, because its
emission is already capped there. That last is a statement about re-truncation and not about the
pool: the output reaches the cap upstream, on $308$ of $\ntest$ substrates ($26.3\%$), and every reference the
decomposition loses between the budgeted pool and the output falls on one of those substrates
(\S\ref{sec:decomp}). The convention silently advantages
whichever methods emit less than the budget, and it is the budget rather than the ranking that
decides. The magnitude is not a rounding effect: SyGMa's F1 at its best budget over
$k \in [1,32]$ is $5.4$ times its F1 scored at the full output it emits.

The binding is partial well below $15$ too, which is why the certified comparison is reported at
several budgets, not one. GRAIL is cut on $63.5\%$ of substrates at $k=5$ and $45.6\%$ at
$k=8$, against SyGMa's $98.8\%$ and $96.5\%$. Against GRAIL, SyGMa is ahead by $0.083$
$[0.065,0.100]$ at $k=3$, $0.071$ $[0.057,0.086]$ at $k=5$, $0.039$ $[0.026,0.052]$ at $k=8$ and
$0.022$ $[0.010,0.034]$ at $k=10$, every interval excluding zero, so the reversal is not an artifact
of one budget inside the region.

\paragraph{What a compact output is and is not worth.} GRAIL returns $8.4$ candidates against
SyGMa's $80.7$, which we have described as an operating point recall@$k$ does not express. The sweep
prices it, and against us throughout. Truncation is free: any user preferring a shorter list can
take SyGMa's top-$k$, and at every budget up to $13$ that shorter list beats GRAIL's, certified
from $k=1$ to $k=10$. Emitting few candidates therefore confers no advantage that cutting a longer
list would not also confer, at any budget below the field's, and GRAIL's lead at $k=15$ is the cap
declining to bind it.

The head of the list is no exception. At $k=1$ both methods are cut, on $92.8\%$ and $99.6\%$ of
substrates, and SyGMa leads there too, by $0.038$ $[0.011,0.064]$. Every ranked list is scored with
the parent removed, on tautomer-InChIKey equality with the substrate, so a method is never credited
or charged for returning the compound it was given.

For GRAIL the deployed operating point can also be certified against a fixed cut, because
its per-candidate ranking scores are released with the frozen predictions. Over the $1{,}166$ scored
substrates, emitting as deployed scores $0.137$ against $0.196$ at $k=1$: a paired difference of
$-0.058$, 95\% interval $[-0.075,-0.042]$.

The obvious next step is to make the cut depend on the substrate, and it does not work. Per
substrate the F1-optimal cut $k^\star$ is computable exactly from the ranked list and the annotated
set, so the ceiling is known: knowing $k^\star$ is worth $48$ to $76\%$ relative over the best
constant across the five methods. Almost none of that is reachable. Cut each list instead at a
per-substrate count predicted out of fold from substrate descriptors, and the part of the headroom
that recovers is $-10\%$ for GRAIL, $-2\%$ for SyGMa and $+5\%$ for MetaPredictor: for
two of the three, predicting the set size does worse than the best constant. The true count is a
property of the substrate and the ranking is a property of the method, and out of fold the first
carries almost none of the gap.

Cross-fitting confirms this out of sample. A gradient-boosted regressor on twelve substrate
descriptors, predicting $k^\star$ and evaluated on held-out folds, recovers $3.2\%$ of the oracle
headroom for GRAIL, $4.3\%$ for MetaPredictor and $1.1\%$ for SyGMa; predicting the set size
directly falls below the constant baseline for two methods of three. Descriptors are not a graph
encoder, so this bounds the direction rather than refuting it, but the mechanism it agrees with is
structural: a head reading only the substrate cannot see which of its own predictions are correct,
and that is where the headroom lives. The lever the measurement points to instead is a stopping rule
on calibrated candidate scores, which is a different object from the abstention that failed in
Table~\ref{tab:levers}, that having been a global threshold judged on recall@$k$, where truncation
can only lose.

\subsection{A similarity-to-training split does not measure transfer here}

Rule-encoded chemistry is general where learned patterns are not, so a rule-grounded method might be
expected to lose less on substrates far from its training data. That would be a claim about the
paradigm, not a leaderboard position, and it is testable on frozen predictions by stratifying
the test split on maximum Morgan--Tanimoto similarity to any training substrate. It does not
survive, and the way it fails is the part worth reporting.

\begin{figure}[h]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.52\textwidth]{fig_transfer}
\end{center}
\caption{The control that decides the axis. If similarity to training measured transfer, SyGMa's
curve would be flat: it is a rule engine that never saw the training split. It falls on a slope this
measurement cannot separate from the learned methods', so the axis is measuring how well studied a
substrate is.}
\label{fig:transfer}
\end{figure}

The design carries a control that decides whether the axis means anything at all. SyGMa is a rule
engine that never saw the training split, so if the axis measures transfer its curve must be flat.
It is not: SyGMa degrades at $+0.180$ on F1 and $+0.291$ on recall against GRAIL's $+0.158$ and
$+0.307$. Resampled on the same draws, so that the comparison is paired, the difference is $+0.022$
$[-0.041,+0.086]$ on F1 and $-0.016$ $[-0.144,+0.115]$ on recall. Those are failures to detect a
difference, not demonstrations that none exists, and the width of the interval is the informative part: the F1
interval still admits a gap of $0.086$ and the recall interval one of $0.144$, against slopes of
$0.158$ and $0.307$ themselves. What the data support is that a rule engine with nothing to
transfer from falls on a slope this measurement cannot separate from a learned method's: not that
the two slopes are equal.

A second stratification points the same way and carries its own confound rather than removing it.
The fraction of a substrate's references the rule bank reaches at all rises from $0.712$ below
similarity $0.3$ to $0.819$ above $0.6$. That is not a quantity untouched by training: $5{,}866$ of
the $\nbank$ rules are mined from the training split, and those are the rules that do the reaching,
covering $0.785$ of the references against the curated remainder's $0.471$ (\S\ref{app:provenance}), and
Appendix~\ref{app:external} says in terms that applying the bank to a substrate it was partly mined
from is not independent of training, so a coverage gradient in similarity to training is exactly
what a training-derived rule set would produce. It cannot serve as the leg of the argument that no
learning touches, and is not used as one; the control above is what carries the section. The common cause is visible in the
same stratification: reference sets are smaller at the far end too, $2.40$ annotated metabolites
above $0.6$ against $1.90$ below $0.3$, though across all five strata the series is not monotone
and the contrast is between its endpoints, not a trend. Training corpus, rule bank and annotation all derive from the
literature of well-studied metabolism, so the axis measures how well studied a substrate is and
moves all three together.

Read at face value the slopes would say the transformer transfers best, at $+0.106$ and $+0.118$,
roughly half the other two. Read correctly they say the measurement cannot support a transfer claim
in either direction, and we make none.

The detector is not ours. \citet{Buttenschoen_2024} stratify a docking benchmark by maximum
sequence identity to its training set and run never-trained classical docking alongside the learned
methods, which is the same design. Their control comes out \emph{flat}: the classical methods score
alike across strata while the learned ones fall on low-identity targets, so there the axis does
measure transfer and the degradation is real. That is what makes the instrument worth using rather
than a formality: it returns opposite verdicts in the two domains, and a control that always
agreed would discriminate nothing.

What is ours is the negative result in this domain and the mechanism behind it. Stratifying by
similarity to training is the standard way to argue generalisation in molecular machine learning,
and wherever a knowledge base and a training corpus share provenance the axis is confounded with
difficulty; \citet{Buttenschoen_2024} did not need that explanation because their control was flat.
Adjacent evidence points the same way without closing it: retrosynthesis accuracy tracks training
template frequency even for template-free models \citep{Tanovic_2026}, and molecular datasets cover
structure space by study intensity rather than uniformly \citep{Kretschmer_2025}.

Had we run the learned methods alone we would have concluded from $+0.158$ against $+0.106$ that the
transformer is the more robust of the two: a conclusion against our own system, and therefore the
kind that attracts least scrutiny, resting on a measurement that cannot carry it. That conclusion
would not even have been a marginal one: paired, the transformer's recall slope is shallower than
GRAIL's by $-0.190$ $[-0.330,-0.044]$, an interval excluding zero. The axis would have delivered a
certified transfer claim from a quantity that does not measure transfer, which is why the control
and not the interval width is what decides here.

\subsection{Partial identification of the metric ordering under incomplete annotation}
\label{app:propensity}

Section~\ref{sec:metric} ranks methods by F1, and every precision figure feeding it is a lower
bound: a predicted structure that is a genuine metabolite nobody recorded is charged as a false
positive. The threat is not symmetric. The method the metric demotes is the one emitting eighty
candidates per substrate, so it is the one with the most room for unrecorded hits, and an ordering
that only holds because the references are incomplete would be an artifact, not a result.

Model the annotation as a uniform propensity: for a substrate with $T$ true metabolites, each is
recorded independently with probability $c$. Fix a prediction set of size $n$ covering $t$ of the
true metabolites. Then $\mathbb{E}[\text{TP}_{\text{obs}}] = ct$ and
$\mathbb{E}[|\text{references}|] = cT$, so

\[
R_{\text{obs}} = \frac{ct}{cT} = \frac{t}{T} = R_{\text{true}},
\qquad
P_{\text{obs}} = \frac{ct}{n} = c\,P_{\text{true}},
\qquad
F1_{\text{obs}} = \frac{2ct}{n + cT}.
\]

Three consequences follow. Recall is unbiased, so the field's reliance on it is defensible on
this ground. Precision is the truth scaled by a factor common to every method, so
while its level is unidentified its \emph{ordering} is identified for any $c$. F1 is neither, since
$c$ enters numerator and denominator differently, and its ordering is therefore a function of $c$
that has to be checked rather than assumed.

We check it by correcting each per-substrate precision to $\min(P_{\text{obs}}/c,\,1)$, leaving
recall untouched, and recomputing macro F1 over the full split. The cap makes the correction
conservative in the direction that matters: a method cannot be credited past certainty, and the
method with the lowest observed precision is the one that benefits from the correction longest.

\begin{figure}[h]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.52\textwidth]{fig_propensity}
\end{center}
\caption{Macro F1 with each per-substrate precision corrected to $\min(P_{\text{obs}}/c,1)$, swept
over the annotation propensity. The reported ordering holds until $c=0.06$, where SyGMa passes
GRAIL; at that rate a substrate with three recorded metabolites would have fifty in truth.}
\label{fig:propensity}
\end{figure}

The reported ordering survives to $c = 0.06$, where SyGMa overtakes GRAIL; MetaPredictor is never
overtaken on a grid running down to $c=0.05$. At $c=0.055$ a substrate carrying three recorded
metabolites would have about fifty-five in truth, which the references do not support.

The same model bounds what any set metric can measure. A predictor that emitted exactly the true
metabolite set would still be scored against the annotated subset, so with completeness $c$ it
measures Jaccard $c$ and F1 $2c/(1+c)$ however good it is. At $c=0.5$ that is a Jaccard ceiling of
$0.5$ and an F1 ceiling of $0.667$; at $c=0.3$, $0.30$ and $0.462$. Set-agreement endpoints are
therefore bounded by annotation completeness in a way recall is not, which is a reason to report
them beside recall, not in place of it, and a caution for any method trained directly on set
agreement: optimising it on incomplete references trains partly on which metabolites were recorded.

The assumption doing the work is uniformity. A propensity that fell systematically with how
implausible a product is would favour the wide-output method more than this correction allows,
because its extra candidates are disproportionately the implausible ones. That is the direction in
which the argument is weakest, and we state it rather than test it: estimating a
structure-dependent propensity would need exactly the exhaustively annotated references whose
absence creates the problem.


\subsection{The prescribed remedy for the selection pathology does not work}

Section~\ref{sec:xmc} identifies rule selection as extreme multi-label classification and cites the
literature in which propensity-scored losses were introduced for exactly that regime
\citep{Jain_2016}. Naming a pathology without trying its standard treatment would leave the
diagnosis untested, so we ran the treatment.

Each positive rule label is weighted by the inverse of its estimated propensity, fit from the rule's
positive count by the model of \citet{Jain_2016} with $A=0.55$, $B=1.5$, and normalised over rules
that carry a positive so the loss keeps its scale. Across the $\nbank$ rules the weights run from
$0.196$ to $1.669$ with a mean of $1.377$, and $81.5\%$ are up-weighted: most of them
the $4{,}271$ that never carry a positive at all, which take the maximum weight, against $0.83$
for a rule with five positives. The training
configuration is otherwise identical to the deployed one, differing in three fields, and the
generator trained to completion over eight epochs with validation loss falling monotonically from
$0.530$ to $0.472$.

It does not help. On the same 245 substrates, under the same protocol and with the same deployed
filter, the learned-only selector reaches recall@15 of $0.249$ against $0.266$ under constant
weighting. Its deficit to the frequency prior widens from $-0.144$ $[-0.196,-0.095]$ to $-0.161$
$[-0.215,-0.107]$; both are significant and the intervals overlap heavily, so the change itself is
not distinguishable while the absence of improvement is clear. The prior-only arm returns $0.410$
in both runs, identical to four decimal places, which confirms the comparison isolates the training
objective. Per-substrate scores from the two runs were not retained jointly, so no paired interval is quoted on the $-0.017$ difference itself.

The intervention was also run end to end, not only in the isolated probe, and there the discipline
this paper argues for is what decides it. Retraining the deployed configuration with the propensity
objective and evaluating it as deployed gives $0.348$ against $0.334$ on test, which read alone
looks like a gain of $+0.014$. On validation, the split model selection is permitted to touch, the
same comparison gives $0.343$ against $0.344$, a difference of $-0.001$. The test-split movement is
a single seed against a seed-to-seed spread of $\pm0.010$ and is not corroborated by the split available for
selection, so it supports no claim. Both figures are on the $300$-substrate evaluation
that configuration runs and are not comparable with the full-split numbers elsewhere in this paper.

The conclusion concerns the domain and not the method. Inverse-propensity weighting exists
to correct a label's rarity when that rarity is an artifact of incomplete annotation. In metabolism
a rule's firing frequency is substantially not an artifact: common rules are common because the
transformations they encode are common in the chemistry. Down-weighting them by a factor of five,
which is what the fitted propensities do at the head of the distribution, therefore discards signal
rather than correcting bias. This is a candidate explanation for the original finding, not a
demonstration of it: separating the annotation and chemistry components of rule frequency would
need an exhaustively annotated reference set, which is the same object whose absence creates the
positive-unlabelled problem throughout this paper.
