\label{app:robustorder}

Fix a set of systems $S$, a set of items, and a grid $C$ of evaluation cells. In cell $c$ each
system has a mean score, and those means induce a total preorder $\prec_c$ on $S$: exact ties
occur, in four cells of the grids reported here, and a tie is not a reversal. A published table
reports $\prec_{c_0}$ for one cell $c_0$ that its paper does not name. Write
\[
  \prec_{\!*} \;=\; \bigcap_{c\in C} \prec_c ,
\]
and say $a$ \emph{dominates} $b$ when $a \prec_{\!*} b$. Say the pair is \emph{separated in every
cell} when in addition the paired bootstrap interval on the margin excludes zero in each $c$.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Proposition 1.} $\prec_{\!*}$ is a strict partial order, and it is the finest relation on
$S$ that no cell of $C$ can contradict.

\emph{Proof.} An intersection of transitive, irreflexive relations is transitive and irreflexive.
Any relation not contained in some $\prec_c$ contains a pair that cell orders the other way.
\hfill$\square$
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Proposition 2.} If $C \subseteq C'$ then $\prec_{\!*}^{C'} \subseteq \prec_{\!*}^{C}$: the
dominating set is monotone non-increasing in the grid, and so is the number of tiers.

\emph{Proof.} Immediate from the definition as an intersection; the tier count is the longest chain
of a smaller relation. \hfill$\square$
\end{quote}

Proposition 2 is the reason a single share is not a property of a leaderboard. It is a property of
the pair (leaderboard, grid), it can only fall as a grid is widened, and any paper reporting one
number could have reported a larger one by declaring fewer choices. The remedy used here is to
report the curve rather than a point: each declared axis alone, and their product. The tables below
give that curve for every leaderboard in the paper, together with the accuracy of every system in
every cell, which is the table all the derived quantities are computed from.


\paragraph{A dual statistic.} The tier count says what survives; the number of distinct total
orders the same systems take across the grid says how much varies. Neither is recoverable from the
other: a leaderboard can take many orderings and still support several tiers if the systems that
move are adjacent, and it can take few and support one if the pairs that never move are also never
resolved.

\paragraph{The grid is the same shape and not the same cells.} The criterion axis is four identity
relations in every domain: which strings name the same compound in the prediction domains, and which
poses count as the crystal one in docking. The second axis is not transferable. Retrosynthesis
tables are read at top-1 through top-10 and metabolite work at fifteen, so each leaderboard is swept
over the budgets its own field reports at; docking reports a single pose and has no budget, and the
choice its papers leave undeclared instead is whether that pose is energy-minimised before it is
scored. Fixing one set of budgets across domains would have measured robustness to a budget nobody
uses. What this costs is that the four shares are comparable in construction rather than in cells,
which is why they are reported as four numbers and not averaged.
One consequence is worth stating because it bounds how much the grid can find: on the seven-system
board exactly one of the $120$ pairs of cells gives identical hit vectors for every system,
canonical and InChIKey at top-1, so the sixteen cells are sixteen and not fewer.

\paragraph{Which leaderboards the grid applies to, decided before the shares were read.} A grid
cell has to be a choice the evaluation could have been made under. Two conditions decide it, and
both exclude cells rather than admit them. First, a matching criterion has to be an identity
relation: \textsc{tanimoto1} is reported in Table~\ref{tab:matchsens}, because published work
compares Morgan fingerprints, but it is not a grid cell, since at radius $2$ over $2048$ bits it
returns $1$ for decane against undecane and for D- against L-alanine, and a rule that identifies a
homologue with its reference is not a candidate answer to whether two structures are the same
compound. Second, the metric must be defined independently of the criterion. That is what excludes
the molecular-generation set: its headline quantity counts \emph{distinct} canonical strings, so the
criterion is not a parameter of the evaluation but part of the definition of what is being counted,
and re-scoring it under another criterion changes the metric rather than the convention. The
generation results are therefore reported in Appendix~\ref{app:xdomain} as a criterion sensitivity
and not as a row here. Both rules were fixed before the shares below were read, and the second
excludes the one leaderboard whose pairs are furthest apart, which would have raised the shares.


\paragraph{A leaderboard from outside chemistry, whose convention ships as a function.} The two
translation rows are the same instrument on a domain sharing none of this paper's data: the ranking
is human, the items are sentences, and the systems are machine-translation models. WMT's general
task publishes an expert annotation of every error in every segment, its category and its severity,
and turns those annotations into a number. The turning is the convention, and it is a function in
the task's own repository. It makes four decisions a paper quoting an \textsc{mqm} score does not
repeat: an error in the source text is charged nothing, a creative reinterpretation is charged
nothing, a minor punctuation error is charged a tenth of an ordinary minor, and a non-translation is
charged five times a major. So the criterion axis is which annotations are charged --- membership,
as a structure-matching rule is, with the metric fixed as the mean of the per-segment sums --- and
the second axis is whether that sum is floored at the non-translation weight, an \textsc{mqm} score
being otherwise unbounded below.

The published cell is checked rather than assumed. The task does not rank by the mean over
segments: it takes the mean within each domain and then the mean of those, which is what its own
clustering script does by default. The cell is weighted so that its mean is that average, and both
boards then reproduce the published ranking system for system. That agreement is a unit test on the
weighting and not evidence about the data: the function that checks it rebuilds the same average
from the same transcribed weights, and two routes to one quantity must agree. What it does
establish is that the cell is the aggregation the task documents, which a reader can check against
that script --- as they can check the transcription of the weights, which this run does not.

Reading the published number as the mean of the column instead is a choice of exactly the kind the
grid varies, and on this board it costs more than either axis in it. Under that reading the
nineteen-system leaderboard has $135$ dominating pairs and no certified reversal, against $125$ and
six under the task's own aggregation, and its published cell no longer reproduces the official
ranking: nine systems change place. On the fifteen-system board the same reading changes almost
nothing --- $97$ dominating pairs against $96$, and no certified reversal either way --- so the cost
of getting the aggregation wrong is a fact about the board rather than a constant. Both readings are
run on both boards and both are kept, so the size of the choice is measured here rather than
asserted.

documents, is this. On $171$ pairs of $19$ systems,

The same rule fixes the cell on every board here, including the ones where it
changes nothing.

\paragraph{Nine more boards from the same task, and why they are nine and not one.} The same task
annotated nine further language pairs under a lighter protocol, \textsc{esa}, in which one expert
marks the error spans in a segment and then scores it out of a hundred. The systems entered differ
per pair, the annotators differ per pair, and the pairs were run independently, so these are nine
leaderboards rather than one leaderboard nine times --- which is the condition for reading a share
as a distribution instead of as a number that happened to come out of one table. Both axes are again
choices left to whoever scores: what the annotation is read as, the number out of a hundred or the
marked spans as a tally, and whether a score is used as the annotator wrote it or standardised
within that annotator first. The second is the field's own oldest open question in evaluation
practice, and a comparison quoting human scores almost never says which it used.

The published cell is the same aggregation as on the \textsc{mqm} boards, the mean within a domain
and then across domains, and one thing is simpler here: the number the task publishes is the
annotator's score itself, so there is no weighting function to transcribe and the only convention
the cell carries is the aggregation. As there, no evidence is claimed from any agreement with the
published ranking.

Across the nine, $553$ of $689$ pairs dominate, $33$ are contested of which $4$ survive correction, and $115$ published places support $62$. The share runs from $0.62$ to $0.91$ with a median of
$0.82$: narrower than the spread across domains, which is what one would expect of nine boards from
one task, and still not a constant.

\paragraph{The share does not replicate across editions, and that is the strongest form of the
claim.} Nine boards from one campaign, annotated under one set of instructions by one pool, cannot
tell a property of a leaderboard from a property of a year. The previous edition of the same task
annotated eight language pairs under a different protocol --- direct assessments, no error spans,
several ratings per segment, and between $116$ and $207$ annotators --- and five of its pairs are
pairs the later edition also scored. That is the one place in this paper where the same question is
put twice to independent data.

The answer is that the share does not carry over. Across the five repeated pairs it moves by $0.114$
on average and by as much as $0.285$; \textsc{en-zh} is at $0.900$ in one edition and $0.615$ in the
next. Nothing about the language, the domain or the difficulty changed enough to explain that, and
the two campaigns were run by the same organisers on comparable data. What changed is the protocol and the
pool, which is to say the leaderboard. The pools cannot be shown to be disjoint --- each campaign
anonymises its annotators into its own identifier space, so the two share no identifier by
construction and a rater who worked on both would be invisible --- and the claim made here is only
that the campaigns are separate, which is what the editions are. So a share is a fact about a table and its grid, and
quoting one as a benchmark's fingerprint --- ours included --- would be an error of the same shape
as the one this paper is about. These eight boards are built on the release put through the preprocessing its own README
documents --- a quality-control filter that discards a rater's whole document when a planted item in
it was failed, and the removal of document-level meta scores --- and reading the file without it inflates every board by between a ninth and two fifths. With the filter the published
cell reproduces the task's own scores to $3\times10^{-14}$ and orders all $909$ pairs the way its
published table does.

Across the eight, $804$ of $909$ pairs dominate, $20$ are contested
of which $1$ survive correction, and $124$ published places support $78$.


\paragraph{One correction per board, when the claim ranges over twenty-three.} Holm is applied
within each grid, which controls the error rate of a claim about \emph{that} leaderboard and not of
the claim this paper makes. The honest family is the union of the grids: $15{,}592$ cell-level
tests. Re-applying the same procedure to the concatenated $p$-vectors --- no board re-analysed, no
prediction re-scored --- rejects $8{,}313$ of them at a cutoff of $p=6.9\times10^{-6}$, and the
count of certified pairs falls from $21$ to $14$. Splitting $\alpha$ across the boards instead, at
$\alpha/23$ within each grid, gives $14$ as well. What the second claim is about does not move at
all: all $5$ certified reversals of an ordering a published cell had separated survive every one of
the three, and the matching rule certifies none under any of them. That is checked under each
rather than inferred from the others, because the three are not nested: splitting $\alpha$ across
the boards and pooling their grids into one family weight a board by different things --- its own
size against the total --- and on sixteen of the twenty-three the union is the more permissive of
the two stricter readings.

What the union costs is concentrated rather than spread: the nineteen-system translation board
keeps $2$ of $6$, two more go on \textsc{en-hi} and one on \textsc{zho-eng}, and the retrosynthesis
boards keep all ten. Those three boards are the whole fall of seven, and every pair that goes is the
reversal of an ordering its own board had never established. What removes them is the size of the
family and nothing subtler: every threshold tightens by roughly the factor the family grew by, and
each lost reversal misses its own rank threshold outright. A step-down procedure can additionally
lose a small-$p$ test to an earlier failure, and on this vector it does not --- no test beyond the
cutoff would have passed its own threshold --- so the losses cannot be charged to the stopping rule.

\paragraph{The task's own answer to the same question.} A count no one else computes is a count no
one else can check, and the objection that the instrument is idiosyncratic is answered by the one
domain that computes its own version and publishes it. The translation task tests every pair with a
Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the paired per-segment scores within each domain, combines the domains
by Stouffer's $Z$, and merges systems into a cluster whenever that test does not separate them at
$0.05$; the number of clusters is its answer to how many ranks its table supports. Their code is
imported rather than reimplemented --- \texttt{get\_pvalues} and \texttt{get\_clusters} as written ---
and run on the two \textsc{mqm} boards, which is where the task ships this clustering in the form we
can execute.

Setting the two counts side by side settles nothing, because they differ in two places at once. The
test that decides whether a pair is separated is one; the construction that turns pairwise verdicts
into a count is the other, and theirs walks the score-sorted table and closes a cluster only when
nothing at or above the current position is tied with anything below it. A cluster is therefore an
interval of the published order and can contain pairs its own test separates, while ours is the
longest chain in the separation relation --- which on identical verdicts is never shorter. So the
two are crossed instead:

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
 & nineteen systems & fifteen systems \\
\midrule
their test, their construction   & $4$  & $6$ \\
our test, their construction     & $3$  & $5$ \\
their test, our construction     & $11$ & $9$ \\
our test, our construction       & $6$  & $8$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Read down the first two rows: holding the construction fixed, our test is the stricter of the two,
resolving one place fewer on each board. Read the next two: holding the test fixed, our construction
is the more generous, because a longest chain counts a place whenever some pair is separated while
a cluster refuses to close over an unresolved tie anywhere below it. The number this paper reports
is the last row, and the difference between it and the task's own is those two effects pulling
against each other rather than either one alone.

What that establishes is narrower than the objection it answers, and worth stating narrowly. It is
run on two boards of the nineteen, since those are the two the task ships in a form this can be
executed on. Neither side corrects for multiplicity here, so both counts are the optimistic ones,
which is what makes them comparable at all. And the ordering our published cell produces is theirs
system for system on both boards --- that is the weighting check of the previous section arriving
again, not an agreement between two instruments, which the table above shows there is not.

\paragraph{A null with a size.} The paper's second claim is negative --- the matching rule
certifies no reversal of an ordering a published cell had separated --- and a negative claim is
worth whatever its power is. The identity supplies that without another statistic. A criterion
reverses a pair exactly when it erases the whole margin between them and more, so the share of the
published margin it erases, $1 - d'/d$, says how close it came, and it is read straight out of the
per-cell means each board already reports. The sign matters: a criterion that moves a margin
\emph{away} from zero has not come close to reversing it, and a direction-blind ratio scores such a
comparison as if it had.

Taken over the pairs the published cell separates --- the only pairs with an established ordering to
reverse --- and with the other axis held at the board's own setting, the matching rule erases at most
$0.0870$ of a margin on the seven-system retrosynthesis board, $0.5755$ on the three-system one and
$0.0857$ on the metabolite board, and on all three the median comparison erases $0.0$: half the time
the criterion leaves the margin exactly where the published cell put it. On the closest of the three
the movement would have to be $1.74$ times larger before an ordering flipped at all. The null is a
size, then, and not a silence: an axis whose movement is well inside the margins it would have to
cross, rather than a test that failed to reach significance. What the axis does instead is exchange
pairs the benchmark never separated, which is this paper's first claim and is measured separately.

Docking is the exception, and is reported as one. There the criterion axis does erase a margin the
published cell had separated and then some --- \textsc{deepdock} over \textsc{unimol}, $2.8357$ of
it, at the published post-processing --- so on that board the matching rule is capable of the
reversal the null denies elsewhere. What it is not capable of is surviving the family it was read
in: that test reaches $p=0.053$ against the board's threshold of $9.8\times10^{-4}$, and no reversal
on that board survives, which is why its certified column reads zero. The one field that published
its own reversal is where one would expect the axis to bite, and it bites hard enough to move an
established margin and not hard enough to be certified.

\paragraph{What the share does not measure.} It is not an error rate and no cell is privileged.
The instrument takes the grid as declared and says what part of a published ordering is invariant
to it; whether a given cell is one a field would defend is a question about the field, argued in
the text and not settled by the instrument. Nor is the interval on a share a statement about the
grid: it resamples items with the grid held fixed, so it reports how much of the share is the
sample. The grid's contribution is what Proposition 2 and the curve below report.
