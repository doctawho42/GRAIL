\begin{abstract}
Computational prediction of drug metabolites is assessed almost universally by top-$k$ recall, the
fraction of known metabolites a method recovers. A single number cannot say why a method scores
what it does, because a metabolite is missed for three unrelated reasons: no rule in the method's
knowledge base expresses the transformation, the applicable rule is not selected, or the correct
product is generated but ranked too low to report. Improving the rule base, the selector and the
ranker are different research programmes, and recall does not indicate which is warranted.

We decompose recall exactly into coverage, selection and ranking for methods built on an
enumerable rule base, and apply it to two of them. The comparison explains the leaderboard rather
than restating it. The method with the wider rule base loses: it reaches more chemistry and
converts a third of that reach, while its competitor reaches less and converts nearly all of it,
having no selection stage to lose anything at. The gap is the price of selecting at all, which
recall charges only to methods that select. That is a claim about benchmark design rather than
about chemistry, and it predicts something checkable: an aggregate crediting hits without charging
for volume must favour architectures that do not filter.

Every factor counts references matched against predictions, so the decomposition is defined only
relative to a stated rule for when two structures are the same molecule. That rule is never stated
in this field and has never been varied. Varying it over five conventions with predictions held
fixed erases a statistically significant advantage between two published tools on the external set;
on our full split it leaves the recall ordering stable to within noise and moves the F1 ordering
instead. The aggregate is the second undeclared parameter and the more consequential one. Reporting F1
rather than recall drops the recall leader to last across three methods on the full split and
reverses two pairs of five with certification on both sides of each reversal --- and the
decomposition says in advance which pairs, from a structural property rather than after the fact. We release the protocol, the audited split and
the frozen predictions that make both measurements repeatable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

When a drug enters the body it is chemically transformed. Enzymes, principally in the liver,
oxidise, cleave and conjugate the administered compound into a set of new molecules called
metabolites. Which metabolites form is a central question in drug development, because a
metabolite may be inactive, may carry the therapeutic effect, or may be toxic in ways the parent
compound is not. Regulatory review attends closely to major human metabolites, and identifying them
experimentally is slow and expensive. Computational prediction of metabolite
structures therefore has direct practical value: given the structure of an administered compound,
predict the structures of the molecules the body will produce from it.

Two families of methods address this problem. Rule-based systems encode known biotransformations
as reaction templates, apply every applicable template to the input compound, and rank the
resulting products by an empirical score \citep{Ridder_2008, de_Bruyn_Kops_2020,
Djoumbou_Feunang_2019}. Machine-learning systems instead learn the transformation directly, either
as a sequence-to-sequence translation between molecular strings or as a classifier over reaction
types \citep{Litsa_2020, Zhu_2024}. Hybrid systems combine the two, using a learned model to
decide which of many templates to apply. Whatever the underlying approach, published methods are
compared on the same quantity: top-$k$ recall, the fraction of experimentally observed metabolites
that appear among a method's $k$ highest-ranked predictions.

That comparison hides what it is measuring. A rule-based or hybrid method can fail to recover a
metabolite for three unrelated reasons: the required transformation may have no corresponding
template in its rule base, the template may exist but not be selected, or the correct product may
be generated but ranked too low to be reported. Recall confounds these, so a low score identifies
no deficiency and suggests no remedy. Improving the rule base, improving template selection and
improving ranking are different research programmes, and the aggregate score does not indicate
which is warranted.

This work separates them. For a method whose rule base can be enumerated, recall factorises
exactly into coverage, selection and ranking, and the factorisation is a measurement rather than an
estimate: the coverage term is obtained by applying the entire rule base and asking what it
reaches. Applied to two such methods it explains a leaderboard instead of restating one. The method
with the wider rule base reaches more chemistry and converts a third of that reach; the one with
the narrower base converts nearly all of its own, because it applies every applicable rule and so
has no selection stage in which to lose anything. It wins by half a leaderboard position for that
reason alone. The observation generalises past this pair and past chemistry: an aggregate counting hits without
charging for the volume emitted is not neutral between architectures, since recall charges the cost
of a selection stage only to architectures that have one. That is a question about benchmark
design, and \S\ref{sec:metric} answers it by changing the aggregate and watching the leaderboard
move as the factorisation predicts.

Making the factorisation well-posed exposes a second problem, which is why the paper addresses it
first. Every factor counts references matched against predictions, so all three move with the rule
deciding when a predicted structure is the same molecule as a reported one, and chemistry offers
several defensible rules: canonical SMILES, hashed identifiers, identifiers with stereochemistry
removed, or fingerprints. They disagree most about tautomers, alternative structures of one
compound differing in the position of a mobile hydrogen. Evaluations here adopt one each, usually
without saying why, and none has varied it. Varying it over five conventions with predictions held
fixed erases a significant advantage between two published tools externally, and internally moves
the F1 ordering while leaving recall's stable to within noise. The second free parameter is the aggregate
itself: report F1 rather than recall and the leader becomes the straggler. That matching rules and
metric choices can reorder leaderboards is established in other fields; that this field's rankings
turn on two undeclared ones is not.

We release the bundle the protocol needs rather than a model: the leakage-audited split and its
audit report, the re-scoring harness with all five criteria, every method's frozen predictions under
each, and GRAIL's checkpoints. Adding a sixth method needs its ranked output and nothing else.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Metabolite-structure prediction.} Rule-based prediction of metabolite structures was
established by SyGMa \citep{Ridder_2008}, which encodes phase I (oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis) and phase II
(conjugation) biotransformations as reaction templates and scores the resulting products by the empirical frequency with which each
template yields an observed metabolite. GLORYx \citep{de_Bruyn_Kops_2020} couples a comparable
template library to a machine-learned model of sites of metabolism, the atoms at which an enzyme acts, and BioTransformer
\citep{Djoumbou_Feunang_2019} extends the template approach to microbial and environmental
transformations. Learned alternatives dispense with templates: MetaTrans \citep{Litsa_2020} treats
metabolite prediction as sequence-to-sequence translation between molecular strings, and
MetaPredictor \citep{Zhu_2024} combines a transformer with a filtering stage. These systems are
evaluated on overlapping but non-identical datasets and, as discussed below, under non-identical
notions of a correct prediction.

\paragraph{Comparative evaluations in this domain.} Several studies have placed these tools side by
side. \citet{Scholz_2023} benchmarked five predictors on 85 agrochemical parent compounds and
reported low first-generation precision together with substantial divergence between rule-based
and learned systems. \citet{Boyce_2022} compared six tools on 37 chemicals and found that the
system with the broadest coverage also produced the largest number of incorrect predictions.
\citet{Gao_2026} evaluated predictors against human radiolabelled ADME data and saw the same
coverage-precision trade-off. Each fixes a single criterion for judging a prediction correct, and
none reports how sensitive its conclusions are to it.

\paragraph{Structure standardisation.} The difficulty of deciding when two molecular structures
denote the same compound is documented independently of metabolite prediction.
\citet{Dhaked_2020} catalogue tautomeric transformations and show that the standard InChI
identifier normalises only a subset of them. \citet{Hahnke_2018} report that roughly 60\% of
PubChem records differ from their canonical InChI representation, predominantly through tautomer
choice, and \citet{Mansouri_2024} describe a standardisation pipeline developed for
quantitative structure-activity modelling. This literature treats standardisation as a
preprocessing step applied before analysis, rather than as a parameter of the evaluation itself.

\paragraph{Evaluation methodology in adjacent fields.} That methodological choices alter the
apparent ordering of methods is recognised more broadly, along two axes this paper also varies.
On the matching rule, \citet{Maziarz_2025} re-evaluated retrosynthesis models under a standardised
procedure and reversed a published ordering, partly because one model had been scored under a
relaxed stereochemistry criterion its baselines did not get; it is the closest precedent here, and
the only one that varies a structure-matching rule. \citet{liu2026massspecgymwild} audit published
mass-spectrometry work and name the mechanism directly, divergent metric implementations and
stereochemistry handling in InChIKey matching among them. Outside chemistry, re-scoring frozen
text-to-SQL submissions under denotation equivalence rather than exact match shows exact match
misrepresenting the state of the art \citep{Zhong_2020}, and re-scoring frozen question-answering
outputs under four answer-equivalence rules makes one system best under a rule it had lost under
\citep{Kamalloo_2023}. On the aggregate, \citet{agarwal2026metric} show for drug-response
prediction that the choice of scoring metric inverts which model wins. The closest precedent to
what we find is \citet{Gao_2025_reframing}, who show that structure-based drug design's standard
score rises with a structural property of the output, its atom count, rather than with quality, so
that re-evaluating under practically aligned metrics changes which models look good; the same
field's benchmark was rebuilt around four complementary axes for the same reason
\citep{Lin_2025_cbgbench}. Weighting examples by difficulty reorders natural-language leaderboards
\citep{Mishra_2021, Rodriguez_2021}. Data
splitting has received similar attention \citep{Joeres_2025}.

\paragraph{Remaining gap.} Neither axis is in doubt in general, and neither has been varied for
metabolite predictors. What is unestablished is how far each moves this field's rankings when it
is the only thing changing; and no stage-wise attribution of a predictor's score has been
reported for metabolite predictors, though the device is established elsewhere, \citet{Maziarz_2025}
separating single-step accuracy from search in retrosynthesis. That is what would let the two be
explained rather than only reported.

\section{TAME: an evaluation protocol with a declared matching criterion}
\label{sec:tame}

TAME is a \emph{protocol, an audited split and a re-scoring harness}, not a leaderboard service:
it exists so that the numbers in \S\ref{sec:results} are directly comparable and regenerable from
committed artifacts.

\paragraph{The matching criterion.} The field has never agreed what counts as a match. GLORYx uses a stereo-blind InChIKey skeleton,
MetaTrans compares Morgan fingerprints, and rule-based systems that canonicalise their own output,
SyGMa among them, effectively compare canonical SMILES. Each is a different equivalence relation,
and each is applied silently. TAME exposes all of them as named quotients: predicted and reference
structures are mapped through one of
\[
q \in \{\textsc{canonical},\ \textsc{inchikey},\ \textsc{inchi-no-stereo},\ \textsc{tanimoto1},\
\textsc{inchikey-tautomer}\}
\]
and a prediction counts as a hit iff $q(\hat{y}) = q(y)$ for some reference $y$. Each map is
computed with RDKit 2022.09.5, the version that reproduces the columns reported here: \textsc{canonical} is canonical SMILES equality;
\textsc{inchikey} is the full InChIKey; \textsc{inchi-no-stereo} is its first block, the
connectivity skeleton, which is blind to stereochemistry, charge and isotopes; \textsc{tanimoto1}
is equality of Morgan fingerprints at radius 2 over 2048 bits, which is Tanimoto similarity of one;
and \textsc{inchikey-tautomer} standardises both sides before hashing, taking the fragment parent,
neutralising charges, canonicalising tautomers, and writing SMILES without stereochemistry.

Our recommended default is \textsc{inchikey-tautomer}. Standard InChI normalises only a
\emph{subset} of tautomers \citep{Dhaked_2020}, so a rule engine emitting a keto form of an enol
reference fails to match under plain \textsc{inchikey} despite producing the same entity. Two
properties of this default bear on every number below: it is the most permissive of the five, and
it is stereo-blind as well as tautomer-blind, so a predicted D-alanine matches an L-alanine
reference. The stereo relaxation is not incidental, since several methods here, GRAIL among them,
standardise their output without stereochemistry and cannot emit a stereocentre, so a
stereo-sensitive criterion would score them on a representation they never produce. We report every
result under all five criteria rather than defending one.

Unless stated otherwise all results use \textsc{inchikey-tautomer};
\S\ref{sec:rankflip} quantifies how much the ranking depends on that choice. A worked example in
which the same fixed predictions score a near-total miss or a full hit purely by changing $q$ is
given in Appendix~\ref{app:worked}.

Standardisation of this kind is established preprocessing (\S\ref{sec:related}); what is new
here is adopting it as the \emph{matching} rule and measuring how far it moves the leaderboard.

\paragraph{Split and leakage audit.} All splits are substrate-disjoint: no substrate in test or
validation appears in train, and no test substrate appears in validation. Clean triples are
constructed and then audited read-only by a separate script that canonicalises SMILES and verifies
zero substrate and zero positive-pair overlap across every split pair, emitting a machine-checkable
report. Leakage-aware splitting is itself not novel, since DataSAIL \citep{Joeres_2025} minimises cross-split similarity on
MoleculeNet-style tasks. The leak that matters here is
substrate--metabolite \emph{identity} overlap, which is domain-specific. As a secondary check, the deployed configuration
gives macro recall@15 $=0.344$ on validation against $0.334$ on test (293 and 291 substrates,
superseded on the full split by \S\ref{sec:diagnosis}). The $+0.010$ gap runs in the expected
direction but matches the seed-to-seed standard deviation, so it corroborates the zero-overlap
audit rather than standing as independent evidence.

\paragraph{Metrics.} We lead with recall@$k$ co-reported with the mean output size, not with
precision alone: under incomplete annotation precision is a \emph{pessimistic lower bound}, since an
unannotated candidate scores as a false positive even when it is a real but unrecorded metabolite.
Recall without output size misleads in the other direction, a method being able to buy recall by
enumerating (Appendix~\ref{app:budget}). Selection happens on validation, with two exceptions we
name rather than imply. The deployed
filter's encoder variant was chosen by comparing both variants on test
(Appendix~\ref{app:arch}); that choice fixes which encoder is deployed and so reaches every GRAIL
number here, the headline included. The exploratory analyses of Appendix~\ref{app:negative} were also scored on test, before this
discipline was tightened, and support no claim. Everything else we report on test --- the
decomposition, the learned-versus-prior comparison, the breadth sweep --- is measurement, not
selection.

\section{The decomposition and the coverage ceiling}
\label{sec:decomp}

A rule-grounded predictor reaches a reference metabolite only by passing three gates in sequence:
the transformation must be
\emph{expressible} by some rule in GRAIL's bank of \nbank\ reaction rules, that rule must be
\emph{selected} into the substrate's candidate pool under a finite budget, and the resulting
product must be \emph{ranked} into the top $k$. Write, for a substrate set $S$ with reference sets
$Y_s$:
\[
\underbrace{\frac{|H|}{|U|}}_{\text{recall@}k}
\;=\;
\underbrace{\frac{|C_{\text{full}}|}{|U|}}_{\text{coverage}}
\;\cdot\;
\underbrace{\frac{|C_{\text{bud}}|}{|C_{\text{full}}|}}_{\text{selection}}
\;\cdot\;
\underbrace{\frac{|H|}{|C_{\text{bud}}|}}_{\text{ranking}},
\qquad
|U|=\textstyle\sum_{s\in S}|Y_s| ,
\]
where $C_{\text{full}}$ are references recovered by applying the \emph{entire} bank without a
budget, $C_{\text{bud}}$ those surviving the selector's budgeted pool, and $H$ those actually
returned in the top $k$. The first factor requires an enumerable rule base, so the factorisation
runs on the rule-grounded methods compared here and not on the sequence-to-sequence ones, which
have no bank to apply. That is a property of the method class rather than a limit of the
measurement: any method with a rule base decomposes, and the two here span the design space from a
selector that chooses to one that does not. The identity is exact by construction (each set contains the next), so
the three factors partition the loss with no residual: it is an accounting device, not a model.
Its value is diagnostic: a single recall number cannot indicate \emph{which} gate closed, while
the three corresponding interventions address unrelated components of the pipeline. Two
aggregations are in use and every figure is labelled with which. Table~\ref{tab:decomp} reports
\emph{micro}, the pooled ratio of sums written above, because only that form makes the factors
multiply out exactly; the head-to-head comparisons report \emph{macro}, the mean of per-substrate
recalls, which is the comparator tools' convention. The two differ here, $0.261$ against $0.330$
(Appendix~\ref{app:formal}).

\paragraph{The ceiling as a primitive.} The first factor is measurable without any model: apply
all \nbank\ rules to every test substrate and ask what fraction of references appears anywhere in
the union. That is the bank's \textbf{coverage ceiling}, the best recall achievable if selection
and ranking were both perfect. On the clean test split it is \ceiling\ (cluster-bootstrap 95\% CI $[0.709,0.762]$). It should be
reported alongside recall for any rule-grounded method: it separates what the knowledge base can
express from what the model does with it, and no ranking improvement can exceed it.

\paragraph{What the decomposition says.} Table~\ref{tab:decomp} localises the loss. The deployed
pipeline converts only $35.5\%$ of its own ceiling into realised recall, and the three factors
separate cleanly, with no pair of intervals overlapping. Selection is the binding constraint:
barely a third of what the bank can reach survives the selector's rule budget. Ranking is close to
lossless at $0.963$ $[0.946,0.978]$, so once a candidate is in the budgeted pool it almost always
reaches the output. The decomposition therefore points at selection, and specifically at how few
rules are applied, as the stage where intervention is warranted.

Applied to a second method it stops being self-diagnosis. SyGMa is rule-grounded with an
enumerable bank, so it decomposes on the same split in the same frame, and its profile is the
inverse of ours: its bank reaches less, $0.542$ against $\ceiling$, but it applies every applicable
rule and emits the result, so it converts almost all of that reach where we convert a third of a
wider one. The gap between us is therefore not a worse rule base but the cost of having a selection
stage at all, which recall charges only to methods that select. Two methods lose recall for opposite reasons, and a single score says only that one scores lower.
The frame also bounds this architecture: a perfect selector and ranker would stop at \ceiling, and
that ceiling is corpus-limited rather than a consequence of tuning, since 53\% of uncovered
transformations are of reaction types absent from the bank (Appendix~\ref{app:props}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The criterion the decomposition rests on}
\label{sec:rankflip}

Because every factor of the decomposition counts matched references, the criterion has to be
settled before the decomposition means anything. Published methods have their \emph{frozen}
predictions re-scored by the TAME harness under all five criteria, so only the match rule changes
across columns, never the chemistry. Three of them
--- GRAIL, SyGMa \citep{Ridder_2008} and MetaPredictor \citep{Zhu_2024} --- have predictions for
the whole clean test split and are compared there, paired on identical substrates
(Table~\ref{tab:matchsens}). Two more, BioTransformer \citep{Djoumbou_Feunang_2019} and MetaTrans
\citep{Litsa_2020}, exist only on a 150-substrate shared subset; that five-method comparison is
reported in Appendix~\ref{app:xdomain}.

Methods are not equally sensitive to the convention. Over the full split GRAIL gains $0.016$
$[0.010,0.023]$ from canonical to tautomer matching against MetaPredictor's $0.040$
$[0.030,0.050]$, a differential of $+0.024$ $[0.014,0.034]$ surviving Holm--Bonferroni over the
three pairs. The same differential against BioTransformer on the 150-substrate subset is $+0.120$,
five times larger; we treat the full-split figure as the estimate, since a subset chosen for data
availability overstated the effect. Output size is a second, orthogonal confound: SyGMa emits $82$
candidates per substrate against GRAIL's $8.4$ (Appendix~\ref{app:budget}).

\paragraph{Primary endpoint.} The endpoint is the \emph{differential} sensitivity, not any individual reversal: the protocol
$\times$ method interaction, how much more one method gains from a more tolerant criterion than
another, with a paired bootstrap over substrates ($10{,}000$ resamples). Every method gains under a
more tolerant criterion, so only the differential can reorder anything. The design was fixed before
the data, the five criteria named and the re-scoring of frozen predictions specified twelve days
before the first result; the estimator was not, the differential and the paired bootstrap being
chosen at analysis time. We correct for multiplicity: under Holm--Bonferroni at
$\alpha=0.05$ every interaction called significant here survives on both sets, internally even when
the family is taken conservatively as all ten pairs the five-method table admits. Two independent
pairs are significant along two protocol axes, BioTransformer gaining \interA\ \interAci\ more than
GRAIL from canonical$\rightarrow$tautomer and SyGMa \interB\ \interBci\ more than MetaTrans from
canonical$\rightarrow$InChIKey. The convention is a \textbf{method-dependent confounder}, not a
neutral scoring choice.

\paragraph{Two reversals follow, neither individually significant.} Two pairs exchange places
between criteria on the shared subset, one non-monotonically because MetaTrans alone emits isomeric
SMILES. Every pairwise $\Delta$ there spans zero at $n=\nshared$ (Appendix~\ref{app:xdomain}), so
the interaction is established and the reversals only illustrate it.

\paragraph{External replication.} The results above live on a $\nshared$-substrate subset of our
own split, so we repeated the analysis on the field's de-facto external hold-out, the \nglory\
GLORYx drugs \citep{de_Bruyn_Kops_2020}, whose own tool is not scored here because the weights of
its site-of-metabolism component are not available for re-execution. Four methods are compared
across the criterion ladder (Table~\ref{tab:matchsens}). Sensitivity spans more than an order of
magnitude across them, and three of the four intervals exclude zero. Of the six method pairs, four show a significant interaction, all four surviving
Holm--Bonferroni over the six; the largest in magnitude are GRAIL$\leftrightarrow$SyGMa ($-0.171$
$[-0.264,-0.086]$) and BioTransformer$\leftrightarrow$GRAIL ($+0.150$ $[+0.069,+0.242]$). The
ordering consequence is sharpest for a third, MetaPredictor$\leftrightarrow$SyGMa ($-0.135$
$[-0.209,-0.072]$): under strict InChIKey SyGMa leads by a \emph{significant} $-0.130$
$[-0.196,-0.065]$; one rung of tolerance later, once stereochemistry is disregarded, that
advantage is gone and the point estimate reverses ($+0.005$, n.s.). A criterion chosen for convenience can therefore erase a statistically significant advantage
between two published tools, on an external literature-standard set with a different method
quartet.



\paragraph{Mechanism.} The spread tracks how far a method's outputs diverge
\emph{representationally} from the references it is scored against, and the intermediate column
says which divergence is responsible. Here it is stereochemistry alone: every separating gain is
realised by the stereo step, and the tautomer step that follows is zero for all but one method. GLORYx references carry stereocentres, and several of
these systems standardise their own output without stereochemistry and cannot emit one, so a
stereo-sensitive criterion scores them on a representation they never produce. Tautomerism is what fails to match internally, where references are already stereo-stripped;
stereochemistry is what fails here. Which relaxation dominates is a property of the reference set,
not of the criterion. In retrosynthesis, whose references are canonical and stereochemically
explicit, the same criteria span about $0.02$ (Appendix~\ref{app:xdomain}).

\subsection{The metric is a second free parameter, and the decomposition predicts it}
\label{sec:metric}

Recall is not the only aggregate this field reports; F1 and Jaccard are standard, and a method
emitting eighty candidates to catch a few more references is rewarded by the first and punished by
the others. Computed on each method's deployed output set over the full split --- untruncated, since precision
and Jaccard are set quantities --- the orderings disagree, and the disagreement is certified in both
directions. SyGMa leads on recall, by $0.026$ over MetaPredictor with a paired 95\% interval of
$[0.003,0.049]$, and finishes last on F1, $0.115$ below it, $[0.108,0.121]$. GRAIL, last on recall,
is $0.089$ above SyGMa on F1, $[0.078,0.100]$, and $0.079$ above it on precision,
$[0.069,0.089]$.

The aggregate has a second dial, where the output is truncated, and it moves this same pair: under
the top-15 cap of Table~\ref{tab:matchsens} SyGMa's lead is gone and MetaPredictor leads by $0.002$.
A cap set by convention rewards the method that never reaches it, deciding a comparison the uncapped
measurement certifies the other way, and it is mis-set for this endpoint in every method here. With the constant chosen out of sample, macro F1 rises from what each emits to
$0.195$ for GRAIL at $k=1$, $0.254$ for MetaPredictor at $k=2$ and $0.217$ for SyGMa at $k=4$,
against the $k=15$ the field reports at. For GRAIL, whose per-candidate scores make the comparison
paired, emitting as deployed is certified \emph{worse} than the constant, by $-0.058$
$[-0.075,-0.042]$ (Appendix~\ref{app:props}).

This is not confined to the two methods the decomposition runs on. Across all five on the shared
subset, four cluster on F1 with every pairwise difference covering zero while SyGMa sits below all
four by $0.094$ to $0.127$; on recall that subset puts SyGMa \emph{above} two, by $0.109$ and
$0.197$ over BioTransformer and GRAIL, both certified. Two pairs therefore reverse with certification on both sides, and both set the one method with no
selection stage against methods emitting a seventh as many candidates. We order comparators here by
emitted output size, not by the selection factor, which is measured only for the two methods the
decomposition runs on. SyGMa alone emits at that scale, so this exhibits a mechanism rather than
estimating a dependence (Appendix~\ref{app:xdomain}).

The decomposition says why, and this is where the paper's two halves meet. SyGMa's selection factor
is $1$ because it emits every product its applicable rules generate. That is what recall rewards and
precision punishes, so a pipeline with no selection stage is scored relatively higher by the first
than by the second. It need not lead on recall outright, coverage being a free axis: SyGMa's bank
reaches $0.542$ against GRAIL's $\ceiling$. The factorisation supplies the direction of the
movement, from a structural property rather than after the fact.

The matching criterion acts on these metrics too, and where it barely touched recall. Over the full
split the recall@15 ordering is stable to within noise, the leading pair exchanging places by at
most $0.0014$ with a differential sensitivity of $-0.002$ $[-0.009,+0.006]$. The F1 gap between
GRAIL and MetaPredictor is another matter: it is certified under four criteria, at $-0.026$ $[-0.038,-0.013]$ under the
tautomer-aware default, and indistinguishable from zero under plain \textsc{inchikey}, at
$+0.003$ $[-0.009,+0.015]$. That is an erased gap rather than a certified reversal, and we claim it
as no more than that. Two undeclared free parameters therefore sit between a set of predictions and
a leaderboard position, and they do not act on the same comparisons.

Precision here is a lower bound, which threatens this result more than anything else in the paper,
since the method the metric demotes emits the most candidates. Modelling the annotation as a uniform
propensity $c$ identifies what survives: recall is unbiased, precision is the truth scaled by $c$ so
its ordering holds for every $c$, and F1 is neither, but its ordering survives to $c\approx0.055$ ---
a rate at which a substrate with three recorded metabolites would have fifty-five
(Appendix~\ref{app:props}).


\subsection{Comparison against published methods}
\label{sec:diagnosis}

Four GRAIL recall figures appear and none is interchangeable: $\realised$ is micro, the pooled ratio
of hits to references; $\grailmacro$ is macro over the output as emitted; $0.340$ is macro after the
harness collapses duplicates, which is what it does for every method and so is the figure used in
any cross-method table; and $0.344\pm0.010$ is the three-seed mean of that last one
(Appendix~\ref{app:xdomain}).
On the full clean test split GRAIL trails both published comparators: SyGMa reaches $0.572$, a
paired difference of $-0.242$ $[-0.271,\,-0.212]$ over the $1{,}168$ common substrates, and
MetaPredictor $0.568$. Nor does it lead at any output budget, a frontier over $k \in [1,64]$ finding
SyGMa ahead at \emph{every} $k$ (Appendix~\ref{app:budget}), so the gap is the selection weakness
\S\ref{sec:decomp} localises, not output-size accounting. They differ on that second axis besides,
$8.4$ candidates against $81.7$, which \S\ref{sec:metric} prices.


\subsection{Selection as extreme multi-label classification}
\label{sec:xmc}

Since selection is the dominant shortfall we ask what makes it hard, and the answer is structural
rather than metabolism-specific. Choosing which of \nbank\ rules apply is \textbf{extreme
multi-label classification} with a near-empty positive set: one to three rules per substrate carry a
positive label, some ${\sim}0.03\%$ of the label space, while far more are applicable, so what is
scarce is annotation rather than applicability. In that regime XMC has established that frequency
and propensity baselines are hard to beat, which is why propensity-scored losses were introduced
\citep{Jain_2016}; rule selection \emph{inherits} that pathology, unnamed here.

The prediction holds. Each arm selects thirty rules and passes them through the same enumeration
and filter: a learned-only selector reaches $0.266$ where rule-firing frequency alone reaches
$0.410$, a paired difference of $-0.144$ $[-0.196,\,-0.095]$, $n=245$. Under the propensity model
that governs precision above, a learner with constant unlabelled weighting recovers a distorted
score dominated by the marginal firing rate, so the prior is Bayes-competitive here \emph{by
construction}. The effect is selection-specific: at the ranking
stage on identical pools the learned filter beats the same prior ($0.413$ against $0.374$).
The framing also prescribes a remedy, and it fails: weighting positives by inverse estimated
propensity \citep{Jain_2016} moves the selector to $0.249$ from $0.266$. It treats rarity as an
annotation artifact, and here rarity is substantially chemistry (Appendix~\ref{app:props}).

\subsection{What constrains each factor}
\label{sec:evidence}

The diagnosis rests on eight measurements, not on the decomposition alone, each designed to move
exactly one factor so that a result failing to move recall is as informative as one that does
(Table~\ref{tab:levers}).


Five of the eight are interventions that failed, and they are the more useful half: applying rules
twice, doubling the training data, adding a site-of-metabolism prior and abstaining on low filter
scores are the obvious remedies, as is the propensity-scored loss the XMC framing prescribes, and
none works. Depth-2 buys $0.012$ of ceiling for $8.5$ times the
candidates and doubling the data changes nothing, which is why we attribute the shortfall to the
structure of the selection problem rather than to under-training. Abstention is sharpest: raising
the threshold removes true and apparently false candidates alike, an unannotated candidate being a
false positive whether or not it is real, so precision stays flat while recall collapses. Its
\emph{level} is bounded by the annotation rather than the threshold, even though
\S\ref{sec:metric} shows its ordering is not. Of the remaining three, two locate recoverable recall
at selection and the third characterises what the bank cannot reach in one step
(Appendix~\ref{app:props}).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Sample sizes differ.} The five-method table rests on $\nshared$ shared substrates,
because two comparators could only be run on that subset; GRAIL, SyGMa and MetaPredictor are scored
on the full $\ntest$, and the external replication is $n=\nglory$. At those sizes the
\emph{interactions} are significant but no individual pairwise reversal is, and we report them that
way throughout.

\paragraph{Two comparators cannot be re-run in this codebase.} MetaTrans's inference pipeline no
longer reproduces here and BioTransformer's configuration is unscripted, so both are represented by
frozen prediction files: reproducible from our artifacts but not regenerable from scratch.

\paragraph{Precision is a lower bound, not an estimate.} Reference sets are \emph{incomplete}: an
unannotated but chemically real product scores as a false positive, so every precision and F1 figure
is pessimistic. We rank on them in \S\ref{sec:metric} only because a uniform-propensity model
identifies the precision ordering and holds the F1 ordering down to an implausible annotation rate.
That model is assumed rather than estimated.

\paragraph{The single-step bound.} The ceiling is single-step-conditional. Some references are
multi-generation products no single rule application reaches, and depth-2 lifts the ceiling by only
${\sim}0.012$ at $8.5$ times the candidate cost, so $\ceiling$ bounds this formulation rather than
the task.

\paragraph{Evaluation hygiene of this paper itself.} Headline numbers come from a single evaluation
of the deployed configuration, with selection on validation but for two exceptions
(\S\ref{sec:tame}). The deployed filter's encoder variant was chosen by comparing variants on test,
and that choice reaches every GRAIL number here, the headline included; and the \emph{exploratory}
analyses in Appendix~\ref{app:negative} were scored on test before we tightened this discipline,
though they inform no headline claim and their conclusions are negative.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\looseness=-1
A recall figure for a rule-grounded predictor factorises exactly into what its rule base can
express, what its selector admits, and what its ranker returns. Applied to two published systems it
explains their ordering rather than repeating it: the wider bank converts a third of what it
reaches, the narrower nearly all of its own, and the gap is the price of selecting.

\looseness=-1
Two free parameters sit between predictions and a leaderboard position and this field declares
neither: the rule for when two structures are the same molecule, not neutral because methods differ
in how their outputs depart from the references, and the aggregate, under which the recall leader
finishes last because a pipeline that selects nothing is what recall rewards and precision punishes.
The paper's halves meet there. Both belong in any report, with the coverage ceiling wherever a rule
base exists.

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility statement}

All numbers are regenerable from committed artifacts, but for one reranker figure whose
per-substrate scores were not retained and which is flagged where it appears. The anonymized
archive holds every method's frozen predictions, the audits, the harness, the split construction,
the analysis code and the GRAIL checkpoints.
