\begin{abstract}
A leaderboard reports one total order over its methods, computed under evaluation choices that its
paper does not state: a rule deciding when a predicted structure counts as the reference, crossed
with a choice the field reports apart from its ranking and never jointly with it. This paper asks
what part of such an ordering survives when those choices are varied and nothing else is. We fix
the items and the released predictions, re-score them in every cell of a declared grid of choices,
and keep the pairwise orderings that no cell contradicts.

We do this for $23$ leaderboards in four domains --- retrosynthesis, metabolite prediction,
protein--ligand docking, and two editions of one machine-translation shared task --- retraining
nothing and reimplementing no method. Together those tables rank $293$ systems, and the orderings
that survive support $167$ ranks. We count the gap in two parts, because they mean different
things. Most of it is not the choices: requiring a leaderboard's own published cell to separate a
pair at all takes $293$ places to $136$, so $157$ ranks go to a benchmark's own resolving power
before any convention is varied and $39$ more to the grid. The surviving share runs from none of
three to nine tenths, and does not replicate between two editions of the same shared task on the
same language pair, so it is a property of a table and its grid rather than of a benchmark.

Of the orderings a table did establish, $5$ are reversed by a declared choice and survive
correction, under each board's own family and under a single correction over all $23$ grids at
once. None of the five is the matching rule's, which is the choice this literature discusses most.
Deciding which orderings are at risk needs no pairwise statistic: a change of criterion reverses a
pair only if it moves the two systems apart by more than the larger of the margins it connects, so
every verdict follows from $n$ scorings per cell, and resampling enters only to tell a contested
pair from an unresolved one. The four choices were found by measuring one predictor in one domain,
and that case study is reported in full. We release the protocol, the audited split, the frozen
predictions of every method compared, a checker that reports these choices from released files
alone, and the analysis code.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

This is a survey of published leaderboards and an instrument for reading them. The instrument
takes a leaderboard's released predictions and a declared set of evaluation choices and returns the
part of its ordering that no choice contradicts; the survey applies it to twenty-three leaderboards
in four domains. One predictor in one domain is used to find the choices worth declaring, and that
predictor is instrumentation rather than a contribution: it is not offered as a state of the art,
and nothing in the survey depends on how it performs.

The domain the choices were found in evaluates chemistry. Machine learning is used to predict what
a molecule will undergo: the metabolites enzymes will convert a drug into, the reactants a target
compound can be made from, the molecules that meet a design objective. All three are evaluated the
same way. A method proposes a ranked list of structures, a curated reference records which are
correct, and the method is scored by how often the two agree.

Whether two structures agree is not self-evident. A single compound can be drawn in several ways
that a chemist reads as one substance: as either of two tautomers, which differ in the position of a
mobile hydrogen; with or without the three-dimensional arrangement at a stereocentre; as any of
several equivalent strings. An evaluation must therefore fix a rule that decides when a predicted
structure counts as the reference structure. It must also fix how many candidates a method may
propose, because a method that proposes more will match more. Where predictions are generated by
applying reaction templates, it must fix the convention by which a template is matched against a
molecule, because a template that names a hydrogen explicitly will not match a structure whose
hydrogens are implicit. And it must fix which molecules the test set contains. These four choices
are made in every published evaluation and stated in almost none.

That evaluation choices can change conclusions is established in several fields
\citep{Maziarz_2025, Zhong_2020, Kamalloo_2023, agarwal2026metric}. What is not established is how
far any single choice moves a ranking when it is the only thing that changes, and in particular
whether a choice can \emph{reorder} a leaderboard rather than shifting every system on it by a
similar amount. The distinction matters, because a shift leaves conclusions intact and a reordering
does not.

We answer that question by re-scoring predictions that other groups have already released, so that
nothing is retrained and no method is reimplemented. Two of the four choices reorder a published leaderboard
and the reorderings are certified as interactions between method and choice, not asserted from a
change in rank; a third changes a reported quantity, what a rule base reaches, without changing any
ordering. The fourth is undeclared but produces no interaction that survives correction, and separating those two statements is possible only because the same test is
applied to all four. A single condition governs when a reordering occurs: the choice must move two
systems apart by more than the margin between them, which makes a benchmark more vulnerable as its
leading methods converge.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt \parskip0pt
\item \textbf{The instrument.} The part of a published ranking that no declared choice can
  contradict is a partial order --- the intersection of the per-cell total orders --- computable from
  released prediction files alone, together with its longest chain. Because it is an intersection it
  can only fall as a grid widens, so it is reported as a curve over the declared axes and never as
  one number, and a pair that fails it fails in one of two ways that are counted apart.
\item \textbf{The survey.} Measured on $23$ leaderboards in four domains and $1{,}922$ pairs, from
  retrosynthesis, metabolite prediction, protein--ligand docking and two editions of one machine
  translation task: they publish $293$ places and what survives supports $167$. The share runs from
  none of three to nine tenths, and does not replicate between two editions of the same task on the
  same language pair, so it is a property of a table and its grid rather than of a benchmark.
\item \textbf{What the gap is made of, which is mostly not the choices.} Requiring only a
  leaderboard's own cell to separate a pair already takes $293$ places to $136$: $157$ go to a
  benchmark's own power before any convention is varied and $39$ more to the grid. The matching
  criterion certifies no reversal of an ordering a published cell had separated, on any of the $23$
  boards --- each board's verdict corrected by Holm over the cell-level tests its own grid creates,
  and $14$ of the $21$ certified reversals of every kind surviving one correction over the union
  of all twenty-three grids; what the criterion does is exchange positions a benchmark never established.
\item \textbf{The arithmetic.} A pair's ordering survives a change of criterion unless the change
  moves the two apart by more than the larger of the margins it connects. That identity is why every
  dominance verdict follows from $n$ scorings per cell with no pairwise statistic, and a resample
  enters only to tell a contested pair from an unresolved one.
\item \textbf{The case study and the release.} The four choices were found by measuring one
  predictor in one domain, and that case study is reported in full in Appendix~\ref{app:casestudy}:
  a coverage figure is a property of a rule set together with the engine that applies it, moving by
  up to $0.374$ with a preprocessing step neither paper reports; the output budget reorders a
  metabolite leaderboard with every ranking held fixed; and the population a dataset name refers to
  is ambiguous but reorders nothing that survives correction. We release the protocol, the audited
  split, the frozen predictions of every method compared, a checker that reports these choices from
  released files alone, and the analysis code.
\end{itemize}

Two claims follow, and they are different sizes. \textbf{C1}: most of what a leaderboard prints it
never established --- the pairs its own cell cannot separate from zero. \textbf{C2}: of the pairs it
did establish, five orderings are reversed by a declared choice and survive correction, and none of
the five is the matching rule's. The rest of the paper says which of the two a number belongs to
whenever both are in view.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{The domain, and what has been compared in it.} Rule-based metabolite prediction was
established by SyGMa \citep{Ridder_2008}, which encodes phase I and phase II biotransformations as
templates and scores products by how often each template yields an observed metabolite. GLORYx
\citep{de_Bruyn_Kops_2020} couples a comparable library to a learned model of sites of metabolism;
BioTransformer \citep{Djoumbou_Feunang_2019} extends templates to microbial and environmental
transformations; MetaTrans \citep{Litsa_2020} and MetaPredictor \citep{Zhu_2024} dispense with
templates altogether. Comparative studies recover the same coverage-precision trade-off on 85
agrochemical parents \citep{Scholz_2023}, on 37 chemicals where the broadest-coverage system also
produced the most incorrect predictions \citep{Boyce_2022}, and on radiolabelled ADME data
\citep{Gao_2026}; each fixes one criterion for a correct prediction and none reports how sensitive
its conclusion is to that choice. That one structure can be written more than one way is documented
independently of this domain: \citet{Dhaked_2020} catalogue tautomeric transformations the standard
identifier normalises only a subset of, \citet{Hahnke_2018} report roughly $60\%$ of PubChem records
differing from their canonical InChI, and \citet{Mansouri_2024} describe a standardisation pipeline.
That literature treats standardisation as preprocessing applied before an analysis, not as a
parameter of the analysis.

\looseness=-1
\paragraph{Evaluation choices in adjacent fields.} None of the four choices is in doubt in general.
On the matching rule, \citet{Maziarz_2025} reversed a published retrosynthesis ordering under a
standardised procedure, in part because one model had been scored under a relaxed stereochemistry
criterion its baselines were not given; \citet{Hastedt_2024} release the ranked predictions of
twelve such systems; \citet{liu2026massspecgymwild} find divergent metric implementations in
mass spectrometry; and outside chemistry a different equivalence rule misrepresents the state of the
art in text-to-SQL \citep{Zhong_2020} and changes which question-answering system leads
\citep{Kamalloo_2023}. On the aggregate, the metric inverts the winner in drug-response prediction
\citep{agarwal2026metric}, the standard score in structure-based drug design rises with output atom
count rather than quality \citep{Gao_2025_reframing}, which motivated rebuilding that benchmark
\citep{Lin_2025_cbgbench}, and weighting by difficulty reorders natural-language leaderboards
\citep{Mishra_2021, Rodriguez_2021}. On the output budget the literature is older: ranked output
evaluated at a declared cut-off \citep{Jarvelin_2002}, under incomplete judgements
\citep{Buckley_2004}, corrected for recording propensity \citep{Joachims_2017}, and a cut-off
metric whose winner changes with how the candidates entering it are counted \citep{Krichene_2020}.

\looseness=-1
\paragraph{Sweeping the choices rather than fixing them.} Computing a result in every cell of a
declared grid of defensible analytic choices and reporting what survives all of them is multiverse
analysis \citep{Steegen_2016} and specification-curve analysis \citep{Simonsohn_2020}; the
machine-learning counterparts account for benchmark variance \citep{Bouthillier_2021} and test a
claim jointly across datasets \citep{Dror_2017}. Those designs sweep an \emph{estimate}.
Section~\ref{sec:robust} sweeps an \emph{order}, whose intersection over cells is a partial order
rather than a distribution, so what survives has a share and a longest chain, both computable from
released prediction files. Closest in aim is work that perturbs a leaderboard and reports how far it
moves: \citet{Knowles_2021} resamples the human evaluation behind the WMT ranking, and
\citet{Jin_2026} corrects the annotations of two text-to-SQL benchmarks and reports shifts of up to
nine places. Both establish that a ranking moves. Neither returns an object a reader can quote in
its place, nor a rule that says in advance which pairs will move.

\paragraph{Staged evaluation, and what is left unestablished.} Separating what a candidate generator
reaches from what a later stage retains is standard \citep{Wang_2011_cascade, Covington_2016,
Karpukhin_2020, Maziarz_2025}, and in each case the first term is estimated from a sample or bounded
by a search budget. The demonstrations above compare studies differing in several respects at once,
so the effect of any one choice is not identified; nor is it known what the first term can be when
the candidate set is finite and enumerable, or whether a coverage figure obtained that way is
comparable across papers. The present work addresses both.

\section{What part of a published ranking is safe to quote}
\label{sec:robust}\label{sec:xdomainmain}

\subsection{The instrument}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

The survey sweeps two of the four choices of \S\ref{sec:intro}, the two a released prediction file
can be re-scored under: the matching rule, which every domain has, and whichever second choice a
field reports apart from its ranking. The other two are the hydrogen convention a template
match assumes and which molecules the test set contains. Neither can be varied without re-running
the predictors, so both are measured in the case study instead (\S\ref{sec:case}).

\looseness=-1
A leaderboard reports a total order computed in one cell of a grid its paper does not report. We fix
the items and the predictions and ask of each pair whether the ordering the table asserts holds in
every other cell. A cell is a criterion crossed with a second axis. The criterion is the rule
deciding when a prediction is the reference: in the prediction domains the four are canonical
\textsc{smiles} equality, the full \textsc{inchikey}, its stereo-blind first block, and a
tautomer-aware hash; in docking, distance to the crystal pose at two tolerances with and without a
validity screen; in translation, what an expert annotation is read as. The second axis is what the
field reports apart from its ranking and never jointly with it: a budget, a post-processing step, or
a normalisation (Appendix~\ref{app:robustorder}). The higher system \emph{dominates} the lower when
the margin is positive in every cell, and is \emph{separated in every cell} when a paired
interval excludes zero in each. Dominance is the intersection of the per-cell orders, so it is
transitive and is the finest relation no declared choice can contradict. Separation is a
sub-relation, reported apart because an interval condition is not transitive.

Formally, fix a set of systems $S$, a set of items, and a grid $C$ of cells. In cell $c$ each system
has a mean score, and those means induce a total preorder $\prec_c$ on $S$. A published table
reports $\prec_{c_0}$ for one cell $c_0$ its paper does not name. Write $\prec_{\!*} = \bigcap_{c \in
C} \prec_c$. A board ranking $n$ systems prints $n$ \emph{places}, the rank slots a reader takes away
from it. What $\prec_{\!*}$ supports is its longest chain: the number of places that survive is the
length of the longest sequence of systems each of which dominates the next.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Proposition 1.} $\prec_{\!*}$ is a strict partial order, and it is the finest relation on
$S$ that no cell of $C$ can contradict.

\emph{Proof.} An intersection of transitive, irreflexive relations is transitive and irreflexive.
Any relation not contained in some $\prec_c$ contains a pair that cell orders the other way.
\hfill$\square$
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Proposition 2.} If $C \subseteq C'$ then $\prec_{\!*}^{C'} \subseteq \prec_{\!*}^{C}$: the
dominating set is monotone non-increasing in the grid, and so is the number of tiers.

\emph{Proof.} Immediate from the definition as an intersection; the tier count is the longest chain
of a smaller relation. \hfill$\square$
\end{quote}

Proposition~2 has a consequence for reporting. A share is not a property of a leaderboard but of the
pair (leaderboard, grid), because it can only fall as a grid widens. Any paper reporting one could
therefore have reported a larger one by declaring fewer choices. The remedy is to report the curve
rather than the point: each declared axis alone, then their product
(Appendix~\ref{app:robustorder}).

\looseness=-2
All twenty-three are run on a grid of four
criteria crossed with a second axis their field reports apart and never jointly: the budget a
table is read at, whether a docked pose is minimised first, whether an error score is floored,
or whether a human score is standardised within the annotator who gave it
(Appendix~\ref{app:robust}).

\looseness=-2
The last three columns are the ones to take away, because most of what a leaderboard prints was
never resolved at all. Eleven of the seven-system table's twenty-one pairs carry margins its own
benchmark cannot separate before any choice is varied. Across all twenty-three leaderboards the
share runs from none of three to nine tenths with a median of $0.83$, so it is not a constant of the
field, and none is reported. Summed, they publish $293$ places and support $167$. Most of that gap
is a benchmark's own power rather than a convention (Appendix~\ref{app:robustorder}). The longest
chain reports the same measurement in a readable form. Seven published places support $3$ tiers,
resampling to $[3,3]$; nineteen support $9$; and the same seven systems take $4$ total orders across
$16$ cells. The system that table places first is not in the top tier of what survives; the one it
places second is the only system that is (Figure~\ref{fig:hasse}). Docking and translation are
controls. Docking's field published its reversal already \citep{Buttenschoen_2024} and the grid
recovers it. Translation's nineteen boards contest $71$ pairs, of which $11$ survive their own
boards' correction and $4$ the union of all twenty-three. Not one of the $11$ reverses an ordering
its own published cell had separated from zero; all five such reversals are the budget's, never the
matching criterion's (Appendix~\ref{app:robustorder}). A null of that shape needs a size, and the
identity supplies one without a further statistic. On the two retrosynthesis boards and the
metabolite one, the matching rule erases at most $0.5755$ of a margin the published cell had
separated, and the median comparison erases none of it. Docking is the exception and is reported as
one. Translation supplies the first claim at scale, on pairs a table never established, and
chemistry supplies the second. What does not survive on the docking board includes its own top pair.
Because dominance is an intersection, all of this can only shrink as cells are added, so each axis
is also reported alone (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}).

\looseness=-1
\paragraph{Three verdicts, not two.} A pair that does not dominate has failed in one of two ways
that should not be added together. In the first, a cell puts the two systems the other way
round with a paired interval excluding zero, so a choice decides against the published ordering. In
the second, no cell does so, and the published ordering merely fails to hold everywhere on a margin
the benchmark cannot resolve. The first is called \emph{contested} and the second
\emph{unresolved}. Only the count of contested pairs is evidence that a choice reverses anything,
because counting sign changes alone would let noise on a tied pair read as a reversal, the error
this instrument exists to avoid.
\subsection{A control with a published answer}

\paragraph{Docking as a control with a published answer.} Three of the four boards are
audited here for the first time, so the instrument's verdicts there are unvouched for: a rule that
reports little surviving everywhere cannot be told apart from a rule that reports little because it
is insensitive. Docking supplies the missing arm, because its own field published a reversal of
this kind before we ran anything. \citet{Buttenschoen_2024} argued that a pose can sit within the
field's distance tolerance of the crystal ligand and still be chemically impossible, named
\textsc{uni-mol} as the program most affected, and released the per-pose table behind that argument.
That table, read through the dominance order without reference to the argument, contests exactly two
of the $21$ pairs, and both are \textsc{uni-mol} against programs it outranks on distance alone.
That is the source's own finding, recovered by an instrument that was not told it.

The verdict is read out of $21$ pairs by $8$ cells, so it is granted inside a family of $168$
cell-level tests, and correcting for them takes it back. Neither reversal survives Holm at
$\alpha=0.05$ over the family. The board scores each item a hit or a miss, so the exact conditional
test on the discordant items is available, and these $p$ are that test; the smallest reversal
reaches $1.5\times10^{-3}$ against a threshold of $9.8\times10^{-4}$. Two exchanges out of $168$
looks is about what noise alone would produce. This control therefore establishes that the grid
recovers the source's finding as a contested verdict, and that it certifies neither half of it.

The control does not show that this board is clean. Of its $21$ pairs, $17$ dominate, $2$ are
contested and $2$ the grid leaves unresolved. One of those two is the board's own top pair,
\textsc{vina} against \textsc{gold}, whose margin no cell of the grid separates from zero. A
benchmark can be large, dense and carefully scored and still not entitle a reader to its first place
over its second. The control is that a well-run board loses four pairs where a mature
retrosynthesis table loses thirteen, so the instrument distinguishes them; it is not that any board
survives whole.

No pair is contested between the search-based programs and the learned ones. On these $428$ items
all ten such pairs dominate, because \textsc{vina} and \textsc{gold} lead on distance alone before
validity is asked for. The exchange the source is best known for lives on the other set it
distributes, and the grid finds it there. Run unchanged on the $85$ astex items, the same eight
cells contest \textsc{vina} against \textsc{diffdock} along with two other pairs, and leave $15$ of
$21$ dominating. We report that run as a second board and not as two more cells, because a
population is not a choice about how a prediction is scored. We report it at all because which
reversal a grid recovers depends on which items a benchmark chose, which is the sharpest form of
the paper's own argument that we have found.

One axis of this board is weaker than a criterion and is labelled so. Minimising a pose before
scoring it is a step the benchmark applies rather than the program, so it is a choice the evaluation
could have been made under. Unlike a matching rule, however, it does not leave the prediction alone:
$99.7\%$ of the $2{,}922$ paired poses differ between the arms, by a median of $0.354$\,\AA{}, and
$6.5\%$ of per-item verdicts change at the field's tolerance. The conservative reading is therefore
the criteria alone at the published post-processing, which leaves $18$ of the $21$ pairs dominating
rather than $17$. The pair the grid loses to that axis is named in the tables below rather than
absorbed into the total.

The released table forces three conventions, which are declared rather than chosen. Its two flatness
checks are recorded only in the un-minimised arm, so a criterion using them would not exist in half
the grid; they are dropped from every cell rather than from one, which keeps each criterion the same
object in each cell, and including them changes the published cell by $0.0000$. A row with no
scorable pose counts as a miss and not as an absent observation, because dropping those rows would
score each program on the subset it managed to produce output for. The crystal ligand ships as an
eighth row; it is the reference re-scored through the same pipeline rather than a method, and it is
excluded.
\subsection{Which axis moves what, and what the gap is made of}

\paragraph{Which choice the certified reversals belong to.} Of the
twenty-one certified reversals, five reverse an ordering the published cell had separated. The other
sixteen reverse pairs the table never established, which reaches the first claim by a second route.
What matters about the five is the axis that moves them, not their domain. All five are
retrosynthesis pairs, and each reverses at top-3 and top-5 under the \emph{published} criterion, so
the budget moves them and the matching rule does not. The matching criterion therefore certifies no
reversal of a separated ordering anywhere in this paper. That is a smaller second claim than a paper
about matching rules might suggest, but it is the one the measurements support. The criterion
exchanges positions a benchmark never separated, which is the first claim. What reverses an
established ordering is a budget nobody reports jointly with the ranking it produces.

\paragraph{Decomposing the gap between published and supported ranks.} The two
numbers in the title sit beside a subtitle about undeclared choices, which invites the reading that
the choices cost the difference. They do not. Proposition~2 keeps the ways a pair can fail as
different quantities, and the same discipline applies to the count that rolls them up. There are
three readings of "supported". Each is a longest chain in a relation on the same systems; they differ
only in what an edge requires. When a leaderboard's \emph{own} cell must separate the pair, the $293$
published places support $136$, and the $157$ lost go to the benchmark's power before any convention
is varied. When every declared cell must separate it, they support $97$, so the grid costs $39$ on
top. The $167$ the title quotes is the weaker reading an intersection of orders gives, in which a
cell must agree in sign and need not separate; it is quoted because dominance is what the instrument
computes. A reader who wants the strictest number has $97$, and one who wants what the conventions
cost has $39$.

\paragraph{The share does not replicate across two editions of one task.} Nineteen of
the twenty-three boards come from one shared task, which invites the objection that they are one
dataset counted nineteen times. Two editions of that task answer it. They ran a year apart under
different protocols --- error spans and a hundred-point score in one, direct assessments with several
ratings a segment in the other --- and five language pairs appear in both. The surviving share does
not carry over. It moves by $0.114$ on average and by $0.285$ at the widest. \textsc{en-zh} stands at
$0.900$ in one edition and $0.615$ in the next, on the same language with the same organisers. So the
share is a fact about a table and its grid, not about a benchmark or a language. A paper quoting one
as a fingerprint, this one included, would repeat the error it is about
(Appendix~\ref{app:robustorder}).

\subsection{Why the verdicts need no pairwise statistic}

\paragraph{Why the verdicts need no pairwise statistic.} Write $d$ for a pair's margin in the published cell, $d'$ for its margin in another,
and $\delta_a,\delta_b$ for how far each system moves between them, so $d'=d+(\delta_a-\delta_b)$.
The sign survives iff $|\delta_a-\delta_b|\le\max(|d|,|d'|)$. This is an identity, not a finding: it
says only that a difference changes sign when the movement exceeds both margins. It is stated for
what follows from it, not as a result. Scoring the $n$ systems once in each cell fixes every one of
the $n(n-1)/2$ dominance verdicts, with no pairwise statistic anywhere; a resample enters only to
tell a contested pair from an unresolved one. On the twenty-three tables that leaves $316$ of
$1{,}922$ pairs failing to dominate, which are exactly the pairs some cell reverses, computed from
$n$ numbers per cell rather than from $1{,}922$ paired bootstraps.

The arithmetic does not say where those pairs are, and that answer is a ratio rather than a level.
The question can be asked of twenty-four leaderboards: the twenty-three plus a molecular-generation
board whose second axis the grid does not cover. Their comparisons at risk run from $0\%$ to
$15.9\%$, and the widest-gapped board is not the safest, because exposure is what happens when
entries converge faster than the conventions separating them. So exposure is readable before any
convention is varied (Appendix~\ref{app:xdomain}). Substituting a typical movement for the domain in
place of the per-system one is all a published table alone supports. It over-predicts fragility by a
third, and is reported as a failure (Appendix~\ref{app:negative}).

\subsection{The case study the choices were found in}
\label{sec:case}

\paragraph{How one choice breaks an ordering.} \looseness=-1
\citet{Hastedt_2024} released the ranked predictions of twelve retrosynthesis systems, eleven of
which parse, on three different test sets (Appendix~\ref{app:xdomainsets}). The largest group is
seven systems on their $4{,}999$ common reactions. Their predictions never move, yet they change places at
top-1: five of twenty-one pairs exchange, and disregarding stereochemistry takes the fourth-placed
system to first. An exchange describes the data. The claim is the interaction, corrected across the
largest family declared in this work: the grid the two axes admit. Of its $448$ testable cells,
$233$ have intervals excluding zero and $110$ survive Holm at $0.05$, concentrated at top-1, the
number this field leads with (Appendix~\ref{app:xdomain}).

\looseness=-1
\paragraph{How many of the margins the benchmark resolves at all.} Since the predictions are
frozen, a paired bootstrap gives an interval on each margin directly. \textbf{Eleven of the
twenty-one margins do not separate from zero, and five of the six adjacent margins in the published
order do not.} The smallest margin this benchmark separates is $0.0146$, the largest amount any
criterion moves a margin on it is $0.0154$, and the three-system group's narrowest is $0.0213$. The
criterion therefore exchanges pairs the benchmark never separated, and where a margin is separated
it erases the gap instead of reversing it.

\looseness=-1
\paragraph{One split, two populations.} A benchmark can mix populations under one name. Of the
\ntest\ substrates here, $845$ are parent drugs and $325$ are annotated products of another
substrate in the split. One question is about a compound a patient swallows and the other about what
is left of it, averaged into one number. Methods do not treat them alike. GRAIL loses $0.152$
$[0.101,0.202]$ of recall between the two while MetaPredictor loses $0.001$ $[-0.053,0.057]$, an
interaction of $+0.150$ $[+0.084,+0.216]$, and $+0.116$ $[+0.051,+0.181]$ against SyGMa. No paper
reporting on this split reports the mixture. The other population question returns a null. A
familiar name can refer to more than one released test set; which one it refers to changes an
ordering in $44$ of $380$ comparisons and $10$ of $60$ on the metabolite split, and none of the
$440$ interactions survives Holm at $0.05$, in a design whose blindest test would see $0.116$ and
$0.202$ at $80\%$ power (Appendix~\ref{app:popaxis}).
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\looseness=-2
\paragraph{What the metabolite numbers bound.} The five-method table rests on $\nshared$ shared
substrates of frozen predictions, because MetaTrans's pipeline no longer reproduces and
BioTransformer's configuration is unscripted; the other three are scored on the full $\ntest$. An
unannotated but real product counts as a false positive, so every precision and F1 figure is
pessimistic by a bounded amount the annotation graph measures (Appendix~\ref{app:closure}). Depth-2 application
lifts the ceiling by ${\sim}0.012$ at $8.5$ times the cost, so $\ceiling$ bounds this formulation and
not the task. And this system's rule labels are built with the substrate expanded while its rules
fire without it, so locating its shortfall in selection is conditioned on a mismatch of about half
the rule--substrate positives (Appendix~\ref{app:enginereach}).

\looseness=-2
The cross-domain numbers come from files produced elsewhere, the reordering there is a top-1
phenomenon, the robust order rests on twenty-three leaderboards in four domains, and two results are bounded nulls.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\looseness=-2
Four choices are settled before any model is compared and none is routinely declared. What a published ranking entitles a
reader to quote is smaller than the ranking in two ways, and they are different sizes: most of its
pairs were never resolved, and a few that were are reversed by a choice no paper reports. Two of the
four choices change a published ordering --- the budget reverses margins an interval separates from
zero, the criterion exchanges systems a mature benchmark never separated --- and which pairs either
reaches follows from an equivalence. Declaring is not the whole remedy, as the one bank of mixed
provenance here shows.

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility statement}

Every quantity reported here derives from a committed artifact and regenerates from the released
material, and the checks that establish this are released as executable scripts. One of them is
intended for prediction files produced elsewhere: given a template bank it reports which templates
depend on the hydrogen convention and whether the bank mixes conventions, and given two or more
prediction files it reports whether they were produced on the same population and how far apart
their output sizes are. It requires no reference data and no trained model, and applied to the
eleven released retrosynthesis files it recovers the three test sets in seconds.

The cost of regeneration differs by analysis. Every split-level result regenerates from the
committed artifacts alone, which comprise the frozen predictions, the matching-key tables and the
per-substrate records. The ablations that rank or re-select candidates additionally require the
trained checkpoints, which the anonymised archive contains together with the frozen predictions of
every method compared, the audits, the evaluation harness, the split construction and the analysis
code.

Three constraints bear on exact, not approximate reproduction. One result depends on a
third-party distribution that is not redistributed here: the reach of BioTransformer's template set
is computed from the $994$ SMIRKS shipped with that tool, which an installed copy supplies; its
weights and those of MetaPredictor are likewise third-party and are cited rather than shipped.
Results measured on a subsample of a split record the cap and the seed that drew it, without which
the substrate set is not recoverable, since sampling proceeds without replacement and a different
cap yields a different set of the same size, not a subset. Confidence intervals are
regenerable as well: per-substrate rows are sorted before resampling, so a repeated run reproduces
an artifact exactly.

Two standing reconciliations guard the analyses against the code they analyse. Where an analysis
path re-derives a deployed output, the two are verified equal before use, and the released
per-candidate score dump reproduces the deployed ranking on all $\ntest$ substrates. Where a
reimplemented loop stands in for the deployed one, it is gated against a figure the deployed loop
produced, at the scale at which the claim is made.

Every set treated here as external or third-party has been audited for overlap with the training and
validation substrates. The GLORYx set overlaps on $24$ of its $37$ drugs, and \S\ref{sec:rankflip}
reports GRAIL on the $13$ that do not overlap; the $\nshared$-substrate shared subset overlaps on
none of its $150$. The audit is released as a script.

\section*{How to read the appendices}

They divide in two, and the division matters for what rests on them. Appendices~\ref{app:formal}
