\begin{abstract}
Metabolite-structure prediction is reported as a single top-$k$ recall number, as if that
transparently measured a method's chemistry-generation capability. It does not, and this paper
quantifies both ways it fails. First, \textbf{the number depends on an unstated convention}:
nothing fixes when a predicted structure ``matches'' a reference, and papers variously use
canonical SMILES, InChIKey, stereo-blind InChI, or fingerprint identity. We introduce
\textbf{TAME}, a tautomer-aware matching and re-scoring protocol on a leakage-audited,
substrate-disjoint split; re-score five methods' frozen predictions under five conventions; and
show with a pre-declared primary endpoint that the convention is a \textbf{method-dependent
confounder that reorders the leaderboard}. The protocol $\times$ method interaction is
\textbf{\interA} (95\% CI \textbf{\interAci}) for one method pair, replicates on a second,
independent pair along a different protocol axis (\textbf{\interB}, \textbf{\interBci}), and
replicates again on the external GLORYx set with four methods. Second, \textbf{one number
conflates three distinct failures}: whether the rule bank \emph{covers} a transformation, whether
the model \emph{selects} the right rule, and whether it \emph{ranks} the product into a bounded
output. We make that decomposition operational --- with the bank's coverage ceiling as a
diagnostic primitive --- using \textbf{GRAIL}, a three-stage rule-based-plus-learned predictor
(\nbank-SMIRKS bank, learned rule selector, PU-trained structural filter). On \ntest\ audited test
substrates the ceiling is \textbf{\ceiling}, but the deployed pipeline realises recall@15 =
\textbf{\realised} (micro) --- 35.5\% of it --- a gap \textbf{dominated by selection loss}
(retention \textbf{\selret}), not by ranking. Stated up front: \textbf{GRAIL does not win on
recall} (\grailmacro\ macro) --- it is the instrument we measure first and hardest. The
contribution is the protocol, the decomposition, and an honestly-diagnosed interpretable
predictor, not a state-of-the-art claim.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Predicting which molecules a drug is broken down into --- its \emph{metabolites} --- decides
whether a candidate is toxic, whether it clears too fast, and what a clinical assay should look
for. The task is structure generation under a hard correctness criterion: given a substrate
molecule, return the set of product structures the body actually produces. The dominant approach
is rule-grounded: a curated bank of SMIRKS reaction templates is applied to the substrate to
enumerate chemically plausible products, and a model decides which templates to apply and how to
order the results \citep{Ridder_2008, de_Bruyn_Kops_2020, Djoumbou_Feunang_2019}.

Progress in this area is reported as a single number: top-$k$ recall against a set of reference
metabolites. We argue that this number is doing two jobs it cannot do, and we quantify both
failures.

\paragraph{Failure 1: the number depends on a convention nobody states.} Recall requires deciding
when a predicted structure ``is'' a reference structure --- and that is a choice of equivalence
relation on molecules, not a fact. Published work makes the choice differently and silently:
stereo-blind InChIKey, fingerprint identity, canonical-SMILES equality. Because methods differ in
\emph{how} their outputs deviate from references (a rule engine emits whichever tautomer its
template produces; a sequence model emits canonical strings), the choice does not shift all
methods equally. It is a \textbf{method-dependent confounder}. We show it reorders the
leaderboard, with a pre-declared endpoint and paired intervals, on two internal method pairs
(interaction \interA\ \interAci; \interB\ \interBci) and again on an external, literature-standard
set where a \emph{significant} advantage between two published tools is erased by switching
convention alone.

\paragraph{Failure 2: one number conflates three different failures.} A rule-grounded prediction
must pass three gates: the transformation must be expressible by the bank, the rule must be
selected under a finite budget, and the product must be ranked into the top $k$. Recall multiplies
these together, so a low score cannot say which gate closed --- yet the three imply completely
different work (mine more chemistry, fix selection, fix ranking). We make the decomposition
operational and argue the bank's \textbf{coverage ceiling} should be reported alongside recall for
any rule-grounded method, since it separates what the knowledge base can express from what the
model does with it.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i) \textbf{TAME}, a tautomer-aware matching protocol with a leakage-audited substrate-disjoint
split and a frozen re-scoring harness (\S\ref{sec:tame});
(ii) the \textbf{coverage $\times$ selection $\times$ ranking} decomposition with the ceiling as a
reportable primitive (\S\ref{sec:decomp});
(iii) a five-method re-evaluation under one protocol establishing that the match convention
reorders the leaderboard, replicated externally (\S\ref{sec:rankflip});
(iv) a diagnosis locating the dominant loss in \emph{selection} and identifying it as extreme
multi-label classification, where a frequency prior beats the learned selector by
$0.144$ (\S\ref{sec:xmc});
(v) \textbf{GRAIL}, the rule-based-plus-learned predictor used as the instrument, released with
every artifact needed to reproduce the numbers.

\paragraph{On not winning.} GRAIL loses to SyGMa on recall at every output budget
(\S\ref{sec:diagnosis}). We report that first, not in a footnote, because a paper about
measurement earns nothing by exempting its own method from the measurement. The contribution here
is the instrument and the diagnosis; the released negative results --- including three of our own
hypotheses that their pre-registered falsifiers destroyed (Appendix~\ref{app:negative}) --- are
part of that contribution, not an apology for it.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Careful re-evaluation reorders chemistry leaderboards.} The closest precedent on our
axis is \textbf{Syntheseus} \citep{Maziarz_2025}, which showed that the ranking of state-of-the-art
retrosynthesis models changes under careful, standardized re-evaluation --- including a case where
one model's relaxed stereochemistry criterion, not applied to its baselines, inflated its reported
accuracy. We differ in kind rather than degree: that result is a qualitative single-case debunking,
whereas we sweep the whole matching-convention family as a controlled factor and report a
\emph{pre-declared interaction with a confidence interval}, plus the decomposition that explains
which stage the reordering acts on.

\paragraph{Concurrent work on evaluation choice.} \citet{agarwal2026metric} is closest in
claim-shape --- evaluation choice inverts which model wins, with significance testing --- but on a
different axis (a scoring metric, not a structure-identity convention) and task (transcriptomic
drug response), and without a coverage/selection/ranking decomposition.
\citet{liu2026massspecgymwild} reach a compatible conclusion from the \emph{implementation} side,
finding leakage, shortcut learning and metric-implementation divergence across independent
codebases; we instead hold implementation fixed in one harness and vary the convention as a
deliberate controlled factor, so the reordering we report is a property of the convention itself
rather than of cross-codebase inconsistency.

\paragraph{Multi-method comparisons in this domain.} We are not the first to place these tools side
by side. \citet{Scholz_2023} benchmarked SyGMa, GLORY, GLORYx, BioTransformer and MetaTrans on 85
agrochemical parents and found low first-generation precision and strong rule-versus-learned
divergence; \citet{Boyce_2022} compared six tools on 37 chemicals and found SyGMa had the highest
raw coverage but was ``prone to significant overprediction''; \citet{Gao_2026} report the same
coverage/precision trade-off against human radiolabelled ADME data. All three corroborate our
findings and none standardizes the structure-match definition, audits split leakage, or asks
whether the ranking is stable under the match choice --- that triad is where we differ, in protocol
rather than in the fact of comparison.

\paragraph{Evaluation confounds outside chemistry.} That evaluation choices reorder leaderboards is
established elsewhere: \citet{Mishra_2021} show difficulty-weighting reorders NLP/ML leaderboards
so ``top models may not be best'', and \citet{Rodriguez_2021} show individual evaluation examples
carry unequal ranking information. Our contribution is the domain-specific instantiation --- in
metabolite-structure prediction the load-bearing, previously unexamined choice is how a predicted
structure is matched to its reference --- together with the measurement showing it is
method-dependent.

\paragraph{What we do not claim.} Not the first to compare metabolite-structure predictors, not the
first to standardize chemical structures for comparison (tautomer standardization is established
preprocessing, \S\ref{sec:tame}), and not the first to build a leakage-aware split
\citep{Joeres_2025}. The claim is the conjunction: a named protocol, an audited split, a frozen
multi-method re-scoring, and the quantified demonstration that the convention is a confounder.

\section{TAME: a tautomer-aware evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:tame}

TAME is a \emph{protocol, an audited split and a re-scoring harness}, not a leaderboard service:
it exists so that the numbers in \S\ref{sec:results} are apples-to-apples and regenerable from
committed artifacts.

\paragraph{The match quotient.} The field has never agreed what counts as a match. GLORYx uses a
stereo-blind InChIKey skeleton, MetaTrans uses Morgan-fingerprint Tanimoto${=}1$, LAGOM uses
canonical-SMILES equality --- each a different equivalence relation on molecules, each applied
silently. TAME makes the choice explicit by exposing all of them as named quotients: predicted and
reference structures are mapped through one of
\[
q \in \{\textsc{canonical},\ \textsc{inchikey},\ \textsc{inchi-no-stereo},\ \textsc{tanimoto1},\
\textsc{inchikey-tautomer}\}
\]
and a prediction counts as a hit iff $q(\hat{y}) = q(y)$ for some reference $y$. Our recommended
default is \textsc{inchikey-tautomer}: standard InChI normalizes only a \emph{subset} of tautomers
\citep{Dhaked_2020}, so a rule engine that emits a keto form of an enol reference fails to match
under plain \textsc{inchikey} even though it produced the same chemical entity. Tautomer-canonicalising
both sides before hashing closes that gap and is the most defensible identity criterion for a
rule-driven generator. Unless stated otherwise all results use \textsc{inchikey-tautomer};
\S\ref{sec:rankflip} quantifies how much the ranking depends on that choice. A worked example in
which the same fixed predictions score a near-total miss or a full hit purely by changing $q$ is
given in Appendix~\ref{app:worked}.

This is not an invented normalization. Tautomer standardization is established \emph{preprocessing}
--- \citet{Hahnke_2018} report that 60\% of PubChem structures differ from their canonical InChI
form, mainly by tautomer choice, and \citet{Mansouri_2024} describe a comparable
desalt/destereo/tautomer-canonicalisation pipeline. What is new here is adopting it as the
\emph{matching} rule and measuring how far it moves the leaderboard.

\paragraph{Split and leakage audit.} All splits are substrate-disjoint: no substrate in test or
validation appears in train, and no test substrate appears in validation. Clean triples are
constructed and then audited read-only by a separate script that canonicalises SMILES and verifies
zero substrate and zero positive-pair overlap across every split pair, emitting a machine-checkable
report. Leakage-aware splitting is itself not novel --- DataSAIL \citep{Joeres_2025} minimises
cross-split similarity on MoleculeNet-style tasks --- but the leak that matters here is
substrate--metabolite \emph{identity} overlap, which is domain-specific. Trustworthiness is
corroborated empirically rather than asserted: on the deployed run's own evaluation, the same
configuration scored on both splits gives recall@15 $=0.344$ on validation and $0.334$ on test, a
gap of $+0.010$ in the expected direction (validation marginally easier, since selection touched
it) and far too small to indicate test-set overfitting.

\paragraph{Metrics.} We lead with recall@$k$ co-reported with \texttt{mean\_output\_size}, not with
precision alone. Under incomplete annotation precision is a \emph{pessimistic lower bound}: an
unannotated candidate is scored a false positive even when it is a real but unrecorded metabolite.
Reporting recall without output size is equally misleading in the other direction, since a method
can buy recall by enumerating (Table~\ref{tab:matchsens}, and Appendix~\ref{app:budget}). Model,
preset and hyper-parameter selection happens exclusively on validation; the test split is touched
once, for the final reported numbers.

\section{The decomposition and the coverage ceiling}
\label{sec:decomp}

A rule-grounded predictor reaches a reference metabolite only by passing three gates in sequence:
the transformation must be \emph{expressible} by some rule in the bank, that rule must be
\emph{selected} into the substrate's candidate pool under a finite budget, and the resulting
product must be \emph{ranked} into the top $k$. Write, for a substrate set $S$ with reference sets
$Y_s$:
\[
\underbrace{\frac{|H|}{|U|}}_{\text{recall@}k}
\;=\;
\underbrace{\frac{|C_{\text{full}}|}{|U|}}_{\text{coverage}}
\;\cdot\;
\underbrace{\frac{|C_{\text{bud}}|}{|C_{\text{full}}|}}_{\text{selection}}
\;\cdot\;
\underbrace{\frac{|H|}{|C_{\text{bud}}|}}_{\text{ranking}},
\qquad
U=\textstyle\bigcup_s Y_s ,
\]
where $C_{\text{full}}$ are references recovered by applying the \emph{entire} bank without a
budget, $C_{\text{bud}}$ those surviving the selector's budgeted pool, and $H$ those actually
returned in the top $k$. The identity is exact by construction (each set contains the next), so
the three factors partition the loss with no residual: it is an accounting device, not a model.
Its value is diagnostic --- a single recall number cannot say \emph{which} gate closed, and the
three interventions those gates imply (mine more chemistry, fix selection, fix ranking) are
entirely different pieces of work. Estimator details, including why we report the micro
(ratio-of-sums) form, are in Appendix~\ref{app:formal}.

\paragraph{The ceiling as a primitive.} The first factor is measurable without any model: apply
all \nbank\ rules to every test substrate and ask what fraction of references appears anywhere in
the union. That is the bank's \textbf{coverage ceiling} --- the best recall achievable if selection
and ranking were both perfect --- and on the clean test split it is \textbf{\ceiling}
(cluster-bootstrap 95\% CI $[0.709,0.762]$, resampling substrates). We argue this should be
reported alongside recall for any rule-grounded method, because it separates \emph{what the
knowledge base can express} from \emph{what the model does with it}, and because it bounds every
downstream claim: no ranking improvement can exceed it.

\paragraph{What the decomposition says.} Table~\ref{tab:decomp} localises the loss. The deployed
pipeline converts only $35.5\%$ of its own ceiling into realised recall, and the largest single
leak is \textbf{selection}: less than half of what the bank can reach survives into the budgeted
candidate pool. Ranking is the mildest of the three. This is a claim about \emph{our} instrument,
and it is the reason the paper spends \S\ref{sec:xmc} on selection rather than on a better
re-ranker --- an intervention chosen by the decomposition rather than by intuition. It also sets
the honest ceiling on what this architecture can become: even a perfect selector and a perfect
ranker would stop at \ceiling, and Appendix~\ref{app:props} shows that ceiling is
corpus-limited rather than a tuning artifact.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The match convention reorders the leaderboard}
\label{sec:rankflip}

Five methods --- GRAIL, SyGMa \citep{Ridder_2008}, BioTransformer
\citep{Djoumbou_Feunang_2019}, MetaPredictor \citep{Zhu_2024} and MetaTrans \citep{Litsa_2020} ---
have their \emph{frozen} predictions re-scored by one harness under five matching conventions, so
only the match rule changes across columns, never the chemistry (Table~\ref{tab:matchsens}). Two
observations follow immediately. Methods are not equally sensitive to the convention: GRAIL moves
by $0.009$ from canonical to tautomer matching while BioTransformer moves by $0.129$. And
\texttt{mean\_output\_size} exposes a second, orthogonal confound --- SyGMa emits $74$ candidates
per substrate against $8.7$--$12.7$ for the others --- so recall alone is not budget-fair either
(Appendix~\ref{app:budget}).

\paragraph{Primary endpoint.} We pre-declared the \emph{differential} sensitivity as the endpoint,
not any individual reversal: the protocol $\times$ method interaction, i.e.\ how much more one
method gains from normalized matching than another, with a paired bootstrap over substrates
($10{,}000$ resamples; substrates are the independent unit). It is significant on two independent
pairs along two different protocol axes: BioTransformer gains \interA\ \interAci\ more than GRAIL
from canonical$\rightarrow$tautomer, and SyGMa gains \interB\ \interBci\ more than MetaTrans from
canonical$\rightarrow$InChIKey. The convention is therefore a \textbf{method-dependent
confounder}, not a neutral scoring choice.

\paragraph{Two reversals follow, neither individually significant.} Under canonical matching GRAIL
leads BioTransformer ($\Delta=+0.041$); under tautomer matching BioTransformer leads
($\Delta=-0.079$). MetaTrans leads SyGMa under canonical, no-stereo and Tanimoto$=$1 matching
($\Delta=+0.010$) but trails under strict InChIKey ($\Delta=-0.053$), driven by a
\emph{non-monotone} response --- MetaTrans is the only method whose recall \emph{falls} under
normalization, because it emits isomeric SMILES that strict InChIKey penalises. \textbf{Honest
note:} every pairwise $\Delta$ here has a CI spanning zero at $n=\nshared$ (GRAIL$\leftrightarrow$
BioTransformer canonical $[-0.044,+0.122]$, tautomer $[-0.166,+0.007]$;
MetaTrans$\leftrightarrow$SyGMa canonical $[-0.064,+0.081]$, InChIKey $[-0.130,+0.023]$). We
certify the interaction that drives the reversals, not the reversals themselves.

\paragraph{External replication.} The results above live on a $\nshared$-substrate subset of our
own split, so we repeated the analysis on the field's de-facto external hold-out: the \nglory\
GLORYx drugs \citep{de_Bruyn_Kops_2020} with four methods, switching only
InChIKey~$\rightarrow$~tautomer-InChIKey (Table~\ref{tab:gloryx}). Protocol sensitivity spans more
than an order of magnitude across methods --- SyGMa $+0.007$, GRAIL $+0.178$ --- and three of the
four intervals exclude zero. The interaction is significant for \textbf{four of six} method pairs,
the largest being GRAIL$\leftrightarrow$SyGMa ($-0.171$ $[-0.264,-0.086]$) and
MetaPredictor$\leftrightarrow$SyGMa ($-0.135$ $[-0.209,-0.072]$). The ordering consequence is
sharpest for the latter: under strict InChIKey SyGMa leads by a \emph{significant} $-0.130$
$[-0.196,-0.065]$; under tautomer matching that advantage \textbf{disappears entirely} and the
point estimate reverses ($+0.005$, n.s.). A convention chosen for convenience can therefore erase
a statistically significant advantage between two published tools --- now shown on an external,
literature-standard set, with a different method quartet, at a sample size where the intervals
still exclude zero.

\paragraph{Mechanism.} The spread is not arbitrary: a method's protocol sensitivity tracks how far
its outputs diverge tautomerically from the references it is scored against. A rule engine that
emits whichever tautomer its SMIRKS produce (GRAIL, MetaPredictor) gains most from tautomer-aware
matching; a method that canonicalises its own output (SyGMa) gains almost nothing. This predicts
where the effect should be \emph{small}, and we test that prediction out of domain in
Appendix~\ref{app:xdomain}.

\subsection{The honest anchor: our own instrument, measured first}
\label{sec:diagnosis}

A benchmark whose authors also ship a method invites the obvious suspicion, so we state the
comparison before any of our own results. On the full clean test split GRAIL reaches
\textbf{\grailmacro} macro recall@15 (3 seeds: $0.344 \pm 0.010$) against SyGMa's \textbf{0.572} ---
a paired difference of $-0.242$ (95\% CI $[-0.271,\,-0.212]$, cluster bootstrap over $n=1{,}168$
common substrates) --- and MetaPredictor's $0.585$ on the shared subset ($0.568$ re-scored on the
full test). \textbf{GRAIL does not win on recall, at any budget.} A budget-matched frontier
sweeping $k \in [1,64]$ finds SyGMa ahead at \emph{every} $k$ with no crossover
(Appendix~\ref{app:budget}): the gap is not an artifact of output-size accounting, it is a genuine
selection weakness, and \S\ref{sec:decomp} localises it.

We report this deliberately and early. The instrument used to build a measurement protocol should
be the first thing the protocol is turned on, and it should be reported even when --- especially
when --- it loses. Where GRAIL is competitive is a different axis: it returns $8.7$ candidates at
precision $0.109$ where SyGMa returns $74$ at $0.029$, an operating point that matters for
interactive use but that recall@$k$ alone does not express.

\subsection{Learning loses to counting: selection is extreme multi-label}
\label{sec:xmc}

Since selection is the dominant leak, we ask what makes it hard, and the answer is structural
rather than metabolism-specific. Choosing which of \nbank\ rules apply to a substrate is an
\textbf{extreme multi-label classification} (XMC) problem with a near-empty positive set:
approximately one to three rules fire per substrate, i.e.\ ${\sim}0.03\%$ of labels. In exactly
this regime XMC has long established that frequency/propensity baselines are hard to beat, which
is why propensity-scored losses were introduced \citep{Jain_2016}. Metabolite rule selection
\emph{inherits} this pathology, which the domain literature does not name.

The prediction is testable, and it holds against us: on the same candidate pools a learned-only
selector reaches $0.266$ while ranking by the empirical rule-firing frequency alone reaches
$0.410$ --- a paired difference of $\mathbf{-0.144}$ (95\% CI $[-0.196,\,-0.095]$, $n=245$). The
learned selector loses to counting. Under PU annotation with an approximately constant labelling
propensity, a learner with constant unlabelled weighting recovers a propensity-distorted score
whose dominant component is the marginal firing rate, so the prior is Bayes-competitive
\emph{by construction} at the selection stage; the learner can only win if substrate-conditional
prevalence variation exceeds estimation noise, and the observed data-scaling saturation indicates
it does not at this scale.

Two qualifications keep this from being read as ``learning does not work here''. First, the effect
is \emph{selection-specific}: at the separate \emph{ranking} stage, on identical broad pools, the
learned filter beats the same frequency prior ($0.413$ versus $0.374$). Second, the pathology is
addressable rather than fundamental --- a dense reformulation over a coarse reaction-type
vocabulary beats the prior roughly threefold at $k{=}5$, though at a cost in reachability that
makes it a diagnosis rather than a drop-in fix (Appendix~\ref{app:props}).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Sample sizes differ across comparisons.} The five-method table rests on $\nshared$
shared substrates, because two comparators could only be run on that subset; GRAIL and SyGMa are
scored on the full $\ntest$. The external replication is $n=\nglory$. At those sizes the
\emph{interactions} are significant but no individual pairwise reversal is, and we report them that
way throughout rather than promoting point estimates.

\paragraph{Two comparators are not re-runnable in-tree.} MetaTrans's inference pipeline no longer
reproduces in our environment and BioTransformer's exact configuration is unscripted, so both are
represented by frozen prediction files rather than by re-executable code. Their numbers are
therefore reproducible from our artifacts but not independently regenerable from scratch --- a
weaker guarantee, and one we prefer to state than to obscure.

\paragraph{Precision is a lower bound, not an estimate.} Reference metabolite sets are
\emph{incomplete}: an unannotated but chemically real product is scored a false positive. Every
precision figure here should be read as pessimistic, which is why we lead with recall and output
size and never rank methods by precision.

\paragraph{The single-step bound.} Our ceiling is single-step-conditional. A non-vanishing fraction
of references are multi-generation products unreachable by any one rule application; depth-2
application lifts the ceiling by only ${\sim}0.012$ at ${\sim}8.5\times$ candidate cost. The
$\ceiling$ ceiling bounds this paradigm, not the problem.

\paragraph{Evaluation hygiene of this paper itself.} Our headline numbers come from a single
evaluation of the deployed configuration, with model and hyper-parameter selection done on
validation. Some \emph{exploratory} analyses reported in Appendix~\ref{app:negative}, however, were
scored on the test split before we tightened this discipline; they inform no headline claim and
their conclusions are negative, but a paper arguing for evaluation rigor should disclose its own
lapses rather than only its protocol. Subsequent exploratory work was moved to validation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A single top-$k$ recall number in metabolite-structure prediction hides two distinct things: an
unstated choice of when two molecules count as the same, and the composition of three separate
failure modes. We gave the first a protocol and a measurement --- the convention is a
method-dependent confounder that reorders the leaderboard, on internal pairs and on an external
literature-standard set --- and the second a decomposition with the bank's coverage ceiling as a
reportable primitive, which localises our own instrument's dominant loss in selection and
identifies that loss as extreme multi-label classification, where counting still beats learning.
GRAIL does not win on recall, and we report that first. What we offer is a way to say precisely
\emph{why} any rule-grounded method scores what it scores, and a protocol under which that score
means the same thing across papers.

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility statement}

All numbers in this paper are regenerable from committed artifacts: the frozen predictions of every
method, the leakage-audit report, and one JSON per experiment holding the values quoted in the
text and tables. The evaluation harness, the matching quotients, the split construction and audit,
and every analysis script named in the text are released together with the model checkpoints
required to reproduce the GRAIL rows. An anonymized artifact archive accompanies this submission;
the de-anonymized repository will be linked in the camera-ready version.
