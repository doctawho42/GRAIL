\begin{abstract}
Computational prediction of drug metabolites is assessed almost universally by top-$k$ recall, the
fraction of known metabolites a method recovers. A single number cannot say why a method scores
what it does, because a metabolite is missed for three unrelated reasons: no rule in the method's
knowledge base expresses the transformation, the applicable rule is not selected, or the correct
product is generated but ranked too low to report. Improving the rule base, the selector and the
ranker are different research programmes, and recall does not indicate which is warranted.

Three parameters sit between a set of predictions and a leaderboard position, and none is declared:
the rule for when two structures are the same molecule, the aggregate reported, and the output
budget. Varying each with predictions held fixed moves the ordering. The matching rule erases a
statistically significant advantage between two published tools on the external set; reporting F1
rather than recall drops the recall leader to last; and the budget, imported from a convention that
never charged for output size, reorders them again.

The third is not arbitrary, and an exact decomposition of recall says why. For methods with an
enumerable rule base, recall factorises into coverage, selection and ranking, each measurable rather
than estimated. Applied to two such methods it explains the leaderboard: the wider bank reaches
$0.735$ of the annotated chemistry against $0.542$ and converts a third of that reach, while the
narrower converts nearly all of its own, having no selection stage to lose anything at. The gap is the price of selecting at all, which
recall charges only to methods that select. That is a claim about benchmark design rather than
about chemistry, and it predicts something checkable: an aggregate crediting hits without charging
for volume must favour architectures that do not filter.

So an aggregate crediting hits without charging for volume cannot be neutral between architectures,
which the decomposition predicts from a structural property rather than observing after the fact.
The reading survives its own counterfactual: removing the selection stage entirely and matching the
output budget, the wider bank passes its competitor by $0.166$ $[0.110,0.222]$ --- but only above
the budget the field uses, which is the third parameter acting again, this time in our favour. We
release the protocol, the audited split and the frozen predictions that make every measurement here
repeatable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

When a drug enters the body it is chemically transformed. Enzymes, principally in the liver,
oxidise, cleave and conjugate the administered compound into a set of new molecules called
metabolites. Which metabolites form is a central question in drug development, because a
metabolite may be inactive, may carry the therapeutic effect, or may be toxic in ways the parent
compound is not. Regulatory review attends closely to major human metabolites, and identifying them
experimentally is slow and expensive. Computational prediction of metabolite
structures therefore has direct practical value: given the structure of an administered compound,
predict the structures of the molecules the body will produce from it.

Two families of methods address this problem. Rule-based systems encode known biotransformations
as reaction templates, apply every applicable template to the input compound, and rank the
resulting products by an empirical score \citep{Ridder_2008, de_Bruyn_Kops_2020,
Djoumbou_Feunang_2019}. Machine-learning systems instead learn the transformation directly, either
as a sequence-to-sequence translation between molecular strings or as a classifier over reaction
types \citep{Litsa_2020, Zhu_2024}. Hybrid systems combine the two, using a learned model to
decide which of many templates to apply. Whatever the underlying approach, published methods are
compared on the same quantity: top-$k$ recall, the fraction of experimentally observed metabolites
that appear among a method's $k$ highest-ranked predictions.

That comparison hides what it is measuring. A rule-based or hybrid method can fail to recover a
metabolite for three unrelated reasons: no template expresses the transformation, the template
exists but is not selected, or the product is generated and ranked too low to report. Recall
confounds these, so a low score identifies no deficiency and suggests no remedy.

This work separates them. For a method whose rule base can be enumerated, recall factorises
exactly into coverage, selection and ranking, and the coverage term is obtained by applying the
entire bank and asking what it reaches. Applied to two such methods it explains a leaderboard instead of restating one. The method
with the wider rule base reaches more chemistry and converts a third of that reach; the one with
the narrower base converts nearly all of its own, because it applies every applicable rule and so
has no selection stage in which to lose anything. It wins by half a leaderboard position for that
reason alone. The observation generalises past this pair and past chemistry: an aggregate counting hits without
charging for the volume emitted is not neutral between architectures, since recall charges the cost
of a selection stage only to architectures that have one. That is a question about benchmark
design, and \S\ref{sec:metric} answers it by changing the aggregate and watching the leaderboard
move as the factorisation predicts.

Making the factorisation well-posed exposes a second problem, which is why the paper addresses it
first. Every factor counts references matched against predictions, so all three move with the rule
deciding when a predicted structure is the same molecule as a reported one, and chemistry offers
several defensible rules: canonical SMILES, hashed identifiers, identifiers with stereochemistry
removed, or fingerprints. They disagree most about tautomers, alternative structures of one
compound differing in the position of a mobile hydrogen; Appendix~\ref{app:worked} works one pair
of predictions through all of them, scoring a near-total miss or a full hit with the chemistry
fixed. Evaluations here adopt one each, usually
without saying why, and none has varied it. Varying it over five conventions with predictions held
fixed erases a significant advantage between two published tools externally, and internally moves
the F1 ordering while leaving recall's stable to within noise. Two more follow: the aggregate, where reporting
F1 rather than recall makes the leader the straggler, and the output budget. That matching rules and
metric choices can reorder leaderboards is established in other fields; that this field's rankings
turn on two undeclared ones is not.

What generalises past this benchmark is the instrument. Recall factorises exactly wherever the
candidate set can be enumerated, and the coverage term is then measured rather than estimated, so it
is reportable for any such method. What it supports is a claim about benchmark design: an aggregate
crediting hits without charging for volume cannot be neutral between architectures that filter and
architectures that do not. We release the bundle that makes it checkable rather than a
model: the audited split, the harness with all five criteria, every method's frozen predictions and
GRAIL's checkpoints. Adding a sixth method needs its ranked output and nothing else.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Metabolite-structure prediction.} Rule-based prediction of metabolite structures was
established by SyGMa \citep{Ridder_2008}, which encodes phase I (oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis) and phase II
(conjugation) biotransformations as reaction templates and scores the resulting products by the empirical frequency with which each
template yields an observed metabolite. GLORYx \citep{de_Bruyn_Kops_2020} couples a comparable
template library to a machine-learned model of sites of metabolism, the atoms at which an enzyme acts, and BioTransformer
\citep{Djoumbou_Feunang_2019} extends the template approach to microbial and environmental
transformations. Learned alternatives dispense with templates: MetaTrans \citep{Litsa_2020} treats
metabolite prediction as sequence-to-sequence translation between molecular strings, and
MetaPredictor \citep{Zhu_2024} combines a transformer with a filtering stage. These systems are
evaluated on overlapping but non-identical datasets and, as discussed below, under non-identical
notions of a correct prediction.

\paragraph{Comparative evaluations in this domain.} Several studies have placed these tools side by
side. \citet{Scholz_2023} benchmarked five predictors on 85 agrochemical parent compounds and
reported low first-generation precision together with substantial divergence between rule-based
and learned systems. \citet{Boyce_2022} compared six tools on 37 chemicals and found that the
system with the broadest coverage also produced the largest number of incorrect predictions.
\citet{Gao_2026} evaluated predictors against human radiolabelled ADME data and saw the same
coverage-precision trade-off. Each fixes a single criterion for judging a prediction correct, and
none reports how sensitive its conclusions are to it.

\paragraph{Structure standardisation.} The difficulty of deciding when two molecular structures
denote the same compound is documented independently of metabolite prediction.
\citet{Dhaked_2020} catalogue tautomeric transformations and show that the standard InChI
identifier normalises only a subset of them. \citet{Hahnke_2018} report that roughly 60\% of
PubChem records differ from their canonical InChI representation, predominantly through tautomer
choice, and \citet{Mansouri_2024} describe a standardisation pipeline developed for
quantitative structure-activity modelling. This literature treats standardisation as a
preprocessing step applied before analysis, rather than as a parameter of the evaluation itself.

\paragraph{Evaluation methodology in adjacent fields.} That methodological choices alter the
apparent ordering of methods is recognised more broadly, along two axes this paper also varies.
On the matching rule, \citet{Maziarz_2025} re-evaluated retrosynthesis models under a standardised
procedure and reversed a published ordering, partly because one model had been scored under a
relaxed stereochemistry criterion its baselines did not get; it is the closest precedent here, and
the only one that varies a structure-matching rule. \citet{liu2026massspecgymwild} audit published
mass-spectrometry work and name the mechanism directly, divergent metric implementations and
stereochemistry handling in InChIKey matching among them. Outside chemistry, re-scoring frozen
text-to-SQL submissions under denotation equivalence rather than exact match shows exact match
misrepresenting the state of the art \citep{Zhong_2020}, and re-scoring frozen question-answering
outputs under four answer-equivalence rules makes one system best under a rule it had lost under
\citep{Kamalloo_2023}. On the aggregate, \citet{agarwal2026metric} show for drug-response
prediction that the choice of scoring metric inverts which model wins. The closest precedent to
what we find is \citet{Gao_2025_reframing}. There, structure-based drug design's standard score
rises with a structural property of the output, its atom count, rather than with quality, so
re-evaluating under practically aligned metrics changes which models look good. The same field's
benchmark was rebuilt around four complementary axes for the same reason
\citep{Lin_2025_cbgbench}. Weighting examples by difficulty reorders natural-language leaderboards
\citep{Mishra_2021, Rodriguez_2021}. Data
splitting has received similar attention \citep{Joeres_2025}.

\paragraph{Remaining gap.} Neither axis is in doubt in general, and neither has been varied for
metabolite predictors. What is unestablished is how far each moves this field's rankings when it
is the only thing changing; and no stage-wise attribution of a predictor's score has been
reported for metabolite predictors, though the device is established elsewhere, \citet{Maziarz_2025}
separating single-step accuracy from search in retrosynthesis. That is what would let the two be
explained rather than only reported.

\section{TAME: an evaluation protocol with a declared matching criterion}
\label{sec:tame}

TAME is a \emph{protocol, an audited split and a re-scoring harness}, not a leaderboard service:
it exists so that the numbers in \S\ref{sec:results} are directly comparable and regenerable from
committed artifacts.

\paragraph{The matching criterion.} The field has never agreed what counts as a match. GLORYx uses a stereo-blind InChIKey skeleton,
MetaTrans compares Morgan fingerprints, and rule-based systems that canonicalise their own output,
SyGMa among them, effectively compare canonical SMILES. Each is a different equivalence relation,
and each is applied silently. TAME exposes all of them as named quotients: predicted and reference
structures are mapped through one of
\[
q \in \{\textsc{canonical},\ \textsc{inchikey},\ \textsc{inchi-no-stereo},\ \textsc{tanimoto1},\
\textsc{inchikey-tautomer}\}
\]
and a prediction counts as a hit iff $q(\hat{y}) = q(y)$ for some reference $y$. Each map is
computed with RDKit 2022.09.5, the version that reproduces the columns reported here.
\textsc{canonical} is canonical SMILES equality and \textsc{inchikey} the full InChIKey.
\textsc{inchi-no-stereo} is its first block, the connectivity skeleton, blind to stereochemistry,
charge and isotopes. \textsc{tanimoto1} is equality of Morgan fingerprints at radius 2 over 2048
bits, that is Tanimoto similarity of one. \textsc{inchikey-tautomer} standardises both sides before
hashing, taking the fragment parent,
neutralising charges, canonicalising tautomers, and writing SMILES without stereochemistry.

Our recommendation is conditional on the reference set rather than universal: report all five, and
choose the default by whether the references carry stereochemistry and tautomeric variety. For this
corpus that is \textsc{inchikey-tautomer}. Standard InChI normalises only a
\emph{subset} of tautomers \citep{Dhaked_2020}, so a rule engine emitting a keto form of an enol
reference fails to match under plain \textsc{inchikey} despite producing the same entity. Two
properties of this default bear on every number below: it is the most permissive of the five, and
it is stereo-blind as well as tautomer-blind, so a predicted D-alanine matches an L-alanine
reference. The stereo relaxation is not incidental. Several methods here, GRAIL among them, standardise their
output without stereochemistry and cannot emit a stereocentre, so a stereo-sensitive criterion would
score them on a representation they never produce. That is also why the choice suits our own system,
and why it is stated as conditional rather than defended.

Unless stated otherwise all results use \textsc{inchikey-tautomer};
\S\ref{sec:rankflip} quantifies how much the ranking depends on that choice. A worked example in
which the same fixed predictions score a near-total miss or a full hit purely by changing $q$ is
given in Appendix~\ref{app:worked}.

Standardisation of this kind is established preprocessing (\S\ref{sec:related}); what is new
here is adopting it as the \emph{matching} rule and measuring how far it moves the leaderboard.

\paragraph{Split and leakage audit.} All splits are substrate-disjoint: no substrate in test or
validation appears in train, and no test substrate appears in validation. Clean triples are
constructed and then audited read-only by a separate script that canonicalises SMILES and verifies
zero substrate and zero positive-pair overlap across every split pair, emitting a machine-checkable
report. Leakage-aware splitting is itself not novel, since DataSAIL \citep{Joeres_2025} minimises cross-split similarity on
MoleculeNet-style tasks. The leak that matters here is
substrate--metabolite \emph{identity} overlap, which is domain-specific. As a secondary check, the deployed configuration
gives macro recall@15 $=0.344$ on validation against $0.334$ on test (293 and 291 substrates). The
$+0.010$ gap matches the seed-to-seed standard deviation, so it corroborates the zero-overlap audit
rather than standing as independent evidence.

\paragraph{Metrics.} We lead with recall@$k$ co-reported with the mean output size, not with
precision alone: under incomplete annotation precision is a \emph{pessimistic lower bound}, since an
unannotated candidate scores as a false positive even when it is a real but unrecorded metabolite.
Recall without output size misleads in the other direction, a method being able to buy recall by
enumerating (Appendix~\ref{app:budget}). Two comparisons are confirmatory and carry the multiplicity correction, the differential
sensitivity of \S\ref{sec:rankflip} and the learned-versus-prior gap of \S\ref{sec:xmc}; every
other interval here is exploratory. Selection happens on validation, with two exceptions we
name rather than imply. The deployed
filter's encoder variant was chosen by comparing both variants on test
(Appendix~\ref{app:arch}); that choice fixes which encoder is deployed and so reaches every GRAIL
number here, the headline included. The exploratory analyses of Appendix~\ref{app:negative} were also scored on test, before this
discipline was tightened, and support no claim. Everything else we report on test --- the
decomposition, the learned-versus-prior comparison, the breadth sweep --- is measurement, not
selection.

\section{The decomposition and the coverage ceiling}
\label{sec:decomp}

A rule-grounded predictor reaches a reference metabolite only by passing three gates in sequence:
the transformation must be
\emph{expressible} by some rule in GRAIL's bank of \nbank\ reaction rules, that rule must be
\emph{selected} into the substrate's candidate pool under a finite budget, and the resulting
product must be \emph{ranked} into the top $k$. Write, for a substrate set $S$ with reference sets
$Y_s$:
\[
\underbrace{\frac{|H|}{|U|}}_{\text{recall@}k}
\;=\;
\underbrace{\frac{|C_{\text{full}}|}{|U|}}_{\text{coverage}}
\;\cdot\;
\underbrace{\frac{|C_{\text{bud}}|}{|C_{\text{full}}|}}_{\text{selection}}
\;\cdot\;
\underbrace{\frac{|H|}{|C_{\text{bud}}|}}_{\text{ranking}},
\qquad
|U|=\textstyle\sum_{s\in S}|Y_s| ,
\]
where $C_{\text{full}}$ are references recovered by applying the \emph{entire} bank without a
budget, $C_{\text{bud}}$ those surviving the selector's budgeted pool, and $H$ those actually
returned in the top $k$. The first factor requires an enumerable candidate set: the bank for rule-grounded methods, a wide
decode for a sequence-to-sequence one, where the same three gates apply --- MetaPredictor factorises
$0.612\times0.804\times1.000$, exactly its realised recall, though a beam is a budget rather than a
knowledge base and bounds that decode rather than the model (Appendix~\ref{app:seq2seq}). The identity is exact by construction (each set contains the next), so
the three factors partition the loss with no residual: it is an accounting device, not a model.
Its value is diagnostic: a single recall number cannot indicate \emph{which} gate closed, while
the three corresponding interventions address unrelated components of the pipeline. Two
aggregations are in use and every figure is labelled with which. Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} reports
\emph{micro}, the pooled ratio of sums written above, because only that form makes the factors
multiply out exactly; the head-to-head comparisons report \emph{macro}, the mean of per-substrate
recalls, which is the comparator tools' convention. The two differ here, $0.261$ against $0.330$
(Appendix~\ref{app:formal}).

\paragraph{The ceiling as a primitive.} The first factor is measurable without any model: apply
all \nbank\ rules to every test substrate and ask what fraction of references appears anywhere in
the union. That is the bank's \textbf{coverage ceiling}, the best recall achievable if selection
and ranking were both perfect. On the clean test split it is \ceiling\ (cluster-bootstrap 95\% CI $[0.709,0.762]$). It should be
reported alongside recall for any rule-grounded method: it separates what the knowledge base can
express from what the model does with it, and no ranking improvement can exceed it.

\paragraph{What the decomposition says.} Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} localises the loss. The deployed
pipeline converts only $35.5\%$ of its own ceiling into realised recall, the three factors separate
with no pair of intervals overlapping, and selection is where the loss sits at this operating point:
barely a third of what the bank can reach survives the selector's rule budget, while ranking is
close to lossless at $0.963$ $[0.946,0.978]$. It binds jointly with the cut-off rather than alone.
Widening the selector moves the loss downstream --- what selection recovers, the cut-off gives back
--- so the honest statement is that the deployed \emph{pair} caps the pipeline near $0.41$ against a
bank ceiling of \ceiling\ (Appendix~\ref{app:props}).

Removing selection entirely tests that reading rather than restating it. Applying the bank with no
selector and no threshold, ranking the whole pool and truncating at SyGMa's output, the bank passes
SyGMa by $0.166$ $[0.110,0.222]$ at $k=82$ --- and is behind at $k=8$, indistinguishable at the
field's $k=15$. The bank converts, and it converts only above the budget the convention sets, which
is \S\ref{sec:metric}'s third parameter acting for us where it acts against us later
(Appendix~\ref{app:noselect}).

Applied to a second method it stops being self-diagnosis. SyGMa is rule-grounded with an
enumerable bank, so it decomposes on the same split in the same frame. Its profile is the inverse of
ours. Its bank reaches less, $0.542$ against $\ceiling$ ($-0.193$ $[-0.213,-0.175]$ paired, signed
comparator minus GRAIL throughout), but it applies every applicable rule and emits the result,
converting almost all of that reach where we convert a third of a wider one. The gap between us is therefore not a worse rule base but the cost of having a selection
stage at all, which recall charges only to methods that select. Two methods lose recall for opposite reasons, and a single score says only that one scores lower.
The frame also bounds this architecture: a perfect selector and ranker would stop at \ceiling, and
that ceiling is corpus-limited rather than a consequence of tuning, since 53\% of uncovered
transformations are of reaction types absent from the bank. It is carried by the curated fifth --- $1{,}715$ curated rules reach $0.660$ where $5{,}866$ mined
ones reach $0.328$ --- and the tail is still not disposable: discarding the $4{,}271$ rules that
never carry a positive label costs recall@15 $-0.036$ $[-0.050,-0.024]$
(Appendices~\ref{app:props} and~\ref{app:negative}).

\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The criterion the decomposition rests on}
\label{sec:rankflip}

settled before the decomposition means anything. Published methods have their \emph{frozen}
predictions re-scored by the TAME harness under all five criteria, so only the match rule changes
across columns, never the chemistry. Three of them
--- GRAIL, SyGMa \citep{Ridder_2008} and MetaPredictor \citep{Zhu_2024} --- have predictions for
the whole clean test split and are compared there, paired on identical substrates
(Table~\ref{tab:matchsens}). Two more, BioTransformer \citep{Djoumbou_Feunang_2019} and MetaTrans
\citep{Litsa_2020}, have internal predictions only on a 150-substrate shared subset
(Appendix~\ref{app:xdomain}); BioTransformer also appears on the external set below.

\paragraph{Primary endpoint.} The endpoint is the \emph{differential} sensitivity, not any individual
reversal: the protocol $\times$ method interaction, with a paired bootstrap over substrates
($10{,}000$ resamples). Every method gains under a more tolerant criterion, so only the differential
can reorder anything. The design was fixed before the data, the five criteria and the re-scoring of frozen predictions
specified twelve days before the first result; the estimator was not. We correct for multiplicity: under Holm--Bonferroni at
$\alpha=0.05$ every interaction called significant here survives on both sets, internally even when
the family is taken conservatively as all ten pairs the five-method table admits. On the $\nshared$-substrate subset two independent
pairs are significant along two protocol axes, BioTransformer gaining \interA\ \interAci\ more than
GRAIL from canonical$\rightarrow$tautomer and SyGMa \interB\ \interBci\ more than MetaTrans from
canonical$\rightarrow$InChIKey. The convention is a \textbf{method-dependent confounder}, not a
neutral scoring choice. Two pairs do exchange places between criteria on this subset, though every
pairwise $\Delta$ spans zero at $n=\nshared$ (Appendix~\ref{app:xdomain}): the interaction is
established, the reversals illustrate it.

\paragraph{External replication.} The results above live on a $\nshared$-substrate subset of our own
split, so we repeated the analysis on the field's de-facto external hold-out, the \nglory\ GLORYx
drugs \citep{de_Bruyn_Kops_2020}, whose own tool is not scored here for want of runnable weights. The set is external for three of the four methods and
not for ours, which we checked late: $19$ of the $37$ drugs are in GRAIL's training split and $5$ in
validation, so GRAIL is reported on the $13$ unseen; the others do not train on this corpus.

The result this section rests on involves neither contaminated arm. Under strict InChIKey
SyGMa leads MetaPredictor by a \emph{significant} $-0.130$ $[-0.196,-0.065]$; one rung of tolerance
later the advantage is gone and the estimate reverses ($+0.005$, n.s.): a criterion chosen for
convenience can erase a significant advantage between two published tools. On the uncontaminated
drugs GRAIL's stereo sensitivity survives at $+0.126$ $[+0.028,+0.239]$, as does its interaction
with SyGMa --- the same measurement, SyGMa's stereo step here being zero. The \emph{external}
interaction with BioTransformer does not, and we withdraw it, leaving the subset estimate untouched.
This set's coverage ceiling stays on all $\nglory$, applying the bank being model-free
(Appendix~\ref{app:xdomain}).



\paragraph{The criterion moves human agreement, and that agreement bounds what recall means.} The
strongest evidence for declaring the criterion involves no model. Twenty-five external drugs are
independently curated by our corpus too, so the two metabolite lists compare as a prediction does to
a reference: the two expert curations of one drug agree at a Jaccard of $0.075$ under canonical
matching and $0.406$ under the tautomer-aware default, on the same drugs either way. Most of what
looks like disagreement about observed chemistry is disagreement about notation.

MetaPredictor's uncapped $0.562$ is a tie inside noise bought with $10.6$ metabolites per drug
against the curation's $6.0$; at the curation's own budget it recovers $0.301$, behind by $0.238$
$[0.123,0.354]$ (Appendix~\ref{app:xdomain}).

\paragraph{Mechanism.} The spread tracks how far a method's outputs diverge
\emph{representationally} from the references, and the intermediate column says which divergence is
responsible: here stereochemistry alone, every separating gain realised by the stereo step and the
tautomer step zero for all but one method. GLORYx references carry stereocentres and several of
these systems cannot emit one, so a stereo-sensitive criterion scores them on a representation they
never produce. Internally, where references are already stereo-stripped, tautomerism fails instead,
so which relaxation dominates is a property of the reference set. In retrosynthesis, whose
references are canonical and stereochemically explicit, the five span about $0.02$
(Appendix~\ref{app:xdomain}).

\subsection{The metric is a second free parameter, and the decomposition predicts it}
\label{sec:metric}

Recall is not the only aggregate this field reports; F1 and Jaccard are standard, and a method
emitting eighty candidates to catch a few more references is rewarded by the first and punished by
the others. Computed on each method's deployed output set over the full split --- untruncated, since precision
and Jaccard are set quantities --- the orderings disagree, and the disagreement is certified in both
directions. SyGMa leads on recall, by $0.026$ over MetaPredictor with a paired 95\% interval of
$[0.003,0.049]$, and finishes last on F1, $0.115$ below it, $[0.108,0.121]$. GRAIL, last on recall,
is $0.089$ above SyGMa on F1, $[0.078,0.100]$, and $0.079$ above it on precision,
$[0.069,0.089]$.

\looseness=-1
The output budget is the third parameter, and it reorders without any budget having to be chosen:
swept over $k$, SyGMa is above GRAIL for every $k$ from $3$ to $12$ and below from $13$ on, where the
field's $15$ sits, leading by $0.054$ $[0.039,0.068]$ at $k=5$ and by $0.019$ to $0.035$ elsewhere in
the region. The
mechanism generalises: a budget binds a method only when it emits more, so at $k=15$ SyGMa is cut on
$93.1\%$ of substrates and GRAIL on none, and the convention advantages whoever emits least --- GRAIL included, and truncation being free, only
at $k\le2$ does its compact output buy a real lead (Appendix~\ref{app:props}).

This is not confined to the two methods the decomposition runs on. Across all five, four cluster on
F1 with every pairwise difference covering zero while SyGMa sits below all four by $0.094$ to
$0.127$; on recall the same subset puts it \emph{above} two, by $0.109$ and $0.197$ over
BioTransformer and GRAIL, both certified. Two pairs therefore reverse with certification on both sides, and both set the one method with no
selection stage against methods emitting a seventh as many candidates. We order comparators here by
emitted output size, not by the selection factor, which is measured only for the two methods the
decomposition runs on. SyGMa alone emits at that scale, so this exhibits a mechanism rather than
estimating a dependence (Appendix~\ref{app:xdomain}).

The decomposition says why, and this is where the paper's two halves meet. SyGMa's selection factor
is $1$ because it emits every product its applicable rules generate. That is what recall rewards and
precision punishes, so a pipeline with no selection stage is scored relatively higher by the first
than by the second. It need not lead on recall outright, coverage being a free axis: SyGMa's bank
reaches $0.542$ against GRAIL's $\ceiling$, the paired gap of \S\ref{sec:decomp}. The factorisation supplies the direction of the
movement, from a structural property rather than after the fact.

The matching criterion acts on these metrics too, and where it barely touched recall. Over the full
split the recall@15 ordering is stable to within noise, the leading pair exchanging places by at
most $0.0014$ with a differential sensitivity of $-0.002$ $[-0.009,+0.006]$. The F1 gap between
GRAIL and MetaPredictor is another matter: it is certified under four criteria, at $-0.026$ $[-0.038,-0.013]$ under the
tautomer-aware default, and indistinguishable from zero under plain \textsc{inchikey}, at
$+0.003$ $[-0.009,+0.015]$. That is an erased gap rather than a certified reversal, and we claim it
as no more than that. Three undeclared parameters therefore sit between a set of predictions and a
leaderboard position, and they do not act on the same comparisons.

Precision here is a lower bound, which threatens this result most since the method the metric
demotes emits the most candidates. Under a uniform-propensity model recall is unbiased, precision's
ordering is identified, and F1's survives to $c\approx0.055$ (Appendix~\ref{app:props}).





\subsection{Comparison against published methods}
\label{sec:diagnosis}

On the full clean test split GRAIL trails both published comparators, SyGMa at $0.572$ (paired
$-0.242$ $[-0.271,\,-0.212]$ over the $1{,}168$ common substrates) and MetaPredictor at $0.568$, and
leads at no output budget (Appendix~\ref{app:budget}). It emits $8.4$ candidates against SyGMa's
$81.7$, which \S\ref{sec:metric} prices against us. Recall figures here differ by population,
aggregation and truncation and several coincide in value without being the same quantity;
Appendix~\ref{app:xdomain} states each.



\subsection{Selection as extreme multi-label classification}
\label{sec:xmc}

Since selection is the dominant shortfall we ask what makes it hard, and the answer is structural
rather than metabolism-specific. Choosing which of \nbank\ rules apply is \textbf{extreme
multi-label classification} with a near-empty positive set: one to three rules per substrate carry a
positive label, some ${\sim}0.03\%$ of the label space, while far more are applicable, so what is
scarce is annotation rather than applicability. In that regime frequency and propensity baselines
are known to be hard to beat \citep{Jain_2016}, and rule selection inherits that pathology unnamed.

It holds here. Each arm selects thirty rules through the same enumeration and filter: a learned
selector reaches $0.266$ where rule-firing frequency alone reaches $0.410$, a paired $-0.144$
$[-0.196,\,-0.095]$ at $n=245$. The effect is selection-specific, since at the ranking stage the
learned filter beats the same prior on the same substrates, $0.413$ against $0.374$, a paired
$+0.039$ $[+0.007,+0.072]$. The remedy the framing prescribes
fails too: inverse-propensity weighting of positives moves the selector to $0.249$, treating as an
annotation artifact a rarity that is here substantially chemistry (Appendix~\ref{app:props}).

\subsection{What constrains each factor}
\label{sec:evidence}

The diagnosis rests on nine measurements (Table~\ref{tab:levers}), each designed to move exactly
one factor, so a result failing to move recall is as informative as one that does.


Six of the nine failed, and are the more useful half. Depth-2 buys $0.012$ of ceiling for $8.5$
times the candidates, doubling the data changes nothing, and the stratification meant to show a rule
bank transfers better off distribution finds an untrained rule engine degrading just as fast, so we
attribute the shortfall to the structure of the selection problem rather than to under-training. Abstention is sharpest: the threshold removes true and
apparently false candidates alike, so precision stays flat while recall collapses. Of the rest, two locate recoverable recall at selection and the third types
what the bank cannot reach in one step (Appendix~\ref{app:props}).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Sample sizes differ.} The five-method table rests on $\nshared$ shared substrates,
because two comparators could only be run there; GRAIL, SyGMa and MetaPredictor are scored on the
full $\ntest$, and the external replication is $n=\nglory$.

\paragraph{Two comparators cannot be re-run in this codebase.} MetaTrans's inference pipeline no
longer reproduces here and BioTransformer's configuration is unscripted, so both are represented by
frozen predictions: reproducible from our artifacts, not regenerable from scratch.

\paragraph{Precision is a lower bound, not an estimate.} An unannotated but chemically real product
scores as a false positive, so every precision and F1 figure is pessimistic. We rank on them in
\S\ref{sec:metric} only under a uniform-propensity model, which is assumed rather than estimated.

\paragraph{The single-step bound.} Some references are multi-generation products no single rule
application reaches, and depth-2 lifts the ceiling by ${\sim}0.012$ at $8.5$ times the candidate
cost, so $\ceiling$ bounds this formulation rather than the task.

\paragraph{Evaluation hygiene of this paper itself.} Headline numbers come from a single evaluation
of the deployed configuration, and selection is on validation but for the two exceptions
\S\ref{sec:tame} names: the filter's encoder variant, chosen on test, which reaches every GRAIL
number here including the headline, and the exploratory analyses of Appendix~\ref{app:negative},
which inform no claim.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\looseness=-1
Three parameters decide a leaderboard position before any model does: the matching rule, which moves
how far two \emph{curators} of one drug agree before it moves any model; the aggregate, under which
the recall leader finishes last, a pipeline that selects nothing being what recall rewards and
precision punishes; and the budget, which binds only methods emitting past it. All three belong in
any report.

\looseness=-1
For a rule-grounded predictor recall also factorises exactly into bank, selector and ranker, which
explains an ordering rather than repeating it and predicts the aggregate's effect from a structural
property. Removing the selector and matching the budget confirms that reading above the field's $k$
and not below. Report the ceiling wherever a rule base exists.

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility statement}

All numbers are regenerable, with one exception flagged where it appears: a reranker figure whose
per-substrate scores were not retained. What regeneration costs differs by analysis, and the
distinction is worth stating rather than leaving a reader to discover. Every split-level result
regenerates from the committed artifacts alone --- frozen predictions, key tables, per-substrate
records --- while the ablations that rank or re-select candidates require running a trained model,
so they also need the GRAIL checkpoints. The anonymized archive holds those, together with
every method's frozen predictions, the audits, the harness, the split construction and the analysis
code. One result depends on a third-party file we do not redistribute: the reach of
BioTransformer's template set is computed from the $994$ SMIRKS in the reaction databases its own
distribution ships, \texttt{metabolicReactions.json}, its \textsc{envmicro} counterpart and
\texttt{standardizationReactions.json}, which a reader reproduces by pointing the script at an
installed copy. Its own weights and MetaPredictor's are third-party too and are cited rather than
shipped. Results measured on a subsample of a split carry the cap and seed that drew it, without which
the substrate set is not recoverable: sampling draws without replacement, so a different cap yields
a different set of the same size rather than a subset. Where an analysis path re-derives a deployed
output, the two are reconciled before use rather than assumed equal: the released per-candidate
score dump reproduces the deployed ranking on all $\ntest$ substrates, a check that caught three
silent divergences in the path that produces it.

Every set this paper treats as external or third-party has been audited for overlap with GRAIL's
training and validation substrates, not only the one where a problem was found. The GLORYx set
overlaps on $24$ of its $37$ drugs, $64.9\%$, and \S\ref{sec:rankflip} reports GRAIL on the $13$
that do not; the $\nshared$-substrate shared subset overlaps on $0$ of $150$. The audit is a
released script rather than a claim, and it is the check whose absence produced the GLORYx error:
it is implied by none of the others reported here and had never been run.
