\section{Introduction}

A drug that reaches the bloodstream does not stay the compound that was administered. Enzymes,
chiefly in the liver, convert it into a series of other compounds, and those compounds carry
their own activity, their own toxicity and their own persistence. A metabolite can be the agent
that makes a drug work, as with the antiviral prodrugs activated by phosphorylation; it can also
be the agent that makes one fail, as with the reactive intermediates behind several
withdrawals. The same question arises outside the clinic: what an environmental chemical becomes
in an organism determines how far it travels and how long it lasts.

Knowing which metabolites will form, as chemical structures rather than as a count or a rate, is
therefore useful early, when a compound exists only on paper and no experiment has been run.
Predicting them computationally has been attempted for three decades, and two families of method
now dominate. Rule-based systems, among them SyGMa \citep{Ridder_2008},
BioTransformer \citep{Djoumbou_Feunang_2019}, GLORY and
GLORYx \citep{de_Bruyn_Kops_2019, de_Bruyn_Kops_2020} and MetaTox \citep{Rudik_2017}, encode
known biotransformations as reaction templates and enumerate the products of applying them to a
query structure. Sequence-to-sequence systems, among them MetaTrans \citep{Litsa_2020} and
MetaPredictor \citep{Zhu_2024}, treat the problem as translation, mapping a text representation
of the substrate to text representations of its metabolites. Recent work extends both lines: a
chemical language model \citep{Larsson_2025}, a mechanistically informed graph
framework \citep{Zhou_2025}, a server predicting pathway, site and product
together \citep{Zhou_2026}, and a preprint predicting all three jointly \citep{Chavan_2026}.

What is missing is not another predictor but a way to tell these apart. The usual measure is
recall: the fraction of the metabolites recorded for a compound that a system returns. That
fraction depends on two decisions made outside the model. The first is what counts as a match,
since two chemical structures can denote the same molecule while differing in how a hydrogen is
placed or how a ring is drawn. The second is how many candidates the system is allowed to return,
since any system recovers more of the answer if permitted to list more guesses. Published
predictors differ on both and report them inconsistently, so a system returning seventy candidates
and one returning eight are not measured on the same scale. Independent evaluations of these tools
report the same obstacle \citep{Boyce_2022, Scholz_2023, Gao_2026}.

This work addresses that. We present GRAIL, a metabolite structure predictor built from a bank of
reaction templates, a learned model that selects which templates to apply, and a learned model
that ranks the resulting candidates. Every prediction it emits carries the template that produced
it and the atoms that template acted on, so a chemist can accept or reject a candidate on
mechanistic grounds rather than on a score. We evaluate it with the two decisions above promoted
to declared axes and varied across their full range, against every published predictor for which
predictions on the same substrates could be obtained. The comparison does not produce a winner:
which system leads depends on how many candidates it is allowed to return, and we report that
dependence rather than a single figure. We further measure how much of the recorded chemistry
lies outside the reach of any single template application, and find that most of it is chemistry
the training corpus does not contain, which no enlargement of the bank would recover.

The article is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the corpus, the three
stages of the system and the evaluation protocol. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the
comparison against published predictors, the contribution of each stage, the sensitivity of the
result to the evaluation choices, a worked example, and the coverage bound.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses where the advantage lies and what the measurements do not
establish, Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} states the conclusions and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} the limitations. Procedures, ablations and supporting measurements are in the Supporting Information.

\section{Materials and methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Which population carries which number}
\label{sec:populations}

The corpus divides into three substrate-disjoint splits: \numSplitTrainSubstrates{} training
substrates carrying \numSplitTrainPairs{} annotated pairs, \numSplitValSubstrates{} validation
substrates carrying \numSplitValPairs{}, and \numSplitTestSubstrates{} test substrates carrying
\numSplitTestPairs{}. Disjointness by substrate is the discipline a molecular dataset requires if
a held-out figure is to mean anything \citep{Joeres_2025, Kretschmer_2025}, and a read-only audit
verifies zero substrate and zero positive-pair overlap across all three split pairs.

Three evaluation populations appear here, each a stated subset of a split rather than a split
itself, and no figure is given without naming one. The \emph{evaluated test set} is the
\numPopulationTestsubs{} test substrates that carry at least one reference, holding all
\numPopulationTestrefs{} references of that split; it is where the coverage ceiling and the type
census are measured. The \emph{validation draw} is a declared sample of \numValdrawCap{} substrates at seed
\numValdrawSeed{} from the validation split. Of these, \numValdrawDeclared{} produced a candidate
pool, and the \numValdrawPaired{} held by both arms of a paired comparison carry
\numHOneFiveNreferences{} references. Every registered ranking and timing threshold is checked
here, since a threshold checked where its rule was chosen is not a check. Sampling is without replacement and the draws are not nested, so a different seed gives a
different set of the same size and a different cap one that is not a subset of this one; cap and
seed are recorded with every figure drawn from it. The
\emph{comparison set} is the \numPopulationN{} substrates on which every method predicts,
carrying \numPopulationReferences{} references.

The annotated substrate--metabolite pairs are assembled from four sources: ChEMBL
\citep{Zdrazil_2024}, DrugBank \citep{Wishart_2018}, MetXBioDB \citep{Djoumbou_Feunang_2019} and
the GLORYx reference set \citep{de_Bruyn_Kops_2020}. The versions used are \CorpusVersions{}. Their licences do not
combine into one redistributable derivative, which the Supporting Information sets out.

The comparison set carries no training contamination: \numPopulationInTrainOrVal{} of its
\numPopulationN{} substrates appear in train or validation under a canonical key.

No external evaluation set is reported, and the reason is the assembly rather than an audit
finding. GLORYx is one of the four sources, so its \numGloryxN{} parent substrates are not
external to this corpus at all, and the \numGloryxOverlap{} of them that fall in train or
validation are there by construction. Holding out a source would not fix this either, because the
sources overlap one another.

That has a consequence for the comparison which no split can remove. Rule-based comparators have
no training set to hold out, and their expert rules may encode the same metabolism reported in
these sources; BioTransformer is built on MetXBioDB, and GLORYx was developed against the
reference set of the same name. Their knowledge therefore overlaps our test substrates by
construction, in a direction that flatters them, and we report it rather than control it.

\subsection{Overview}

GRAIL predicts in three stages, each separately inspectable. A generator scores every rule in a
bank of \numBankRules{} SMIRKS templates against the substrate. The selected rules are then
applied with RDKit 2022.09.5, and their products are
standardised \citep{Hahnke_2018, Mansouri_2024} and deduplicated. A filter scores each
(substrate, candidate) pair, and the candidates are ordered by a rank fusion of the filter's and
the generator's orderings. Every emitted candidate carries the identity of the rule that produced
it and the site at which it fired.

A candidate reachable by several rules, or by one rule firing at several sites, receives a single
score. Writing $R(c)$ for the rules that produce $c$ and $p_r$ for the generator's probability
that rule $r$ applies, the aggregation is noisy-or,
\begin{equation}
p(c) = 1 - \prod_{r \in R(c)} \left( 1 - p_r \right),
\label{eq:noisyor}
\end{equation}
so evidence from independent rules accumulates rather than competing. Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor}
is why deduplication is not a cosmetic step: the key under which two products are judged the same
decides which scores are combined, and changing that key moves the ranking. Tautomers are the
hard case, and their treatment is not settled by the standard identifiers \citep{Dhaked_2020}.

\subsection{Rule bank}

The bank holds \numBankRules{} templates spanning \numBankTypes{} distinct reaction types, where a
type is the multiset of bonds that change between mapped atoms. It is the deduplicated union of \numAttribNamed{} templates
from three curated collections and \numMinedRules{} mined from the training split alone,
together with \numAttribUnattributed{} that were carried as a fourth curated collection and are
not one: they are an earlier machine extraction from the same annotated pairs, identified in the
Supporting Information by three marks that agree. The bank therefore holds two extractions and
one curated body, and the curated body is \numAttribNamed{} templates. The split is disjoint by substrate rather
than by molecule, so a molecule that is a substrate in training may appear as a product in test;
the split counts are in Section~\ref{sec:populations} and the molecular overlap between splits is
in the Supporting Information. For each annotated substrate--product pair a ring-aware maximum common substructure is
located, the atoms and bonds that changed are flagged as the reaction centre, the centre is
expanded by one bond and emitted as a mapped SMIRKS. A template survives only if applying it to
its own source substrate regenerates the annotated product after canonicalisation.

Support is heavily skewed and mining is saturated: re-running the miner on the same split
re-derives every template it derived before and none new.

\subsection{Rank fusion}

Both learned stages produce an opinion about each candidate. The obvious way to combine two such
scores is their product, and a product is scale-sensitive: if one scorer occupies a narrow range
and the other a wide one, the wide one determines the order.

The deployed combination is reciprocal rank fusion \citep{cormack2009},
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RRF}(c) = \sum_{i} \frac{1}{K + r_i(c)}, \qquad K = 60,
\end{equation}
where $r_i(c)$ is the rank of $c$ under scorer $i$. Only positions enter, so the two opinions
contribute equally whatever their scales.

The change was registered as prediction P1, with a threshold of $+\numHSevenThreshold$ fixed
before it was checked, and evaluated on a validation draw where the fusion had not been selected:
$+\numHSevenDiff$ of recall, 95\% CI $[+\numHSevenLo, +\numHSevenHi]$. On the pool the deployed
system ranks, which is capped, the same contrast is $+\numHSevenDeployed$
(Table~\ref{SI-tab:si-ranking}): the cap and the fusion recover overlapping ground, so applying
both gains less than the sum of the two. The Supporting Information gives the two figures on both
populations with their intervals, and the cost of having selected the fusion rule on one of
them.

\subsection{Pool cap}

Before fusion the pool is truncated by generator score, a quantity available before any pair graph
is built:
\begin{equation}
\hat{C}(s) = \operatorname*{arg\,top}_{c \in C(s)}{}^{\,\numHNineCap} \; p(c),
\end{equation}
where $C(s)$ is everything the applied rules enumerate. Registered as prediction P2 with a threshold of
$+\numHNineThreshold$; measured $+\numHNineDiff$, 95\% CI $[+\numHNineLo, +\numHNineHi]$, mean
pool \numHNinePoolBefore{} to \numHNinePoolAfter{}. The cap was registered to gain and it gains:
it removes a few candidates that were a substrate's only correct answer and many more that were
occupying the middle of the list. The value was fixed by a rule rather than by search, the
smallest round cap at twice the largest reported budget, so that the widest column is not a
tautology.

\subsection{Rule budget and two operating modes}

The trained checkpoint records a rule budget of \numHOneZeroTopk{}, and the pools this paper
reports elsewhere apply the whole bank, so the two differ by a factor of \numBankRules{} to
\numHOneZeroTopk{} in how much of the bank they consult. What the difference buys was registered
as prediction P3 with a ceiling of $+\numHOneZeroThreshold$: on validation
$+\numHOneZeroBought$, 95\% CI $[\numHOneZeroLo, +\numHOneZeroHi]$, mean pool on that draw
\numHOneZeroPoolBank{} to \numHOneZeroPoolTrained{} candidates before deduplication.

\subsection{Emission}

Under the product combination the system emitted candidates scoring within $\alpha = 0.5$ of the
leader. That rule does not transfer to a rank fusion, and the reason is structural: the worst
candidate scores $0.88$ of the best under fusion against $0.016$ under the product, because a
reciprocal-rank sum is a rank statistic without scale. At $\alpha = 0.5$ the rule admits
$16.22$ candidates from a pool of $16.3$ on the validation draw, truncating nothing. Three
counts of the interactive arm's output appear in this paper and mean different things: the pool
before deduplication (\numHOneZeroPoolTrained{} on the validation draw), the pool this emission
grid reads ($16.3$ on the same draw), and the list finally emitted on the comparison set
(\numOutputTrained{}).

The deployed emission is therefore $\hat{C}(s)$ itself, ordered by fusion and truncated by no
threshold, so $|\hat{Y}_s| = |\hat{C}(s)|$. For the interactive mode that length is set by the
rule budget and the substrate's chemistry, and its mean of \numOutputTrained{} sits well below
any cap. The exhaustive mode differs: its pool is capped at 100 by the cap prediction P2 registers, and
its mean output of \numOutputBank{} is close enough to that cap that the cap sets its length on
most substrates. That cap is a parameter of this system, it is declared, and it applies to that
arm and not to the comparators. Registered as prediction P4 with a criterion of zero lost cells
over a grid of five matching criteria by eleven budgets: \numHOneOneLost{} of
\numHOneOneCells{}.

\subsection{Evaluation}

The rule deciding when a predicted structure counts as one of the references is the
\emph{matching criterion}. It is treated here as a declared axis with five settings: canonical
SMILES equality, the full InChIKey, its stereochemistry-blind first block, Tanimoto similarity of
one over Morgan fingerprints, and a tautomer-aware key that canonicalises tautomers on both
sides. The last is the
default, because the references neither carry stereochemistry nor distinguish tautomers, and
because standard identifiers normalise only a subset of tautomers \citep{Dhaked_2020}. All five
are reported for every comparison.

A third choice sits behind both: how the substrate itself is drawn. Templates are matched
against the structure the corpus stores, and that corpus draws amides as imidic acids, so the
choice is not neutral. Rebuilding both GRAIL arms with every substrate redrawn by the
standardiser moves \numDsweepMoved{} of \numDsweepCells{} arm-against-comparator verdicts, none
of them a lead claimed here, and the coverage ceiling on that population by
$\numDsweepCeilingdiff$ with the interval covering zero.

The number of candidates a system is permitted to return is the \emph{output budget}, written
$k$, and it is the second declared axis. Recall at budget $k$ for substrate $s$ with references
$Y_s$ and the first $k$ predictions $\hat{Y}^{(k)}_s$ is $|Y_s \cap \hat{Y}^{(k)}_s| / |Y_s|$. Two aggregations are distinguished
throughout and never mixed within a comparison,
\begin{align}
\mathrm{macro} &= \frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{s \in S}
                   \frac{|Y_s \cap \hat{Y}^{(k)}_s|}{|Y_s|}, \\
\mathrm{micro} &= \frac{\sum_{s} |Y_s \cap \hat{Y}^{(k)}_s|}{\sum_{s} |Y_s|},
\end{align}
and every figure states which it is. Intervals are a paired bootstrap over substrates: for each of
$B = 10\,000$ resamples $S^{(b)}$ drawn with replacement,
\begin{equation}
\delta^{(b)} = \frac{\sum_{s \in S^{(b)}} \left( h^A_s - h^B_s \right)}
                     {\sum_{s \in S^{(b)}} |Y_s|},
\label{eq:bootstrap}
\end{equation}
with $h^A_s$ and $h^B_s$ the hits of the two arms, and the reported interval is the empirical
$2.5$ and $97.5$ percentiles of $\{\delta^{(b)}\}$. Resampling substrates rather than
predictions is what makes the interval one on the \emph{difference} between two arms read on the
same items. Precision is reported in Table~\ref{SI-tab:si-precision} but is not used to order systems: under incomplete
annotation an unannotated but real metabolite counts as a false positive, so a system emitting
fewer candidates is rewarded for reasons unrelated to its chemistry. It does order them
differently, which is the point of printing it.

The parent-drop convention of Table~\ref{tab:sweep} is swept like the other declared choices.
The arms differ sharply in how often they return the substrate, from none of SyGMa's lists to
\numPdropReturnsBank{} of the exhaustive mode's \numPopulationN{}, so the convention had room to
favour one over another. It does not: the largest effect on recall anywhere in the grid is
\numPdropMaxEffect{} and no cell separates from zero (Table~\ref{SI-tab:si-parentdrop}).

Every interval in this paper is a per-comparison 95\% interval with no family-wise adjustment.
The sweep is \numCritBudgets{} budgets wide and Table~\ref{SI-tab:si-criterion} is \numCritCriteria{} criteria deep, so
treating the grid as a single family would require a corresponding discount; the narrowest lead
claimed here, at $k=30$ against MetaTox, is the cell that would not survive such a correction.

No single budget is privileged. A claim that the system leads is made for a cell only where that
cell's interval excludes zero, and where an ordering is not established the table says so.
Reporting the whole space of defensible analysis choices rather than one chosen
cell is the multiverse and specification-curve discipline \citep{Steegen_2016, Simonsohn_2020},
applied here to the two choices this literature leaves undeclared.

\subsection{What each stage contributes to the order}

The two learned scorers are combined, and neither has been reported alone. Re-ranking one pool
five ways, with the pool, the matching rule and the budget fixed, separates the contribution of
each from the contribution of the form in which they are combined. No model runs: the pools carry
both component scores for every candidate.

At $k=15$ on the comparison set the fusion reaches \numRankCmpFusionOneFive{}, the generator's
score alone \numRankCmpGeneratorOneFive{}, the filter's alone \numRankCmpFilterOneFive{}, their
product \numRankCmpProductOneFive{} and a seeded permutation \numRankCmpRandomOneFive{}. The
fusion separates from every one of them: $+\numRankCmpVsgenerator$
$[+\numRankCmpVsgeneratorlo, +\numRankCmpVsgeneratorhi]$ over the generator alone,
$+\numRankCmpVsfilter$ $[+\numRankCmpVsfilterlo, +\numRankCmpVsfilterhi]$ over the filter alone,
and $+\numRankCmpVsproduct$ $[+\numRankCmpVsproductlo, +\numRankCmpVsproducthi]$ over their
product. The validation draw ranks the arms in the same order by point estimate, with one
difference noted below.

Two qualifications belong with that. Which single scorer is better does not transfer: on the
comparison set the generator's ordering beats the filter's, on validation the two do not separate.
And the fusion's advantage over the product is a property of depth rather than of the head: at
$k=1$ the difference is \numRankCmpVsproductkOne{} and the interval covers zero on both
populations. Neither stage is sufficient alone, and the gain this paper attributes to the form of
the combination is not a property of one population alone.

\subsection{What the learned rule choice contributes}

The rule-budget prediction compared \numSelBudget{} templates against the whole bank, which asks
about size. What the choice buys is a different question, and it is answered by picking
\numSelBudget{} rules three other ways with the application, the standardisation, the filter and
the fusion identical downstream. About \numSelApplicable{} of the \numBankRules{} templates match
a given substrate, so the choice is made within a large applicable set rather than over the bank.

Against \numSelBudget{} rules drawn at random from the whole bank, the learned choice gains
$+\numSelVsrandom$ at $k=15$: most randomly chosen templates do not fire, and that arm
returns a mean pool of \numSelRandomPool{} candidates. Against \numSelBudget{} drawn at random
from the applicable set the gain is $+\numSelVsrandomApplicable$
$[+\numSelVsrandomApplicablelo, +\numSelVsrandomApplicablehi]$, and that arm returns
\numSelRandomApplicablePool{} candidates against the learned arm's \numSelLearnedPool{}: choosing
badly among applicable rules produces four times the candidates and recovers far less.

The comparison that decides is against the \numSelBudget{} applicable rules with the highest
training frequency, which uses the same applicability filter and no learning. There the learned
choice gains $+\numSelVspriorApplicable$ of micro recall at $k=15$,
$[+\numSelVspriorApplicablelo, +\numSelVspriorApplicablehi]$, on a base of
\numSelPriorApplicableOneFive{}. The interval excludes zero from $k=15$ but not below it: at
$k=5$ the gain is $+\numSelVspriorKFive$ and the interval covers zero. The substrate-specific part
of stage one is therefore worth a small and budget-dependent amount over a frequency table
restricted to the same rules, and we report it as such rather than as the stage's justification.

\subsection{A ranking that needs no training}

Metabolism usually leaves the scaffold recognisable. Many metabolites differ from their parent
by an added oxygen, a cleaved bond or a conjugate, so they stay close to what they came from, and
closeness is a fingerprint away. The premise is not universal. A cleavage that removes most of
the molecule, or a conjugate that doubles its heavy-atom count, moves a metabolite a long way from
its parent. It holds often enough, though, that similarity to the substrate is a real baseline. Any learned ranker over (substrate, candidate) pairs competes against that
baseline whether or not the baseline is reported, and this literature does not report it.

Re-ranking one pool four ways holds the pool, the matching rule and the budget fixed so that only
the order varies. Against Tanimoto similarity to the substrate, which needs no training and no
labels, the deployed fusion gains $+\numSimCmpGapOneFive$ of micro recall at $k=15$ on the
comparison set, 95\% CI $[+\numSimCmpLoOneFive, +\numSimCmpHiOneFive]$, and
$+\numSimValGapOneFive$ on the validation draw, $[+\numSimValLoOneFive, +\numSimValHiOneFive]$.
The interval excludes zero at every budget on both populations. A seeded permutation of the same
pool reaches \numSimCmpRandomOneFive{} against the fusion's \numSimCmpFusionOneFive{}, so the
pool alone is not what produces the result.

Asking the same question of the product ordering this paper replaced gives a different answer,
$+\numSimOldGap$ with the interval covering zero, and the reason is a property of that experiment
rather than of the two rankings. It ran on a pool whose median size is \numSimOldMedianPool{}
candidates, where \numSimOldPoolUnderBudget{} of \numSimOldN{} substrates hold no more
candidates than the budget admits. A ranking comparison run at a budget wider than the pool has
nothing to order and cannot separate anything.

\subsection{How much of the verdict is the criterion}

The argument above is that a recall figure depends on evaluation choices this literature leaves
unstated. It is an argument about published numbers, and it applies to ours. Recomputing the whole
comparison under each of the \numCritCriteria{} declared criteria, holding the population, the
arms, the ranking and the parent-drop convention fixed, moves the verdict at up to
\numCritMovedMax{} of \numCritBudgets{} budgets (Table~\ref{SI-tab:si-criterion}).

At $k=15$ the margin between GRAIL's better arm and the strongest comparator runs from
$\numCritWorstOneFive$ under canonical SMILES equality to $+\numCritBestOneFive$ under the full
InChIKey, a range of \numCritSwingOneFive{}, and the sign of the conclusion changes with it. Under
two of the \numCritCriteria{} criteria GRAIL leads at no budget at all; under two others it leads
at four.

The criterion this paper reports is not the one that flatters it. Ordered by how many budgets each
yields a lead, the tautomer-aware key sits \numCritDefaultRank{} of \numCritCriteria{}, exactly
the median: it gives \numCritLeadsDefault{} against the \numCritLeadsBest{} available. It was
chosen because the references carry no stereochemistry and do not distinguish tautomers, not
because of what it returns.

Ranking is settled before any key is taken, so the criterion changes what counts as a hit and
never what is ranked. A single cell from this grid, reported without its criterion, would have
supported almost any claim about which system is better.

\numCaseExhFound{}, reaching every one of them by $k=30$. Both timings are one substrate on one
run rather than the medians of Table~\ref{tab:modes}. The division the sweep shows across
\numPopulationN{} substrates is visible on this one molecule: the interactive mode is right about what

The attribution is what a score cannot supply. All three phosphorylations fire at the same
substrate atoms, the 5$^\prime$ hydroxymethyl, under three different rules, and the deamination
fires on the pyrimidine ring, so the output states which transformation is claimed and where,
and a candidate can be rejected on mechanistic grounds. On the stored drawing all four rules are
mined, so the part of the bank with no expert provenance is what carries this molecule; on the
drawing a chemist would use, the deamination is found instead by a curated template and thirty
ranks lower. Which templates carry a molecule is itself a function of how the molecule is drawn,
and Figure~\ref{fig:case} shows both.

\subsection{Where the design came from}

Every deployed choice is a registered hypothesis with a threshold and a falsification condition,
tested where it was not selected (Table~\ref{tab:hyp}). Of the sixteen registered, ten are checked here: seven
confirmed and three failed, on the falsification condition each was written with, which compares
the point estimate to the threshold. Read instead against the interval, three of the seven
confirmations are unresolved rather than clear: P1, P2 and P5 each have a threshold inside their
own interval, so the data establish that the effect exceeds zero and not that it exceeds the
registered bar. Both readings are reported and neither verdict is revised. The remaining six are registered against work this paper does not
report and are stated with their thresholds, so that a later result on any of them cannot be a
threshold chosen after the fact.

\subsection{Group ranking, and two mechanisms that failed}

Candidates group by molecular formula. An oracle ordering these groups by whether they contain a
reference is worth $+\numOracleHeadroom$ over the ranking it is measured against, which is the
product ordering with each group emitted as a block on the uncapped pool. That is not the deployed
ranking, so the figure bounds a configuration that is not deployed.

P6 trained a listwise group scorer applied to whole groups. Registered threshold $+\numHEightThreshold$; measured
on the comparison set, $\numHEightDiff$. The deficit splits into $\numHEightDesign$ from the
design, block emission being worse than interleaved (\numHEightBlocked{} against
\numHEightInterleaved{}), and $\numHEightModel$ from the model. Because the oracle is measured
in blocked form, \numHEightDesignShare{} of the headroom it promises is the return of what the
design spends.

P5 used the same model untrained further, entering its group score as a third ranking in the same
fusion. Measured $+\numHOneTwoDiff$, 95\% CI $[+\numHOneTwoLo, +\numHOneTwoHi]$. The effect is not
selection: all twenty configurations beat the base on validation and 70\% of that margin survived
the move.

P7 tested the converse, the group signal as a gate before fusion, at a registered
$+\numHOneFourThreshold$. Measured $\numHOneFourDiff$,
separated against the hypothesis, and the mechanism is closed by an oracle rather than by model
quality: a perfect group ranking through the gate gives $\numHOneFourCeiling$, worse than the
generator cap it replaces. The median substrate has one group containing a reference while filling
the cap admits at least seven, so after the single informative group the gate spends its budget on
groups its signal cannot separate. The two results are symmetric, $+\numHOneTwoDiff$ as a nudge on
the generator's ordering and $\numHOneFourDiff$ as a replacement for it.

A binary group ordering entered as the third term reaches \numHOneTwoCeilingrecall{}, the same the
trained scorer reaches. That is not an upper bound and we do not use it as one: a binary ordering
leaves every reference-free group tied and on validation the trained scorer exceeds it,
$+\numHOneTwoVal$ against $+\numHOneTwoValceiling$.

\subsection{What retraining moves}

Every recall figure in this paper comes from one trained checkpoint, and the intervals throughout
are paired bootstraps over substrates, which quantify the population and not the training. To
give the registered effects a scale, the generator and filter were retrained under
\numSeedN{} seeds and the whole pipeline re-evaluated on the full test split: macro recall@15 is
\numSeedMacroMean{} with a standard deviation of \numSeedMacroStd{}, and micro
\numSeedMicroMean{} with \numSeedMicroStd{}. That study ran on an earlier operating point than
the one reported here, so its level is not comparable to the tables above; its spread is what it
is offered for. Read against it, the rank fusion of P1 is an order of magnitude larger than
retraining noise and the pool cap of P2 several times larger, while the rule budget of P3, at
$+\numHOneZeroBought$ on validation, is within it.

Standardisation is the binding constraint (Figure~\ref{fig:cost}).
Three other explanations were tested and rejected by measurement: the filter's maximum-common-substructure
step, rule application, and substrate size as an axis. The cause was product standardisation, at
\numHOneThreeEnumeration{} times the remaining work, run once per product of every template. A
cache had concealed it, a repeated substrate running many times faster.

Moving standardisation to surviving candidates (P8) freed recall, $\numHOneThreeRecallDiff$
against a cap of $0.01$, but failed on time, $\numHOneThreeFactor\times$ against a registered
$10\times$. The mechanism was right and the arithmetic was not: enumeration collapsed, and
standardising the survivors became the majority of the new arm. Removing most of the work does not
remove most of the time once the remainder is the new majority.

Adding a tautomer enumeration budget of \numHOneFiveBudget{} (P9) passes both halves: median
\numHOneThreeTimeBefore~s to \numHOneFiveTime~s against a registered target below ten, recall loss
\numHOneFiveRecallDiff{} against a cap of $0.01$. Of \numHOneFiveCandidates{} candidates,
\numHOneFiveMoved{} standardise to a different molecule under the bounded budget.

The two arms did not run under the same machine load. Correcting for it leaves
\numHOneFiveAttributable$\times$ attributable to the budget itself, still inside the target;
the correction and the safeguard that kept the budget from moving what counts as a hit are in
the Supporting Information.

Cost is reported at the tail as well as at the median, because a service is used at both. The
interactive mode's mean is six times its median, and its slowest substrate is three orders of
magnitude above it.

\subsection{The ceiling, and how much of it is the corpus}

Applying the whole bank to every test substrate recovers \numCeilingCoverage{} of references in
one step, under the same tautomer-aware matching criterion the comparison defaults to and on the
same evaluated test set. The remainder divides by the reaction-centre change it requires: \numCeilingNovel{} need
a transformation type the bank does not hold, \numCeilingKnown{} need a type it holds whose rule
did not fire, and \numCeilingUntypeable{} cannot be typed.

The presentation of hydrogen is part of that measurement and is named here rather than left to the
code. Templates are matched against the substrate with hydrogens implicit, which is the convention
every deployed firing path uses. Two conventions are defensible, and across them the uncovered
count is \numHydUncoveredimplicit{} with hydrogens implicit and \numHydUncoveredcompleted{} with
them made explicit and the templates completed to match; the figure reported here sits between the
two, at the conservative end. A third arm, hydrogens made explicit without completing the
templates, is an incomplete loop rather than a convention and reaches only
\numHydUncoveredexpanded{}; the instrument's own record excludes it. The released public entry
point defaults to that third presentation, so calling it without naming the convention
returns \numHydUncoveredexpanded{} uncovered and not \numCeilingUncovered{}. The residual
dependence of the bound on the convention, measured per template, is
$\numHydResidual{}$, 95\% CI $[\numHydResiduallo{}, \numHydResidualhi{}]$, an interval that
excludes zero: the bound is convention-dependent, by \numHydResidualrefs{} references in
\numHydReferences{}.

Relaxing the convention-dependent primitives in the templates recovers three of the
\numCeilingKnown{}, moving the ceiling by $+0.0012$. A bound computed before the run predicted
about eight.

The \numCeilingNovel{} misses of absent type collapse into \numCensusTypes{} distinct types, of
which \numCensusOnce{} occur once and carry \numCensusOnceSharePct{} of the mass. The objection that
this is a consequence of how finely a type is defined is answered by varying the definition. What
that answer covers should be said exactly: it covers the \numCeilingNovel{} misses of absent
type and not the other \numCeilingKnown{} plus \numCeilingUntypeable{} of the
\numCeilingUncovered{}. A miss whose type the bank holds and whose rule did not fire is an
implementation failure by construction, and three of the \numCeilingKnown{} were recovered by
relaxing convention-dependent primitives, so that arm is not invariant to anything.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Where the advantage is, and where it is not}

The comparison does not produce a winner. SyGMa recovers more at the head of the list, and at
$k \le 5$ the interval separates against GRAIL. GRAIL continues to improve at budgets where the
other systems stop. At a budget of three candidates SyGMa is the better choice; at fifty, GRAIL
is.

That division has a consequence for how such tools are compared. A single recall figure at a
single budget would have named a different winner depending on which budget its author chose,
and each of those choices would have been defensible. That is the case for reporting the sweep
rather than a single cell, and it is made here on this system's own numbers.

\subsection{What the system offers that the incumbent does not}

Three properties, none of them a recall figure. Every prediction names the transformation rule
that produced it and the site at which it fired, which allows a candidate to be accepted or
rejected on mechanistic grounds. The whole output is ranked, whereas the incumbent ranks part of
its output and leaves the remainder in file order. And every evaluation choice is declared with
every cell of the resulting grid reported, which is what makes the comparison independently
reproducible.

\subsection{What the numbers do not support}

GRAIL does not lead at the tight budgets. At $k=15$ and $20$ no arm separates from the strongest
comparator. The established lead is at $k=30$ and $50$. The system is not compared against the 2025--26 wave, because that wave cannot be run as
released. Of \numComparatorsUnavailable{} such systems, one releases training code without a
checkpoint \citep{Larsson_2025}, one has a withdrawn module \citep{Zhou_2025}, one offers only a
web form \citep{Zhou_2026} and one has no repository \citep{Chavan_2026}. No ranking in this
literature can presently be verified independently, which is the concrete case for leaderboards
releasing per-item predictions rather than aggregate scores \citep{Rodriguez_2021, Mishra_2021}. Precision figures are bounded by annotation completeness. And the advantage at
wide budgets is partly a statement about output length on all sides rather than about ranking.

\subsection{The ceiling bounds this formulation, not the task}

The shortfall is mostly a property of the corpus the templates are mined from, established by
invariance rather than asserted, though the hydrogen convention moves it by
\numHydResidualrefs{} references. Mining is saturated. Extending coverage requires new reaction corpora,
and the census narrows the search: synthetic corpora are excluded by measurement, leaving
enzymatic and regulatory sources. Metabolite identifications from radiolabelled human ADME studies
reported in regulatory submissions are human \emph{in vivo} data on exactly the population
predicted here, have been used to benchmark this class of tool \citep{Gao_2026}, and are, to
our knowledge, not systematically mined as a source of rules. The bound applies to any
predictor trained on a comparable corpus, including sequence-to-sequence systems, which do not
report it.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

GRAIL predicts metabolite structures by selecting templates from a bank of \numBankRules{}
reaction rules, applying them, and ranking the products by a reciprocal rank fusion of a pair
filter and the rule selector. The form in which the two learned scores are combined mattered more
than what produced them: replacing their product with the rank fusion gained more recall than any
architectural change tested here. Every prediction carries the template that produced it and the
atoms it acted on, and the whole output is ranked.

Measured against every published predictor that could be run on the same substrates, the
comparison divides by output budget rather than resolving into an ordering, and the division is
the result. Under the evaluation choices swept here, the verdict moves with the matching
criterion and with the budget, so a single recall figure would have named a different leader
depending on decisions its author never had to state. Reporting the grid is what makes the
comparison checkable, and releasing every arm's per-substrate predictions is what lets a third
party recompute it.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Predictions are single-step in the sense that one template is applied once, which is not the same
as one enzymatic step, and the gap between the two is larger than one instrument alone shows. The
first counts the loci of the reaction centre of the pair a template was mined from and flags
\numCompositeLoci{} of \numCompositeScored{} mined templates, \numCompositeLocisharePct{}. That is
a floor and not a bound: two edits that share or neighbour centre atoms arrive as one locus and
are counted once. A second instrument, whose threshold was registered before it was written,
counts core-incident edits (bonds between matched atoms whose order or presence moves, plus
attachment points gained or lost, with bonds inside an added fragment not counted) and calls a
template composite at \numCompositeThreshold{} or more, above every single enzymatic turnover.
It flags \numCompositeEdits{}, and the two instruments agree on only \numCompositeBoth{}, so
their union is \numCompositeUnion{} templates, \numCompositeUnionsharePct{} of the mined templates.
Registered prediction P10 set the bar at \numCompositeBarPct{} before either count existed and the
union clears it.

The corpus annotates metabolites several enzymatic steps from their parent, and a template mined
from such a pair performs those steps together: inspection finds a rule oxidising two ring
positions at once and a rule that both substitutes and conjugates glutathione. The coverage
ceiling this paper reports is therefore a one-template ceiling and not a one-enzyme one, and the
corrected share explains why applying the bank at depth two lifts it by only about $0.012$ at
$8.5$ times the candidate cost: a fifth of the mined bank already carries the compositions a
second application would have to discover, so the second application mostly rediscovers them. Precision is bounded by annotation
completeness. The rule-budget threshold in P3 is a property of the requested budget rather than
of the system. The group-ranking headroom is real and unreached by both registered
compositions for structural reasons. Every method runs out of candidates at wide budgets, so the
widest columns measure list length on all sides. The comparison population is the
\numPopulationN{} substrates on which all methods predict, a quarter of the evaluated test split
and not a random quarter of it.

\section{Data and software availability}

Source code, the deployed generator and filter checkpoints, the \numBankRules{}-template rule
bank, the split manifest, the annotated references every recall figure in this paper is measured
against, the frozen per-substrate predictions of every comparator, the evaluation harness, the
provenance sweep and the preregistration with every hypothesis and its outcome are at
\texttt{github.com/doctawho42/GRAIL}, and are available to reviewers at submission. The
repository's licence is \RepoLicence{}. The released model is to be deployed as a service on
\texttt{way2drug.ru}; that deployment is not a condition of anything reported here, and every
figure in this paper is reproducible from the repository without it. Releasing the checkpoints is the same bar this paper applies to others: a system
whose weights are not obtainable cannot be compared against, which is why one of the
\numComparatorsUnavailable{} systems of 2025--26 is excluded here. The train and
validation candidate pools are deposited at \ZenodoDoi{}, minted on publishing the deposit. The annotated
substrate--metabolite corpus is assembled from ChEMBL \citep{Zdrazil_2024}, DrugBank
\citep{Wishart_2018}, MetXBioDB \citep{Djoumbou_Feunang_2019} and the GLORYx reference set
\citep{de_Bruyn_Kops_2020}, at versions \CorpusVersions{}.

The corpus is not redistributed as a corpus, and the reason is a limitation of this release
rather than an open question. What is released is narrower and is named exactly: the evaluated
test substrates and their annotated metabolites, as structures, so that every recall figure here
can be recomputed under any of the five criteria; not the training and validation splits, not the
negatives, and not the source records the corpus was selected from. ChEMBL is released under a ShareAlike licence and DrugBank under a
NonCommercial one; a single derivative drawing on both cannot satisfy each, because the
non-commercial clause is a restriction ShareAlike forbids adding and dropping it breaches
DrugBank's terms. Third-party restrictions therefore apply to the corpus records, and the
question of the derived corpus's licence is \CorpusLicence{}. What is deposited holds
tautomer-canonical hashes of every substrate and of every annotated metabolite, together with the
candidate structures this work's rule bank produced, which are our output and not source records.
Holding licences for the four sources, the records can be obtained, the same hashes computed and
joined to everything released here; the split manifest confirms that a rebuild matches rather
than merely reporting that it differs. The one step that cannot be reproduced is the
extraction that selected from the four sources, which is not in the repository and is recorded as
such in the Supporting Information.

The numbers in this paper come from \numProvSources{} artifacts. A build gate establishes that
by running the number generator with its artifact reader instrumented, so the count is of files
opened rather than of matches in the source: \numProvSourcesunpinnedWord{} of the
\numProvSources{} fall outside the pinned set and \numProvSourcesunverifiableWord{} carry a
producer the provenance sweep cannot identify.
Each records the commit that wrote it; a sweep recomputes the hash of its producer and refuses the
artifact if that producer has changed, and all \numProvPinned{} are current. A split manifest
pins by digest the triple files, the substrate sets, the evaluated test set, the rule bank and all
third-party artifacts, and a verify mode recomputes and names any leaf that has moved.

Two pinned artifacts are too large to commit: the train and validation candidate pools, which
hold every candidate the generator produced for every substrate of their population together with
both component scores, and which are the deposit named above. The repository pins the
SHA-256 of both the raw file and the uploaded archive in
\texttt{paper2/zenodo\_manifest.json}, and \texttt{scripts/zenodo\_deposit.py -{}-verify}
checks a download against it, so the deposit is verifiable against the version used here rather
than merely available.

The guarantee covers the pinned set and not the directory: of \numProvFiles{} files under
\texttt{results/}, \numProvUnstamped{} carry no stamp and \numProvChanged{} were written by a
producer that has since changed. Those files support no number in this paper.
