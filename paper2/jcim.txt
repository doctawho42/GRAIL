Introduction

A drug that reaches the bloodstream does not
stay the compound that was administered. Enzymes, chiefly in the liver, convert it into a series
of other compounds, and those compounds carry
their own activity, their own toxicity and their
own persistence. A metabolite can be the agent
that makes a drug work, as with the antiviral
prodrugs activated by phosphorylation; it can
also be the agent that makes one fail, as with
the reactive intermediates behind several withdrawals. The same question arises outside the
clinic: what an environmental chemical becomes
in an organism determines how far it travels and
how long it lasts.
Knowing which metabolites will form, as chemical structures rather than as a count or a rate,
is therefore useful early, when a compound exists only on paper and no experiment has been
run. Predicting them computationally has been
attempted for three decades, and two families of method now dominate. Rule-based systems, among them SyGMa 1 , BioTransformer 2 ,
GLORY and GLORYx 3,4 and MetaTox 5 , encode
known biotransformations as reaction templates
and enumerate the products of applying them
1

to a query structure. Sequence-to-sequence systems, among them MetaTrans 6 and MetaPredictor 7 , treat the problem as translation, mapping
a text representation of the substrate to text
representations of its metabolites. Recent work
extends both lines: a chemical language model 8 ,
a mechanistically informed graph framework 9 ,
a server predicting pathway, site and product
together 10 , and a preprint predicting all three
jointly 11 .
What is missing is not another predictor but
a way to tell these apart. The usual measure is
recall: the fraction of the metabolites recorded
for a compound that a system returns. That
fraction depends on two decisions made outside
the model. The first is what counts as a match,
since two chemical structures can denote the
same molecule while differing in how a hydrogen
is placed or how a ring is drawn. The second is
how many candidates the system is allowed to
return, since any system recovers more of the
answer if permitted to list more guesses. Published predictors differ on both and report them
inconsistently, and the difference is not small:
the four compared here emit between 10.7 and
73 candidates per substrate by default, so a recall figure quoted for one is not on the scale
of a recall figure quoted for another. Independent evaluations of these tools report the same
obstacle 12–14 .
This work addresses that.
We present
GRAIL (Graph-scored Rule Application with
Inspectable Localisation), a metabolite structure predictor built from a bank of reaction
templates, a learned model that selects which
templates to apply, and a learned model that
ranks the resulting candidates. Every prediction it emits carries the template that produced
it and the atoms that template acted on, so
a chemist can accept or reject a candidate on
mechanistic grounds rather than on a score. How
much mechanism that carries depends on the
template: a large part of the bank is patterns of
three atoms or fewer, for which the named site
is a report of where the pattern matched rather
than of a mechanism, and Section 4.3 measures
both the size of that part and how often the
named site is the site that changed. We evaluate it with the two decisions above promoted

to declared axes, meaning that each is stated
and varied across its full range instead of being
fixed silently, against four published predictors
we obtained and ran on the same substrates,
and with the one comparator whose knowledge
is built on a source of this corpus named and
its overlap measured. Two others are absent
and neither absence was forced: GLORYx, the
closest modern counterpart to this system, was
not sought, and DeepCYP was not run, both
for the cost of submission rather than for want
of availability (Section S33). The comparison
does not produce a winner: which system leads
depends on how many candidates it is allowed
to return, and we report that dependence rather
than a single figure. We further measure how
much of the recorded chemistry lies outside the
reach of any single template application, and
find that most of it is chemistry the training
corpus does not contain, which no enlargement
of the bank would recover.
The article is organised as follows. Section 2
describes the corpus, the three stages of the
system and the evaluation protocol. Section 3
reports the comparison against published predictors, the contribution of each stage, the sensitivity of the result to the evaluation choices, a
worked example, and the coverage bound. Section 4 discusses where the advantage lies and
what the measurements do not establish, Section 5 states the conclusions and Section 6 the
limitations. Procedures, ablations and supporting measurements are in the Supporting Information.

That has a consequence for the comparison
which no split can remove. Rule-based comparators have no training set to hold out, and their
expert rules may encode the same metabolism
reported in these sources; BioTransformer is

name. Their knowledge therefore overlaps the

cleared: MetaTox and MetaPredictor publish

with it. What is reported is the one overlap that
could be computed, not a survey of all four.

Overview

Each design choice below is stated with the measurement that decided it and the prediction it
was registered as, rather than with a pointer
forward to Results. That is deliberate: a configuration and the evidence for it are one object,
and separating them is what lets a configuration be reported without its evidence. Results
reports the comparison, the ablations and the
coverage bound; the numbers in this section are
the ones that fixed the system before any of that
was measured.
GRAIL predicts in three stages, each separately inspectable. A generator scores every
rule in a bank of 7,581 reaction templates, written as SMIRKS transformation patterns, against
the substrate. The selected rules are then applied with RDKit 2022.09.5, and their products
are standardised 19,20 and deduplicated. A template that breaks a bond returns more than one
species, and each is treated as a candidate in its
own right: every product of an application, and
every disconnected component of every product,
enters the pool separately, provided it has at
least two heavy atoms, which drops a lone leaving group such as water or a halide and keeps
a small metabolite such as formate. No annotated metabolite in the evaluated test set is a
multi-component species, so the convention decides what is offered and cannot decide whether
a reference is reached. A scorer scores each
(substrate, candidate) pair, encoding the two
molecules as separate graphs rather than as one
merged graph, and the candidates are ordered
by a rank fusion of that scorer’s and the generator’s orderings. The merged variant, which
joins the two graphs along their maximum common substructure, was built and measured and
is not what ships (Section S21). Every emitted
candidate carries the identity of the rule that
produced it and the site at which it fired.
A candidate reachable by several rules, or by
one rule firing at several sites, receives a single
score. Writing R(c) for the rules that produce c
and pr for the generator’s probability that rule

2.7

Evaluation

The rule deciding when a predicted structure
counts as one of the references is the matching criterion. It is treated here as a declared
axis with five settings: canonical SMILES equality, the full InChIKey, its stereochemistry-blind
first block, Tanimoto similarity of one over Morgan fingerprints, and a tautomer-aware key that
canonicalises tautomers on both sides. The last
is the default, because the references neither
carry stereochemistry nor distinguish tautomers,
and because standard identifiers normalise only
a subset of tautomers 21 . That the annotation
carries no stereochemistry is also a limit on what
any of these systems can be asked for: nothing
in this pipeline is stereochemistry-aware, so sulfoxidation, epoxide hydrolysis and most conjugation are predicted as constitutions and not as
configurations. All five are reported for every
comparison, with the levels behind every verdict
in Table S12.
A third choice sits behind both: how the substrate itself is drawn. Templates are matched
against the structure the corpus stores, and that
corpus draws amides as imidic acids, so the
choice is not neutral. It is a deployment question and not only an evaluation one, because
a user submits the drawing a chemist would
make: given gemcitabine as a chemist draws it,
the interactive mode returns 16 candidates and
none of the four annotated metabolites, against
one of four on the stored drawing. Pooled over
the comparison set the effect is small because
the standardiser changes only 81 of 291 substrates; on those it changes it is −0.0354 at
a budget of 15 against −0.0105 pooled. Rebuilding both GRAIL arms with every substrate
redrawn by the standardiser moves 1 of 54 armagainst-comparator verdicts, none of them a
lead claimed here, and the coverage ceiling on
that population by −0.0075 with the interval
covering zero.

3.2

What each stage contributes
to the order

The two learned scorers are combined, and neither has been reported alone. Re-ranking one
pool five ways, with the pool, the matching rule
and the budget fixed, separates the contribution of each from the contribution of the form
in which they are combined. No model runs:
the pools carry both component scores for every
candidate.
At k = 15 on the comparison set the fusion reaches 0.5353, the generator’s score alone
0.4271, the filter’s alone 0.3895, their product 0.4526 and a seeded permutation 0.1128.
The fusion separates from every one of them:
+0.1083 [+0.0774, +0.1399] over the generator
alone, +0.1459 [+0.1140, +0.1792] over the filter alone, and +0.0827 [+0.0584, +0.1078] over
their product. The validation draw ranks the
arms in the same order by point estimate, with
one difference noted below.
Two qualifications belong with that. Which
single scorer is better does not transfer: on the
comparison set the generator’s ordering beats
the filter’s, on validation the two do not separate.
And the fusion’s advantage over the product is
a property of depth rather than of the head:
at k = 1 the difference is 0 on the comparison
set and +0.0153 on validation, and the interval
covers zero on both. Neither stage is sufficient
alone, and the gain this paper attributes to the
form of the combination is not a property of one
population alone.

3.5

How much of the verdict is
the criterion

The argument above is that a recall figure depends on evaluation choices this literature leaves
unstated. It is an argument about published
numbers, and it applies to ours. Recomputing the whole comparison under each of the 5
declared criteria, holding the population, the
arms, the ranking and the parent-drop convention fixed, moves the verdict at up to 6 of 9
budgets (Table 4).
At k = 15 the margin between GRAIL’s better

4.1

Where the advantage is, and
where it is not

The comparison does not produce a winner.
SyGMa recovers more at the head of the list,
and at k ≤ 5 the interval separates against
GRAIL. GRAIL continues to improve at budgets where the other systems stop. At a budget
of three candidates SyGMa is the better choice;
at fifty this work’s exhaustive arm recovers more,
and against SyGMa it does so for a reason that
survives matching the two on list length, while
against MetaTox and MetaPredictor it does not.
The first half of that is a result about the field
and not about this system. SyGMa is the oldest
and by far the smallest method in the comparison, 175 published rules against this bank’s
7,581, and at every budget of five or fewer it
recovers more of the annotated metabolites than
any other method here, including two published
since. A chemist reading a short list is working
at exactly those budgets. That a rule set of this
size is not beaten at the head of the list by any
of the systems here, two of them released since,
is worth stating plainly, and it is visible only
because the budget is swept: at a budget of fifty
the same table has this work’s exhaustive arm
ahead of SyGMa by +0.191, and ahead of every
comparator by at least +0.0752, so the ordering
at the head of the list reverses.
The division has a consequence for how such
tools are compared. A single recall figure at
a single budget would have named a different
winner depending on which budget its author
chose, and each of those choices would have been
defensible. That is the case for reporting the
sweep rather than a single cell, and it is made
here on this system’s own numbers.

Conclusions

GRAIL predicts metabolite structures by selecting templates from a bank of 7,581 reaction
rules, applying them, and ranking the products
by a reciprocal rank fusion of the generator’s
score and the filter’s. The form in which the two
learned scores are combined mattered more than
what produced them: replacing their product
with the rank fusion gained more recall than any
architectural change tested here. Every prediction carries the template that produced it and
the atoms it acted on, and the whole output is
ranked.

Limitations

Predictions are single-step in the sense that one
template is applied once, which is not the same
as one enzymatic step, and the gap between
the two is larger than one instrument alone
shows. The first counts the loci of the reaction centre of the pair a template was mined
from and flags 522 of 5,855 mined templates,
8.9%. That is a floor and not a bound: two edits that share or neighbour centre atoms arrive
as one locus and are counted once. A second
instrument, whose threshold was registered be23

mations suggests. Three of the seven confirmed
predictions clear the falsification condition as
written, which compares a point estimate to a
threshold, and not the stricter reading that asks
the whole interval to clear it: P1, P2 and P5 each
have their threshold inside their own interval, so
what the data establish for those three is that
the effect exceeds zero and not that it exceeds
the registered bar. P3’s threshold is in any case
a property of the budget requested rather than
of the system. The group-ranking headroom is
smaller than the uncontrolled figure suggests:
over a partition of matched granularity, only
the transformation-type grouping carries anything, and what it carries is unreached by both
registered compositions, for structural reasons.
Two of the reported quantities are bounded by
things outside the system. Precision is bounded
by how completely the corpus annotates, so
every precision figure here is a lower bound of
unknown depth. And every method runs out
of candidates at wide budgets, so the widest
columns measure list length on all sides as much
as they measure ordering.
The comparison population is the 291 substrates every method has an entry for, a quarter
of the evaluated test split and not a random
quarter of it: it is the submission list sent to
the comparator that is a web service, and how
those substrates were drawn from the split is not
recorded anywhere in this repository. Nothing
was excluded for emitting nothing, and the population still contains substrates a method answers
nothing for. One comparator, BioTransformer,
is built on a source this corpus is assembled from,
which favours it, and that is not corrected.
The deployed configuration is not the best one
this work knows of. The rule that combines
the templates reaching a candidate treats them
as independent evidence, and this bank does
not satisfy that; a blend that discounts duplication, weighting the largest single template’s
probability 0.65 against 0.35 for the noisy-or of
all of them (Equation 1), beats the deployed
rule on the validation draw at every budget
from one to 20, with the interval excluding zero
(Section S20), which includes the budget the
headline here is read at. Every number in this
paper is measured under the deployed rule, so

the release keeps it and the better setting is reported rather than substituted; a reader deploying this system should know that the shipped
aggregation is not the one a selection on validation prefers for a short list. A second system in
the same position, GLORYx, is absent from the
comparison, and its absence is not licensed by
that overlap, since a system with a comparable
one carries a column: it is absent because its
predictions could not be obtained here.
Nothing in this pipeline is stereochemistryaware. The standardiser strips configuration,
the matching criterion is chosen to ignore it,
and the annotation carries none, so sulfoxidation, epoxide hydrolysis and most conjugation
are predicted and scored as constitutions. For
a metabolite structure predictor that is a real
restriction and not a technicality: sulfoxide and
epoxide configuration carry the pharmacology of
several drug classes, and a system that cannot
represent them cannot be asked which enantiomer an enzyme makes. What is reported here
is therefore recall over constitutions. The criterion sweep does not bound what a configurationaware criterion would cost, and cannot: two of
its five settings return the identical verdict at
every one of the nine budgets, because the annotation has no configuration for them to disagree
about. Those two differ in more than configuration. The first block of an InChIKey is the
skeleton hash, so it drops the protonation and
isotope layers along with stereochemistry, and
under it a thiol and its thiolate are one structure
(Section S13). That layer has its own ceiling
and it is measured on the same population: 202
of the 2,597 annotated metabolites, 7.8%, carry
a formal charge on some atom, 165 of them as
net-neutral zwitterions and 37 net-charged. Neither layer moves a verdict in the sweep; both
bound how much a criterion that kept them
could ever move. What can be bounded is the
size of the question, and it is: 938 of the 2,597
annotated pairs, 36.1%, produce a metabolite
carrying a stereogenic element its substrate does
not have (832 a tetrahedral centre, 198 a stereogenic double bond). That is a ceiling on how
many references such a criterion could ever separate, not an estimate of what it would cost, and
nothing here assigns a configuration to any of
24

them.
No result here is external. Every figure is measured on one corpus assembled from four sources,
of which the only published reference set is one
of the four, so a held-out split of it is not an
independent test of the chemistry. The set that
would serve is named in Section 4: metabolite
identifications from radiolabelled human ADME
studies, already used to benchmark this class of
tool 14 . Until a system here is measured on one,
the comparison establishes an ordering within a
corpus and not a ranking of the methods.
