Their licences do not combine. ChEMBL 34 is released under CC BY-SA 3.0, which
requires a derivative to carry the same or a compatible ShareAlike licence and forbids adding

clause is a restriction ShareAlike does not permit, and dropping it breaches DrugBank’s
terms. MetXBioDB 1.0 is distributed with BioTransformer, whose terms require explicit
permission for commercial use or redistribution.
What the deposit contains follows from that, and the decision is taken here rather than
deferred. The candidate structures are produced by applying this work’s rule bank and are
not corpus records, so they are released. Every corpus structure, the substrates as well as
the annotated metabolites, appears only as a tautomer-canonical InChIKey, which is a hash
and does not carry the structure. No source record is redistributed in the deposit, and the
licence of the derived corpus is settled by not redistributing them: the deposit carries no
source structure. The repository is a separate channel and it is settled the same way, by
a substitution rather than by an answer to a question that does not resolve. It ships the
evaluated test substrates as structures, because reproducing the comparison means running a
predictor on one, and their annotated metabolites as the descriptors the matching criteria
are decided by: a hash of the canonical SMILES, the InChIKey, the tautomer key and the
on-bits of a folded Morgan fingerprint. A hash decides string equality exactly and a folded
fingerprint decides Tanimoto of one exactly, so all 5 declared criteria survive the substitution
and none of the metabolites survives it; a check compares each criterion’s verdict on the
descriptors with its verdict on the structures and reports no disagreement. What a reader
without the source licences loses is not a recall figure or a criterion but the structure-level
analyses of this document, which read the reference itself, and those need the corpus that
reader already needed. The deposit’s keys must still be joined against a substrate set rebuilt

S1.1

How the corpus was assembled

The corpus files were written in a single run months before this repository’s first commit,
and no script among its 857 commits reads any of the four sources or writes the structure
files. The assembly cannot therefore be read off code. What follows separates what the
shipped files establish about it from what only their producer could, and states the second as
a bounded absence rather than as silence.

What each record carries. The three splits hold 464,966 structure records, each with
five fields: an identifier, an InChI, a state, a SMILES string and an index. The identifier is
the one field that could carry a source accession, and it is empty in every one of the 464,966.
Per-source counts are therefore not recoverable from the data, and none is reported. The
state field takes three values, and its meaning is checked against the triples rather than
assumed: every substrate of a positive triple is marked as a substrate and every product of
one is marked as annotated, the remaining records being rule-applicable products carrying
no annotation. The positive-unlabelled negatives this work trains against are consequently
not derived when the data is loaded. They are stored, and which rule bank produced them is
not recorded.

The drawing the standardisation left. Every structure in the corpus is a fixed point of
the InChI round-trip: 378,228 of 378,238 train records, 41,591 of 41,591 in validation and
45,133 of 45,133 in test are exactly what their own stored InChI reads back to, and the
ten exceptions are salts whose counter-ion the SMILES has lost and the InChI kept. That
round-trip places a mobile hydrogen on oxygen, so an amide arrives as the imidic acid and
cytosine as its lactim. This is not the declared standardiser’s output: run under ten RDKit
releases spanning four years, standardize mol returns the amide in ten of ten, from both

S3

the amide and the imidic acid. The drawing therefore entered before the standardiser this
work declares, and rules are matched against it. Section S14 treats it as the axis it is.

De-duplication, and the conflicts there were none of. There is no de-duplication
code, and with the accession field empty there is no source field a conflict rule could have
arbitrated on. The files show that none was needed. 234 substrate structures appear under
more than one index across the three splits, and the two annotation sets differ in none of
them; one pair in the whole corpus is listed both as positive and as negative. The effective
rule is set union when the data is loaded, applied to structures after standardisation, with no
precedence between sources because no source is recorded. This is also why the split table
counts 1,246 test substrates by index and 1,198 by structure.

What is inside, for the sources that can be checked. Two of the four, MetXBioDB
and the GLORYx reference set, are physically present here and can be intersected with
the corpus by structure skeleton. The corpus holds 22,134 distinct annotated pairs. Of
MetXBioDB’s 2,035, 1,248 are inside it, 0.6133; of the GLORYx set’s 136 pairs over 37
parents, 63 pairs and 23 parents are. So the assembly ingested neither source wholesale: a
filter of unknown shape dropped nearly two fifths of MetXBioDB, and the measurement
bounds that filter without recovering it. What it does not do is attribute any pair to a source,
because a pair in MetXBioDB may equally have entered through DrugBank. ChEMBL and
DrugBank are not present here and admit no such measurement, so no fraction is claimed for
them.

The partition, and its seed. The three splits are substrate-disjoint and not moleculedisjoint, which the leakage audit records and the main text states. The repair that produced
the shipped clean triples ran in the substrate-level mode of scripts/fix splits.py; its
molecule-disjoint mode is a different partition and was not used. Test was passed through
untouched, and its clean file is byte-identical to its input. The three shipped clean files match

S4

the digests the split manifest declares. There is no split seed: the repair is deterministic and
draws on no random number generator, and the partition it repairs has no producer in this
repository. It is a fixed historical artifact carried forward by digest.

What a third party can and cannot rebuild. With their own licences for the four
sources, every source record can be obtained. What cannot be reconstructed is the extraction
and filtering that selected from them, because that step is not in the repository and the data
carries no accession to reverse it by. They can, given the structure files, reproduce every step
from there: the standardisation is declared, the label convention is checked above, the repair
is deterministic and released, and the manifest’s digests confirm a rebuild rather than merely
report that one differs. The unrecoverable step is the first one, and it is the whole of what is
missing.
The annotated metabolites of the evaluated test set are released as matching descriptors,
in results/test reference descriptors.json, alongside every arm’s per-substrate predictions. All 5 matching criteria are therefore recomputable on the comparison population,
not only the default one, and none of the metabolites is. That is checked rather than
asserted: reproduce from descriptors.py rebuilds the whole comparison under all 5 from
the released descriptors and the released predictions, reading no corpus structure, and its
default-criterion column for the whole bank matches Table 2 at every budget. The repository
and the Zenodo deposit differ in this, and the repository is the wider of the two.
The corpus is divided by substrate, so no substrate of one split appears in another.
Molecular overlap between splits is non-zero and expected, because a molecule that is a
substrate in one split may appear as a product in another: 2,408 molecules occur in both
train and test, and 243 test substrates appear as molecules in train. Both counts are recorded
so that neither is later discovered and mistaken for leakage.
Three evaluation populations are drawn from these splits, and every figure in the paper
names one. The evaluated test set is the 1,170 test substrates that carry at least one reference,

S5

holding all 2,597 references of the split; the substrates it excludes, 1,198 minus 1,170 of
them, carry none. The validation draw is a sample of 300 substrates at seed 0, of which
294 produced a candidate pool and 293 are held by both arms of a paired comparison. The
comparison set is the 291 substrates on which every method predicts.
No external evaluation set is reported. GLORYx is one of the four sources this corpus
is assembled from, so its 37 parent substrates are not external to it, and the 24 that fall
inside train or validation are there by construction rather than by leakage. Holding out one
source would not produce an external set either, because the sources overlap one another:
MetXBioDB and the GLORYx reference set both draw on the same published metabolism,
and DrugBank overlaps both.
The same fact bounds the comparison. Rule-based comparators have no training set that
could be held out, and their rules may encode the metabolism reported in these sources:
BioTransformer is built on MetXBioDB, and GLORYx was developed against the reference
set of that name. Their knowledge overlaps our test substrates by construction. No prior
benchmark in this area controls this asymmetry, and neither does this work; it is reported
because anyone comparing these numbers to published ones should know that the rule-based
figures are, in this respect, optimistic.

S2

What the annotation contains

Every recall figure in this paper is a statement about this annotation, and the paper reported
the figures at length without describing it. The census is here. Nothing in it is a model
output; it is the corpus read back.
The evaluated test set carries 2,597 annotated metabolites over 1,170 substrates, 2,292
of them distinct under the paper’s default key. All 2,597 parse and all are keyable, so no
reference is dropped before scoring. 226 keys are shared by more than one substrate, which
is expected: a common metabolite of two related drugs is one molecule.

S6

By heavy-atom count against the parent, 790 references are smaller, 283 are the same
size and 1,524 are larger. The median change is 1 heavy atoms and the fifth and ninety-fifth
percentiles are −13 and 12, but the tails are long in both directions: the extremes are −84
and +50. A bank of single-template edits is not the natural instrument for either tail, and
the coverage census in the main text is where that shows.
Ten elements appear. Carbon, oxygen and nitrogen account for almost all of the atoms,
with sulfur, chlorine, fluorine, phosphorus and bromine behind them and iodine and selenium
in single figures. Nothing in the annotation is outside what the rule bank’s chemistry
addresses.
Two properties bear directly on how the recall figures should be read. 8 references are
equal to their own substrate under the default key, an annotation that names a molecule
as its own metabolite, and the parent-drop convention discards exactly those, which is why
that convention’s effect is negative for the substrates concerned rather than zero. And the
annotation is positive-unlabelled throughout: a pair that does not appear is not a pair that
was tested and rejected. That is why precision is reported and is not used to order systems,
and why an absent prediction is never counted as a false positive anywhere in this paper.

The arms behave very differently, which is what makes this a strong test rather than a
formality. SyGMa never returns the substrate, in none of 291 lists, while 239 exhaustive-mode

that far in how often it bites has the maximum opportunity to move one and not the others,

them: the largest effect anywhere in the grid is 0.0030, no cell of 54 separates from zero, and
the effect is at most 11.1% of the narrowest separating margin anywhere in the comparison,
which is 0.0271. Where it is negative, the cause is the 2 substrates of this population whose
own tautomer key is among their annotated references, for which the convention discards a
hit the annotation counts. Across the whole evaluated test set there are 8 such substrates;
the comparison set holds 2 of them.

What kind of molecules these are

The corpus is assembled from four drug-centric sources and the manuscript motivates the
problem with environmental chemicals as well, so what the substrates actually are belongs
on the record beside the annotation, which is characterised elsewhere by heavy-atom change
and element composition. Table S4 describes them.
They are drug-like. Median molecular weight is 333.9 on the evaluated test set with a
calculated logP of 3.01, and the comparison set sits in the same place, which is one more
reading on whether that draw is unlike the split it came from. The 1,170 substrates carry 579
Bemis–Murcko scaffolds and 399 of those are carried by a single substrate, so the population
is diverse in skeleton rather than a few series sampled repeatedly.
What that does not establish is an applicability domain. A domain is a statement about
where performance holds, and it would need recall measured against these axes rather than
the axes described; the table says what was measured on, and a reader whose compounds sit
outside this range should read every figure here as untested for them.

Which chemistry each method recovers

A recall figure aggregates over transformations of very different kinds, and a reader deciding
whether any of these systems is usable on their compound class cannot read that off one
number. Table S8 splits the comparison set’s 665 references into 17 classes and reports recall
within each.
A class here is a change in molecular formula. That is what the annotation determines:
the corpus records a substrate and a product, not a mechanism, so a mechanism cannot be
recovered from it without guessing. One added oxygen is aliphatic hydroxylation, aromatic
hydroxylation, N-oxidation or S-oxidation, and nothing in the record separates them; the
class is named for the change and the ambiguity is stated with it in the artifact. Of the
references, none failed to resolve to a structure.
Three things in the table are worth naming. The largest class is not a functionalisation
at all but a cleavage losing two or more carbons, 151 references, and it is where the whole
bank pays for its size: 0.6159 against 0.4768 for the best comparator. The second largest, 142
references, is the class of deltas this vocabulary does not name, and no method exceeds 0.2394
on it, so a fifth of the annotated chemistry is transformation types none of these systems is

S15

built for. And of the eleven references that add two oxygens, five of the six methods recover
none and the sixth recovers 0.0909: a shared blind spot rather than a difference between
systems.
Two more are worth naming because they run the other way. The exhaustive arm is not
the best arm on 10 of the 17 classes, and on 5 of them it is below the interactive arm, which
returns a sixth as many candidates. Demethylation is the clearest: 0.8333 exhaustive against
0.9167 interactive and 0.9722 for the best comparator, on 36 references. A wider pool cannot
lose coverage, so what is lost there is position: candidates the smaller rule budget never
produced are ranked above the correct answer and push it past fifteen. That is the same
mechanism the ranking ablation measures in aggregate, seen on the class where it costs most.
Recall within a class is over that class’s references alone, so the small classes carry wide
uncertainty. No intervals are quoted for them and none should be read into the ordering of a
row with five references in it.

S9

The comparison at matched list length

A nominal budget is not a length. At k = 50 most comparator lists have already ended while
the exhaustive arm is still at its cap, so declaring the budget does not control the thing the
budget was introduced to control, and a difference read there is partly a difference in how
much each system emits. Table S7 gives the exhaustion rates; this gives the comparison with
the length removed as a variable.
The design is deliberately hostile to this work. On each substrate both arms are cut to
the number of candidates that comparator returned on that substrate: where MetaPredictor
returns two, the exhaustive arm is allowed two. The two are then read over the same slots on
every substrate rather than on average, which is what a nominal budget cannot do.
One bound on that length is this work’s and has to be declared, because a slot count is a
configuration and this paper’s argument is that a configuration left unstated is the defect.
The comparator’s own list is deduplicated, has a prediction equal to the substrate dropped,
and is then cut at 105 candidates, five past the pool cap of 100: beyond that neither of this
work’s arms has a candidate to put in the slot, so the comparison there would measure the
pool cap rather than the ordering. The cut binds on 45 of the 291 substrates for SyGMa and
6 for MetaTox, and on none for either of the two arms whose lists are shorter than the cut
everywhere, MetaPredictor and BioTransformer. What it costs the comparators is measured
rather than assumed: every contrast in Table 3 is identical with the cut removed, to four
decimal places, because SyGMa places none of the annotated metabolites past that rank, on
any substrate where its list runs longer.
The result splits the wide-budget leads in two. Against SyGMa the exhaustive arm
keeps +0.182 [+0.1446, +0.2208] over 58.37 slots, which is an ordering result and the largest
margin that survives this control. Larger differences are printed elsewhere in these documents,
at nominal budgets where list length is not held; those are the ones this control exists
to discount, so the comparison is between the margins in this table and not with them.
Against the other three nothing survives: +0.0015 [−0.0309, +0.0349] against MetaTox,
S17

−0.0195 [−0.0616, +0.0212] against MetaPredictor and −0.0045 [−0.0402, +0.0315] against
BioTransformer, every interval covering zero and two of the three pointing the other way.
What the sweep reports as a lead over those three at wide budgets is therefore a statement
about list length, and this work’s own thesis requires saying so. BioTransformer’s row
reproduces the figure its own section reports from a different instrument, which is the check
that the two agree rather than a second reading of the same number.
The interactive arm loses to MetaTox at matched length by −0.1504 [−0.1979, −0.1057],
which is the same deficit the main text reports at fixed budgets and is not an artefact of
either.

S10

The comparison under the other aggregation

Every figure in the main text’s comparison is micro, the ratio of sums. Macro, the mean
of per-substrate recall, weights a substrate carrying one annotated metabolite the same as
one carrying twelve, and the two therefore answer different questions rather than estimating
each other. Which one is used is a choice of the same kind as the matching criterion and the
output budget, so the whole comparison is recomputed under it.
The choice moves eight verdicts of the seventy-two contrasts computed under both, and
the table’s caption names each. Three of them are cells the main text states: the exhaustive
arm’s lead over MetaPredictor at a budget of fifteen and BioTransformer’s trail at a budget of
one, both of which separate under micro and not under macro, and the interactive arm’s trail
against MetaTox at twenty, which does the same. The wide budgets the paper reads its leads
at are unaffected. It also moves the levels, upward for every arm but one, because substrates
with few references are easier and count for more under this aggregation; BioTransformer is
the exception and its macro recall is the lower of the two at the widest budgets. A reader
who suspects the aggregation was chosen to suit the result can read which way each moved
cell goes from the caption, and the one that bears on a claim goes against this work.

S11

Precision

Precision is reported because a reader is entitled to it and not because it decides anything
here. Precision at k is hits over the predictions inside the window, so an arm whose list is
shorter than the budget is not charged for the empty slots; that is why MetaPredictor is flat
from k = 15 onwards, having emitted its whole list by then.
These figures order the systems differently from recall, and the ordering is not used. The
annotation is incomplete, so a predicted metabolite that is real but unannotated is counted
as a false positive. Every value below is therefore pessimistic by an unknown amount, and
the amount is larger for an arm that emits more, which flatters the short lists. Recall carries
the claims in this paper for that reason.

S12

The comparison’s differences and their intervals

The main text reports the level of each arm and the verdict at each budget. Table S11 gives
the quantity those verdicts are read from: the difference between each GRAIL arm and each
comparator, with the paired interval. Two of them are worth seeing rather than inferring.
The exhaustive mode’s lead over MetaTox at k = 30 excludes zero by little, and its two
unresolved budgets, k = 15 and k = 20, have intervals straddling zero narrowly rather than
lying far from it.

S13

Sensitivity of the comparison to the matching criterion

The verdict grid itself is Table 4 of the manuscript, since it is the evidence the paper’s title
advertises and belongs where the argument is made. The levels it is read from are Table S12:
every arm at every budget under every criterion, which is what lets a reader see whether a
sign moved because a level moved or because the arms reordered. What follows is what the
S19

grid does not show on its face.
The paper argues that a recall figure depends on evaluation choices that this literature
leaves unstated. The argument is made about published numbers, and Table 4 applies it to
our own: the same comparison, the same population, the same ranking, the same parent-drop
convention, with only the criterion moving.
Ranking is settled before any key is taken, so the criterion changes what counts as a hit
and never what is ranked. The parent-drop convention is re-derived under each criterion so
that it means the same thing in every column.
The two criteria under which GRAIL never leads sit at opposite ends of strictness.
Canonical SMILES equality is the strictest of the five, and under it a hit requires that
two pipelines produce the same string for the same molecule, which is a property of their
standardisation conventions rather than of their chemistry. Tanimoto similarity of one on
Morgan fingerprints is the loosest, and it merges structures a fingerprint cannot separate,
which credits every method with hits it did not earn and credits the comparators with more
of them. Neither is a defensible default, and the paper’s argument is not that one criterion is
right but that the choice has to be declared and its consequences shown.
The strict end carries a second entanglement that a reader should have before the column
is read as strictness alone. The references are stored in the corpus’s drawing, which writes
amides as imidic acids (Section S14), and every method’s output passes through a standardiser
that writes the amide. Canonical SMILES equality between the two is therefore partly a test
of whether a method reproduces the corpus’s own tautomer rather than of whether it found
the molecule. What that does not explain is the shape of the column, because the loosest
criterion produces the same one: under Tanimoto similarity of one the verdicts are identical
to canonical’s at every budget, and those are the two criteria under which no advantage of
this work separates anywhere. Whatever the two ends share, it is not strictness and it is not
the dialect, and the entanglement is stated here as something a reader of the strict column
should know rather than as the reason it reads as it does. The tautomer-aware key exists

S20

because reproducing a corpus’s tautomer is not the question a comparison should be asking,
and the canonical column is reported because declaring a criterion means printing what it
does, not only the one whose behaviour suits the argument.
The reference row of Table 4 reproduces the verdicts of the main text’s comparison exactly,
though it is computed by a different script from the same frozen predictions. The number
of references differs by one between criteria, at 665 for three of them and one fewer for two,
and the two cases have different causes. Under the first block of the InChIKey, the thiol
and thiolate annotated for butyrylthiocholine merge, because that block is the skeleton hash
and carries neither the protonation layer nor stereochemistry; the full InChIKey separates
them in its final character. Under Tanimoto similarity of one, two of the carboxychromanol
metabolites of α-tocopherol merge, because they differ by a single isoprenoid unit in the side
chain and a Morgan fingerprint of fixed radius counts the substructures a repeating unit
contains without counting how many times it repeats. Both merges are the criterion acting
as declared rather than a defect, and both are the reason the count is reported per criterion
rather than once.

S14

How the substrate is drawn

This paper sweeps five ways of judging whether a prediction is a hit and nine ways of deciding
how many predictions to make. It did not say how the substrate is drawn. That is a third
choice of the same kind, it is not neutral, and it decides which rules can fire at all.

The corpus speaks one dialect. Every structure in the corpus is a fixed point of the InChI
round-trip, and the round-trip places a mobile hydrogen on oxygen. An amide is therefore
stored as the imidic acid, R-C(= O)-NH-R′ arriving as R-C(OH) = N-R′ , and cytosine as its
4-imino-2-hydroxy lactim. Across the evaluated test set, 345 of 1,170 substrates are not fixed
points of the declared standardiser and 229 carry an imidic-drawn amide. The same holds
split by split, counted over the distinct structures each split marks as a substrate rather
S21

than over the substrates that carry a clean triple, which is the population Table S1 counts:
2,892 of 9,194 in train, 281 of 1,063 in validation, 350 of 1,200 in test. The two denominators
differ for the reason just given and by the amount it predicts: test marks 1,200 structures as
substrates and Table S1 counts 1,198, the two that differ carrying no clean triple. Every one
of the 345 moves is a pure tautomer move, invisible to this paper’s default matching criterion:
the drawing changes which rules fire and does not change what molecule it is.

The bank learned that dialect. Mining reads these pairs, so the templates inherit the
drawing. Counting templates by what their reactant side requires, the mined templates ask
for an imidic reactant 684 times against 628 for a real amide: in the portion of the bank this
work built, the unpopulated tautomer of the amide is the more frequently required of the
two. The curated collections, written by chemists, run the other way, 170 amide against 62
imidic, and not one of SyGMa’s 175 templates requires the imidic form. Amide hydrolysis
and amide N -dealkylation are core phase-I chemistry, so this is not a corner of the bank.

What it does to the worked example. Gemcitabine is stored as the lactim. On that
drawing the interactive mode finds 1 of 4 annotated metabolites, and the one it finds is
produced by mined template 4913, whose reactant side requires the exocyclic imine and which
cannot fire on the drug as a chemist draws it. Presented with the 4-amino-2-oxo structure
instead, the interactive mode finds 0 of 4. The exhaustive mode still finds all 4, at ranks 3,
15, 18, 32 against 3, 16, 18, 20 on the stored drawing, and the deamination it had attributed
to a mined template is found instead by curated template 251 at rank 32. Both drawings are
reported in Section S30 for that reason.

The arms did not all meet the same drawing. Five of the six arms of the comparison
were handed the substrate exactly as the corpus stores it: both modes of this system,
and SyGMa, MetaPredictor and BioTransformer. MetaTox was not. Its submission file
re-tautomerises the substrate to the natural form for 79 of the 291, on the stated ground

S22

that sending an unnatural imidic acid to an external service would be unfair to that service.
The reasoning is right and the asymmetry it leaves is real, and it runs against the arm that
took the trouble: MetaTox met the drawing its rules expect and SyGMa, whose published
templates are written entirely in the amide notation, did not.

What it does to the comparison. The axis is swept like the others. Both GRAIL arms
were rebuilt on the identical substrates, presented as the declared standardiser draws them,
with the same checkpoints, the same rule budgets, the same pool cap and the same ranking;
the pools stay keyed by the corpus string, so the annotation is one annotation and the two
drawings are paired substrate by substrate. Table S13 gives the paired effect on each arm
at each budget. The largest anywhere is −0.0105 for the exhaustive mode and −0.006 for
the interactive one; one of nine cells separate from zero for the first and none of nine for the
second.
Two properties of that measurement should be stated before it is read. The stored column
reproduces the comparison table of the main text at every one of its cells, which is the check
that the sweep measures the same quantity and not a near neighbour of it; and pointing
both dialects at the same pools returns exactly zero everywhere with every interval collapsed,
which is the check that the pairing is right. A sweep that could not show a non-difference as
zero would hide its own defect inside a plausible number.

The rule bank

The bank holds 7,581 templates spanning 4,417 distinct reaction types, of which 7,580 parse
under RDKit 2022.09.5. It is the deduplicated union of three curated collections, a fourth
that was carried as curated and is a second machine extraction, and the templates mined
from the training split alone, which is disjoint by substrate and not by molecule.

S25

The two halves are counted rather than described, and the split between them turns out to
be drawn in the wrong place. 1,725 templates are nominally curated and 5,856 mined. Three
curated collections ship with the code: data/smirks.txt (474), data/merged smirks.txt
(656) and resources/notebooks rules.txt (1,051), contributing 1,233 distinct rules to the
bank after deduplication. The remaining 492 match none of those three files.
They are not curated, and three independent marks say so. 477 of the 492 are verbatim
lines of a file whose one commit in the history describes it as extracted reaction rules. They
are written in element notation with multi-component reactant groups, like that file and
unlike every hand-written collection here. And they carry the corpus’s imidic drawing at
the rate the mined templates do, 0.1179 against 0.1168, where the named expert collections
carry it at 0.0032, a factor of thirty-seven; that rate is the fingerprint of a template extracted
from these annotated pairs rather than written by a chemist. The bank therefore holds two
machine extractions and one curated body, in two different template notations, and the
curated body is 1,233 templates rather than 1,725. Where the named collections came from
can be settled for most of them by measurement rather than recollection, and the answer is
sharper than the curated half’s own total suggests. All 966 of the borrowed templates fall
inside the named body of 1,233 and none among the 492 of the earlier extraction, so the share
to read is 78.3% of the collections described as curated chemistry rather than 56% of the half
they sit in. They are present character for character in a rule set somebody else published:
611 of BioTransformer’s 668 reaction templates; 273 templates attributed to SyGMa, which
is more than the 152 of its own distribution counted below because GLORYx’s rule file
carries a further set of SyGMa rules in a notation that distribution does not use; and 82 that
GLORYx’s file attributes to itself. String equality cannot trace a rule that was rewritten, so
this is a lower bound on borrowing and never an upper one, and the 759 templates matching
nothing are not thereby shown to be original.
The curated half is therefore largely not original to this work, and saying so is the point
of measuring it. It also carries an obligation: these rule sets are distributed under terms of

GPL with no version and ships no licence text, which is read here the way

One further fact about the curated collections bears on how the comparison in the main
text is read. 152 of SyGMa’s 175 published rules are present in this bank verbatim: 86.9% of
SyGMa’s rule set, and 8.8% of the 1,725 templates nominally carried as curated. All 152 fall
among the named collections and 0 in the mined half, so the containment is inherited from
an expert collection rather than rediscovered by mining. Absorbing a published rule set is
ordinary practice and is not the point. The point is that the two banks in the comparison
are not independent objects, which bears on how the result reads: SyGMa’s lead at the
tightest budgets is a ranking result over chemistry the two systems largely share, and this
work’s advantage at the wider ones is that shared chemistry plus the 7,581 minus 152 further
templates around it. The 23 SyGMa rules that are absent are the second half of the same
fact and are not claimed as coverage.
Mining locates a ring-aware maximum common substructure for each annotated substrate–
product pair. Atoms whose element, formal charge, hydrogen count, aromaticity or degree
changed, atom pairs whose bond order changed, and leaving or entering atoms are flagged as
the reaction centre. The centre is expanded by one bond and emitted as a mapped SMIRKS
template. A template survives only if applying it to its own source substrate regenerates the
annotated product after canonicalisation, and a selectivity filter rejects templates producing
more than 200 distinct products on average across a 50-substrate sample.
That bar bounds how unspecific a template may be; it does not make the bank specific,
and the distribution is worth reporting beside it. Counting the atoms on each template’s
reactant side, 261 of 7,580 parseable templates, 3.4%, have two atoms or fewer and 818,
10.8%, have three or fewer. A template of that size matches almost anywhere it can match at
all, so for those the site discrimination is supplied by the learned stages rather than by the

S28

template. That qualifies the claim that a prediction is rule-attributable without withdrawing
it: the rule and the firing atoms are still reported, and what is weaker is the rule’s own
selectivity. The tail is not marginal in practice: 11 of the twenty highest-ranked candidates
in the worked example come from templates with three reactant atoms or fewer.
Support across mined templates is heavily skewed: 4,655 of 5,856 rest on a single distinct
training pair, and 4,274 on a single occurrence of one, the difference being templates whose
catalog entry lists the same pair more than once. The first is the count that bears on
saturation, because withholding a pair withholds every occurrence of it, and it is the one
Section S19 uses. Re-running the miner on the same split re-derives every template it derived
before and none new, which shows it is deterministic and nothing more.

What the rule budget buys

The interactive mode applies 30 templates because that is the value the released checkpoint
was trained at. That is a fact about how the run was configured and not an argument for the
number, so the curve is measured: validation pools built at a range of budgets with the pool
cap, the fusion, the filter and the matching criterion identical, on 293 substrates. Validation
and not the comparison set, because a budget chosen on the population the comparison is
reported on would be chosen on the test.
That the model is identical across the points is checked and not assumed, because it is
the one thing a pool did not record. Both scores in a pool are deterministic functions of
the substrate and the candidate, so the models that wrote them can be recovered: scoring a
pool’s own candidates with every trained checkpoint held here reproduces its generator scores
under exactly one file and its filter scores under exactly one file, with every other checkpoint
disagreeing on nearly all of them. Every point of this curve resolves to the deployed pair on
both stages, two runs that ship a byte-identical generator counting as one file rather than as
an ambiguity, and the producer of the curve refuses to write unless that measurement exists,

covers every pool it reads and returns the deployed run for all of them. A curve whose points
differ in the model as well as in the parameter measures the model, and nothing else here
would have said so.
The curve runs to the whole bank. Stopping it at a rule budget of fifty would have left the
sweep short of the configuration the exhaustive mode actually applies, and the comparison
between the two modes then rests on two points measured on different populations rather
than on one curve.
The deployed value sits at the knee. Both smaller budgets lose to it at a budget of fifteen
with the interval excluding zero, and the next larger one gains +0.0015 [−0.0164, 0.0196],
an interval covering zero, while returning 24.72 candidates against 16.42. The whole bank
gains +0.0092 [−0.0231, 0.0399] on the same terms, an interval that also covers zero, for
97.99 candidates, close enough to the pool cap of 100 that the cap sets the list length on
most substrates. Every one of those candidates is standardised, which the runtime section
identifies as the binding cost, and no rule budget up to and including the whole bank beats
the deployed one at an output budget of fifteen, which is where the headline is read.
That is not the whole story, and the column for an output budget of thirty makes it
plain: read there, the larger rule budget does buy something, because the pool it builds
has not run out. The whole bank reaches 0.6015 there against 0.4901, a paired gain of
+0.1115 [+0.0802, +0.1431] and the largest step in the column, which is the exhaustive mode’s
advantage measured as a point on this curve rather than as a separate design. The contrast is
computed at both output budgets the table is read at, because a recommendation that rests
on two point estimates is the thing this work asks other papers not to make. A deployment
answering with thirty or fifty candidates should raise the rule budget for exactly the reason
the Discussion gives for the interactive mode’s deficit there, and pay the standardisation cost
that follows.
One substrate of the validation draw does not finish the whole bank in any time worth
spending, so the curve is paired on the 293 substrates every budget holds and that one sits

anything

The sweep over how the substrate is drawn reports its effect pooled over the whole comparison
set. That is the right number for reading the comparison, because the comparison is over the
whole set, and it is the wrong number for a user, because most substrates in that set carry
no amide: their two drawings are the same molecule and contribute a difference of exactly

Conditioned on the substrates it changes, and on the same pools, the exhaustive arm loses

Whether template discovery has saturated

Re-running the miner on the same split re-derives every template and none new, which is a
determinism check and not a saturation one. Saturation is a claim about what more data
would yield, and it can be measured on files already held: the mining catalog records which
training pairs each template was derived from, so pairs can be withheld and the templates
that would still have been found counted.
Of 5,856 mined templates over 15,747 distinct training pairs, 4,655 rest on a single pair,
79.5%. A quarter of the pairs yields 1,892 templates, half 3,346, three quarters 4,653 and the
whole set 5,856, so the curve is close to linear and at its right-hand end is still gaining 298.8
templates per thousand further pairs. Good and Turing’s estimate of the probability mass
belonging to types not yet seen is 0.2956. On every one of those readings the population is
undersampled rather than exhausted.
What that does not establish is that mining harder would raise the ceiling. The shortfall
Section 3 reports is transformation types the corpus does not contain, and no template mined
from a corpus can supply a type absent from it. The two statements are compatible and only
the first was measured; the manuscript claims only the first.

S21

Model architectures and training

S21.1

Generator

The generator estimates P (r | s), the probability that rule r applies to substrate s, for
the whole bank in one forward pass. Substrate graphs carry 16-dimensional node and 18dimensional edge features. A three-layer GATv2 encoder (16 → 192 → 256 → 128, batch
normalisation, ReLU, dropout 0.1, mean-pooled readout) embeds the substrate, and a parallel
multilayer perceptron embeds a 1024-bit Morgan fingerprint, the two combined by a gated
fusion. Each rule is embedded independently by a rule-graph encoder of the same family
(128 → 192 → 128) alongside a six-dimensional branch of global rule features.
The logit sums four learned terms, each with its own learned scalar: a cross-attention
term in which the rule embedding queries the substrate’s atom states through single-head
S38

attention; a global term, the cosine similarity between substrate and rule embeddings; a local
term, the cosine between the attention context and the rule; and a term reflecting whether
the rule’s SMARTS matches. To these are added a per-rule bias and a frequency-prior term
at weight 0.4, the log-odds that the rule yields a true metabolite estimated from training
statistics. A penalty of 7.5 is subtracted for rules whose SMARTS does not match, which
masks inapplicable rules before the sigmoid.
Training is multi-label rule classification with a margin-ranking auxiliary. Every setting
either component was trained under is in Table S18, read from the configuration each run
recorded beside its weights rather than transcribed. Both released checkpoints come from
one run, the one whose label matrix was built in the firing convention, and that is established
rather than recalled: scoring a pool’s own candidates with every trained checkpoint held here
reproduces its generator scores under that run’s generator and no other, and its filter scores
under that run’s filter and no other (Section S34).

S21.2

The gate the release applied and the measurement did not

One of the settings in that table describes a selection no number here is measured under, and
it is worth separating from the ones that are. The generator checkpoint carries a calibrated
rule threshold of 0.6, a gate that drops a rule scoring below it before the rule budget is
applied. Every pool this paper is scored on is built with no such gate: the selection is the
top of the score ranking cut at the budget, and nothing else.
The two are not the same selection. On the comparison set a median of 6 rules clear the
gate against a budget of 30, and it binds on 185 of 291 substrates, 63.6%. Because both
orderings are by the same score, the gated set is a prefix of the ungated one wherever the
gate binds, so it removes candidates the evaluated arm has and never adds one. A caller
who ran under the gate would therefore be running a narrower configuration than any arm
reported here, and no number in this paper describes it.
The release is the evaluated configuration: an unspecified rule threshold means no gate,
S39

and the checkpoint keeps the value so a caller who wants the gate can pass it. The same
holds for the evaluation path a reader would use to select a configuration of their own, since
selecting on a setting the release does not run is the same defect one layer up. A regression
test holds all three together, because a released default that differs from an evaluated one is
invisible to every other check here and is the defect this paper argues about.

S21.3

Filter

The filter scores a (substrate, candidate) pair. Substrate and candidate are embedded
independently, each through its own three-layer GATv2 encoder of the same dimensions with
a mean-pooled readout. The two 128-dimensional embeddings are concatenated with both
Morgan fingerprints, and the resulting 2304-dimensional vector is mapped through a threelayer perceptron (→ 128 → 64 → 1) to a single logit. The estimator is selectable between a
non-negative positive-unlabelled risk on logits and binary cross-entropy on probabilities; the
released configuration uses the second.
A variant encoding one merged substrate–product graph, with cross-edges placed by
an element-aware maximum-common-substructure correspondence, was implemented and
measured. It loses on accuracy (recall@15 0.357 against 0.366, paired difference −0.0090,
95% CI [−0.017, −0.001], n = 1,170) and on cost (34.0 ms against 9.1 ms per pair), and is
not deployed.

S21.4

How the incomplete annotation is handled

An annotated metabolite is a positive and a rule-applicable product without an annotation is
unlabelled, not negative, because a missing annotation does not establish that a transformation
does not occur S5 . The two learned components answer that differently, and the released
configuration of each is what follows.
The generator down-weights rules that match the substrate but carry no annotation, by
half rather than treating them as negatives, so the label semantics reach the objective directly.
S40

The filter does not: it can be trained under a non-negative positive-unlabelled risk, and the
released checkpoint is trained under cross-entropy instead, so its unlabelled pairs enter as
negatives. The consequence is that its scores are conservative on exactly the candidates the
annotation is silent about. The same silence makes precision pessimistic by a bounded but
unknown amount, which is why the paper reports it without using it to order systems.
That leaves an ablation this work’s own framing asks for and does not report: the same
filter trained under the positive-unlabelled risk instead, scored the same way. It is not a
re-ranking of stored scores, which is why it is absent rather than deferred; it needs a training
run at the deployed configuration and a re-scoring of every pool from it. That is the same
run the retraining spread needs (Section 6), so one campaign would settle both, and neither
is settled here. What can be said without it is which estimator the release uses and in which
direction its bias runs, and both are stated above.

S22

Whether the site a prediction names is the site
that changed

Every prediction carries the template that produced it and the atoms that template fired on,
and the main text offers that as a reason to prefer this system. A rule can reach the right
product from the wrong place, so the claim is testable and had not been tested.
For every candidate the generator produces whose key matches an annotated reference,
the atoms fired on are compared with the atoms that differ between substrate and reference.
The comparison is made before the filter and before any budget, so what is measured is
localisation and not ranking. Of 327 such matches on the comparison set, the reported site
touches the change in 92.3% and lies wholly inside it in 46.2%. The second figure is lower for
a reason that is not a failure: a template matches its whole pattern, which is larger than the
part it alters.
What the change is, exactly, is inferred and not annotated. The corpus records a substrate
S41

and a product and no site, so the reference centre here is derived: the common substructure
of the two is computed, the substrate atoms it does not cover are taken as changed, the
substrate atoms carrying a part of the product the substructure does not cover are added,
and the neighbours of a removed atom with them. That is a structural inference. What this
establishes is agreement between the site a prediction names and the change it claims to
explain, not agreement with an experimentally determined site of metabolism, for which this
corpus has no labels.
The figure needs a floor, because both sets are subsets of the same molecule and on a
small substrate with a large centre they would meet whatever the rule did. Two floors are
measured. A set of atoms of the same size drawn uniformly touches the centre 50.5% of the
time, so the reported sites exceed it by 41.8%. That null is the wrong shape: a template
match is a connected fragment, not a scatter. Growing a fragment of the same size along
bonds from a random seed atom touches the centre 35.2% of the time, and the reported sites
exceed that by 57.1%.
The connected null is the lower of the two, which is the opposite of what we expected and
worth saying: a scattered set of atoms reaches into more of the molecule than a fragment
clustered in one place, so it meets a given centre more often. Against the null the comparison
actually needs, the margin is wider than the uniform one suggested. The reading remains
that a raw agreement rate near nine in ten is not the size of the effect; the size is the margin,
and it is 0.5715.

S23

What a single step is

The paper’s predictions are single-step in the sense that one template is applied once. Whether
that is also one enzymatic step depends on the templates, and it is not always.
Every mined template comes from an annotated substrate–product pair, so the question
can be put to molecules rather than to templates. Two instruments do so, and neither alone

S42

is enough.

The first instrument: how many loci. The reaction centre of the source pair, located
by the same maximum-common-substructure route the transformation types use, falls in
more than one piece for 522 of the 5,855 mined templates, 8.9%. One mined template could
not be scored, so the denominator throughout this section is 5,855 and not 5,856. A centre
in two pieces is one event when a bond appears or disappears between them, which is what
a cleavage or a cyclisation looks like, and two independent edits otherwise; this instrument
counts the pieces and does not separate the two cases. It has a blind spot in the other
direction as well, and the blind spot is the larger problem: two edits that share or neighbour
centre atoms arrive as one piece and are counted once. This share is therefore a floor and not
a bound.

The second instrument, and its bar, fixed first. The remedy is a second lens with no
threshold chosen after the fact, so the threshold was registered as H16 before the instrument
was written. On the same pair, let core be the matched atoms and count E: every bond
between two core atoms whose presence or order differs, plus every attachment point at which
a substituent is gained or lost, each once. Bonds internal to an added or removed fragment
are not edits to the substrate and are not counted, so a glucuronide costs one and not twelve.
The cut is set from chemistry rather than from a histogram: a conjugation breaks one bond
and forms one, E = 2; an epoxide hydrolysis reaches E = 3; an aromatic hydroxylation
through the arene oxide within one enzyme reaches E = 4 at worst. Nothing one enzyme
does in one turnover reaches five, so the threshold is E ≥ 5.

What the two find together. The second instrument flags 903 templates, 15.4%. The
two agree on only 130, which is the point of having both: each sees most of what the other
misses. Their union is 1,295 templates, 22.1% of the mined templates. The registered bar was
13.4% and the prediction is confirmed. The composite share of the mined bank is therefore

S43

about a fifth, not the 8.9% one instrument reports, and both counts are reported here, so
that the contribution of the instrument itself is visible.
Inspection of the flagged pairs finds both kinds. Among the composites are a pair whose
product carries two new carbonyls on one piperazine ring, a pair in which a ketone is reduced
and a pyridine nitrogen oxidised, and a pair gaining sixteen heavy atoms as a glutathione
conjugate after a substitution. None of these is one enzyme acting once.
The consequence for the paper is confined and stated where it bites: the coverage ceiling is
a one-template ceiling. A rule that performs two edits can reach a metabolite two steps away
in one application, so the ceiling is not the reach of one enzymatic step, and the depth-two
experiment in the Limitations measures a second template application rather than a second
enzymatic step. The corrected share also explains that experiment’s small effect. If a fifth
of the mined bank already performs two edits in one application, as P10 finds, a second
application has little left to discover that the first did not already reach, and the observed
lift of about 0.012 is what that predicts rather than a surprise.
The instrument reads the source pairs and not the templates, and that is deliberate: a
cleavage whose bridging atom carries no map number is invisible to a comparison of mapped
bonds, and a cyclisation joins two distant loci in a single event, so parsing partially mapped
SMIRKS answers a different question from the one asked and answers it differently from
what the molecules show.

S24

How much of the annotation is one enzymatic step

Section S23 measures how many mined templates perform more than one enzymatic edit and
draws the consequence for the coverage bound. That measurement is over templates. The
question a reader of a recall figure has is about the references, because a corpus recording a
metabolite several steps from its parent asks a predictor for something no single application
can be, and counts a hit when one template stands in for the lot.

S44

The same two instruments are therefore applied to every annotated pair of the evaluated
test set. Of the 2,575 pairs that can be typed, 248 have a reaction centre falling in more
than one connected locus and 192 are composite by core-incident edit count at the registered
threshold; their union is 404, 15.7% of the annotation.
Nothing in that makes a composite reference unreachable, and it is worth being clear
about why: a template mined from a composite pair performs the whole transformation in
one application, which is how the bank reaches many of them and why the ceiling is as high
as it is. What the figure settles is what a recall number means. Roughly one annotated
metabolite in six is not a single enzymatic step, so the same is true of roughly one hit in six,
and a reader who takes recall for the share of one-step chemistry a system finds will be wrong
by about that much.

Group ranking and its oracle

Candidates group naturally by molecular formula, and an oracle over those groups motivated
three of the registered predictions. Table S19 recomputes that oracle under four partitions
of one candidate pool, on the deployed configuration rather than on the uncapped pool the
original measurement used.

How the two failures decompose. The listwise scorer applied to whole groups (P6)
measured −0.1188 against a registered +0.05. The deficit splits into −0.0902 from the design,
block emission being worse than interleaved (0.4376 against 0.5023), and −0.0286 from the
model itself. Because the oracle above is measured in blocked form, 0.3 of the headroom
it promises is the return of what the design spends. The gate before fusion (P7) measured

ranking through the gate gives −0.018, worse than the generator cap it replaces.
S46

Why the oracle is not an upper bound on the signal. A binary group ordering entered

S27.1

Ties in the deployed ranking

The deployed configuration caps the pool by rule score before the fusion sees it. On a pool
smaller than the cap that truncates nothing, but it fixes the order the fusion reads, and that
matters because the fusion has exact ties: for 14 of the 291 substrates the two best candidates
receive identical fused scores, so which of them is returned first is decided by the order the
pool arrives in rather than by the ranking rule. Fusing the pool as stored instead of after the
cap moves micro recall at k = 1 from 0.1053 to 0.1068, a difference of 0.0015. Every arm of
every ablation here uses the deployed convention, so the differences reported are differences
of method and not of tie-breaking.

S28

When a ranking comparison can measure anything

Two experiments in this work asked whether a ranking beats an alternative ordering of the
same pool, and the first of them could not have answered. It compared the product ordering
against Tanimoto similarity on a pool whose median size is 8 candidates, at a budget of 15.
For 887 of 1,170 substrates the entire pool fits inside the budget, so every ordering returns
the same set and no arm can separate from another. The measured difference, +0.0024 with
the interval covering zero, is what that design is able to produce and not evidence that the
two orderings are equivalent.
The same limit governs a null model of the form used elsewhere in this literature, a
uniform draw of min(k, N ) from a method’s own pool of N . Its lift tends to one as the budget
approaches the pool, so at k = 15 a method emitting about eleven candidates has a lift of

S48

1.055 whatever its ranking is, while a method emitting eighty still has room. Comparing
methods by lift at a fixed budget therefore ranks them partly by how much they emit, which
is the same confound the budget sweep in the main text is designed to expose.
The rule both cases give is simple and we state it because neither experiment stated it
in advance: a ranking comparison carries information only over the substrates whose pool
exceeds the budget, and the number of such substrates should be reported with the result.
The comparison in the main text is run on the deployed pool, capped at 100, where the
budget is narrower than the pool for every substrate.

S32

Runtime methodology

Correcting the speed-up for machine load. The bounded and unbounded arms did
not run under the same load. The enumeration step is identical between them, so the ratio
of its medians, 1.73, measures load alone. Applying that correction to the step the budget
changes, standardising the survivors, whose medians are 11.88 s unbounded against 2.73 s
under the budget, leaves 2.52× attributable to the budget itself. Carried to the whole arm it
puts the median at 9.12 s under the other arm’s load, inside the registered target.

A safeguard the budget needed. The tautomer enumeration budget lives in a modulelevel singleton shared with the matching key, so lowering it would have moved what counts
as a hit at the same time as it moved the cost. A private enumerator was introduced for the
bounded arm and the match keys were verified unchanged before the run.
Table 1 prints “did not finish” for the exhaustive mode’s slowest substrate and never gives
the rate, in a paper that says a service must publish the tail as well as the median. The

S52

rate is 11 of 106 sampled validation substrates, 10.4%, at a deadline of 600 seconds. It is
a validation-timing figure at that deadline and not a property of the comparison. The two
are not in conflict: the timing sweep kills a worker that exceeds the deadline, and the run
that produced the predictions imposes none, so on the test split the same arm was allowed
to finish and records 0 failures over 1,170 substrates. What the rate says is how often the
exhaustive mode would miss a ten-minute deadline, not how often it fails.
Every timing in the paper is a cold measurement. A cache makes a repeated substrate
run 16 to 47 times faster, so any timing not taken cold measures a mixture of two regimes.
Standardisation is the binding constraint on the generator, at 94 to 99% of cold generator
time. Three other candidates were tested against it and rejected by measurement: the pairwise
filter’s maximum-common-substructure step, which does not bind because the generator
outruns the filter a hundred to one; rule application, which applies all 7,580 parseable
templates to a 109-atom molecule in 5.2 s, yielding 20,655 products at no more than 0.58 s per
rule; and substrate size as an axis, which fails as a predictor because two 43-atom substrates
take 64.7 s and no completion respectively.
Two of the registered predictions address the constraint. Moving standardisation to
surviving candidates alone (P8) reduces enumeration by a factor of 13.9, but standardising
the hundred survivors then becomes 72% of the new arm, so the whole arm gains only 2.95×
against a registered 10×: removing 94 to 99% of the work does not remove 94 to 99% of the
time. Adding a tautomer enumeration budget of 200 passes both halves of its prediction.
That budget lives in a module-level singleton shared with the tautomer matching key, so
setting it globally would silently move what counts as a hit. It is applied through a private
enumerator instead: the global setting is untouched, and matching keys on 96 molecules are
identical with and without it.

Comparators

SyGMa 1.1.0 is taken from the installed module and run with the scenario [[phase1, 1],
[phase2, 1]], one cycle of each ruleset, which is the worked example in that version’s own
documentation. It is stated because it is the one comparator knob that sets how many
candidates a system may emit, and a paper arguing that such a knob must be declared should
declare it.
That scenario is a knob of exactly the kind this paper asks other work to declare, so it is
swept rather than only stated. SyGMa’s engine composes: phase II fires on the products of
phase I, so the deployed scenario is a two-step run and not one application of each ruleset.
Applying each ruleset once to the parent and composing nothing reaches 0.5325 of the
references against 0.5391 for the deployed scenario, so the composition step buys +0.0065,
95% CI [0.0038, 0.0096]. Against this work’s own uncapped single pass the difference is 0.278
at the deployed setting and 0.2846 depth-matched: the comparison of the two banks does
not turn on which of the two SyGMa is run at.
The other direction is the one that bears on the only wide-budget lead this work still
claims, and it is swept too. Running SyGMa at two cycles of phase I against one of phase II
takes its mean emission from 73 candidates to 526.1, more than five times what this work’s
exhaustive arm returns, and its recall at a budget of 50 from 0.5113 to 0.5729. The lead
survives it: +0.1293 [+0.0837, +0.1755] against +0.1910 [+0.1518, +0.2308] at the deployed
setting, both intervals excluding zero. 5 substrates did not finish the deeper scenario inside a
ninety-second deadline and keep their deployed lists, which can only favour SyGMa’s arm in
the comparison rather than harm it. That deadline is wall-clock, so this count and the mean
emission it produces depend on how loaded the machine is: a re-run on a quieter machine
finished a substrate that had previously timed out, which lowers the count and raises the
mean emission, since that substrate then contributes the deeper scenario’s longer list. Every
recall, every gap and every interval in this sweep was identical across the two runs. The fields
a re-run may move are named in the artifact, so a check holds the rest to equality rather

than reading any movement as noise.

MetaPredictor’s beam, swept the same way. The other comparator whose emission is
a declared setting is MetaPredictor, and its flatness from k = 15 is the clearest case in this
work of a budget that has stopped measuring ranking: 273 of its 291 lists are shorter than
that budget. Decoding the same substrates at a wider beam takes its mean output from 10.7
candidates to 34.2 and its recall from 0.4797 at both thirty and fifty to 0.5925 and 0.603; at
fifteen it reaches 0.5158. Decoding at the wider setting lost none of the substrates, so the
two rows are scored on the same population under the same criterion, and the prediction
equal to the substrate is dropped from both.
What that does to the margin is the point of running it. At thirty the exhaustive arm leads
by +0.1729 [+0.1263, +0.2188] at the deployed beam and by +0.0602 [+0.0184, +0.1024] at the
wide one, both excluding zero. At fifteen the deployed reading is +0.0556 [+0.0149, +0.0955]
and the wide one is +0.0195 [−0.0243, +0.0625], which does not exclude zero. So the headof-list margin against MetaPredictor is a statement about the beam it was run at, and the
depth margin is not.
Both rows are printed rather than the one that favours this work. A paper arguing that
an undeclared emission budget reorders a published table cannot report its own comparison
at one budget and call the ordering a property of the systems, and the matched-length
control reaches the same verdict from the other direction by truncating the longer list rather
than lengthening the shorter one. The two settings are the tool’s own documented decode
configurations, not settings invented here.
The comparison is reported at the documentation default because that is the setting a
user gets, and the sweep is reported because a paper asking others to declare an emission
knob has to turn its own.
Only two of the four comparators expose a version string at all. SyGMa reports 1.1.0
from the installed package and BioTransformer 3.0 carries the build stamp 20230525 in its

S55

jar, whose digest is pinned. MetaTox is a web service that publishes no version to a user,
and MetaPredictor is a source checkout with no release tag. For those two the record is
what can be pinned rather than what would be convenient to claim: the exact submission
set, the returned predictions frozen per substrate, their digests, and the date each entered
this repository, which bounds when the run happened. Those dates are 2026-07-25 for
MetaTox and 2026-07-13 for MetaPredictor, against 2026-07-27 and 2026-07-13 for the other
two. A comparison against a service cannot be made reproducible by the party running the
comparison; declaring what it can pin, and naming what it cannot, is the whole of what is
available, and the shortfall belongs in the record rather than out of it.
What the MetaTox arm is, item by item: a single file of 291 structures submitted to the
service on 2026-07-25, and the file it returned, frozen per substrate and pinned by digest.
That is the whole of what this repository holds about the run. The service publishes no
version to a user, the returned file records no setting, and no setting was chosen by us, so
nothing further about how those predictions were produced can be established from what is
held here. That is a shortfall and it is named as one rather than filled in: this paper argues
that a comparator’s emission knob must be declared, and for the incumbent it sets out to
replace it declares a submission and a response and no knob. It is also why the arm cannot
be re-run here and why the sweep applied to SyGMa’s scenario has no counterpart for it.
What can be said beyond that is about review and not about settings, and the two should
not be confused. The first author of MetaTox S7 is a co-author of this paper and read the
arm it is compared in, so the question to ask of any comparison, whether the comparator
was run in a configuration its own authors would recognise, has been put to one of them
for the incumbent this work sets out to replace. That is a fact about the method and not a
disclosure: the paper argues for replacing MetaTox, so any interest the relationship could
create runs against this work rather than for it.
GLORYx is not a column here, and the honest reason is that its predictions on these
substrates were not obtained: no distribution of it is held in this repository and none was

S56

sought. Whether it could have been run is a question about effort and about its interface
that we did not settle, so the absence is not defended as unavoidable. Its reference set is also
one of the four sources this corpus is assembled from, so its development data sits inside our
corpus, and Section 2.1 states that such overlap flatters a comparator. That second fact is
not what excludes it. BioTransformer stands in the same relation to MetXBioDB, 61.3% of
whose pairs are inside this corpus, and it carries a column with its advantage stated and
uncorrected; excluding one system for an overlap while seating another with a comparable
one would be a rule applied in whichever direction suited the result. The overlap is therefore
reported for both and corrected for neither, and GLORYx’s absence stands on obtainability
alone. It is a standard arm in this journal’s recent benchmarks of this tool class S8 , so the
absence is a real weakness of this comparison rather than a neutral one, and it is recorded as
such in the manuscript’s Limitations.
BioTransformer is pinned by digest rather than by version string, because “3.0” names
several builds: jar c70cad91, 115,415,197 bytes, upstream commit 6432cf88 of 2023-05-25,
its 994 templates loaded at freeze time rather than taken from a constant. That count is
every template the jar loads across all its rule databases and is not the one Section S15
partitions on: the core rule file this repository reads holds 668 of them, and 611 of those are
in this bank character for character.
It carries a column. Supplying no ranked predictions would not have excluded it, since
that does not separate it from MetaPredictor, whose list is also unranked and shorter than the
budget on nearly every substrate. Running it takes one thing worth recording for anyone who
tries: the native InChI library its distribution caches is built for one processor architecture,
so on another the tool aborts at load, and an x86-64 Java 8 runtime resolves it. It is the
comparator a reader of the rule bank most wants, since 611 of its templates are inside that
bank, so its column measures how much of this work’s reach is BioTransformer’s reach under
another name.
It is run at allHuman for one step, which is one application of its own rules and the closest

S57

counterpart to what this work’s arms do; it is not a claim that every arm is allowed the same
enzymatic depth, since SyGMa’s deployed scenario composes a phase-one and a phase-two
ruleset and a mined template may itself perform more than one edit. Its file order is its
ranking, which is the treatment MetaPredictor’s unranked list already receives. It emits 10.7
candidates per substrate and returns nothing for 7 of 291. The exhaustive arm leads it by
+0.2060 [+0.1678, +0.2478] at a budget of thirty. Cut to BioTransformer’s own list length on
every substrate, the same control the other comparators receive, the difference is −0.0045
[−0.0402, +0.0315] over 10.7 slots, an interval covering zero and running the other way. The
wide-budget gap against BioTransformer is therefore an emission gap and not an ordering
one, which is what a bank sharing 611 of its templates should produce.
Two things follow for the reading of the rest. The declared family the family-wise
correction is computed over names its three comparators and was fixed before this arm
existed, so it is unchanged: BioTransformer’s contrasts are reported with their own intervals
and are not folded into a correction declared without them. A family fixed in advance is not
widened after the fact, which is the whole of what declaring one buys, and the objection that
the paper then prints more tests than its correction covers is answered by measurement rather
than by the argument: running the same Holm procedure over all 72 contrasts the paper
prints removes one of the cells the declared family licenses, the exhaustive arm’s trail against
SyGMa at a budget of five, which is a trail and not a lead and is reported either way. And
the deeper scenario is not swept for this comparator as it is for SyGMa. That asymmetry
was decided after the matched-length control had been read, which is worth stating because
it is exactly the kind of choice this work asks other papers to declare: the control removes
list length as a variable and finds no separation, so a scenario that emits more would narrow
a gap this paper does not claim as an ordering result and would change no verdict here. A
reader who judges that insufficient should read the absence as unforced.
Its templates are also measured for reach, which is a different quantity from its recall,
on the same 150-substrate subset drawn for the earlier MetaTrans evaluation (Section S29):

they reach 0.4786 of the references there, 95% CI [0.4138, 0.5495], against 7,581 templates of
ours reaching 0.7014. A reach figure and a ranked recall are not the same quantity and they
are not placed in one table.
The substrate was handed to BioTransformer as the corpus stores it, as it was to SyGMa
and MetaPredictor. Handed the natural tautomer instead, the same arm reaches 0.4451
against 0.4466, a difference of −0.0015 at a budget of thirty. That is the second comparator
for which both drawings could be run and the two disagree in direction: the drawing that
costs SyGMa recall costs BioTransformer nothing, which is what a tool that standardises its
own input would do.
Four systems published in 2025–26 could not be run on our split as released. LAGOM S9
releases training code under Apache-2.0 but no fine-tuned checkpoint, so comparing would
mean comparing against our own reimplementation. DeepMetab S10 releases weights and
site prediction but its reaction module was withdrawn by the author for copyright reasons,
leaving only Python 3.7 bytecode. DeepCYP S11 offers a web form and no models or API, so
a comparison would mean submitting substrates one at a time through a form.
MetaTox is also a service rather than a package and was run, so the rule that separates
them has to be stated and it is a weak one. It is not reproducibility: neither service’s settings
are recorded here, and Section S33 says as much for MetaTox. It is not availability: both
are reachable. It is the cost of submission, that MetaTox accepts a file of 291 structures
and returns a file where DeepCYP takes one substrate at a time. That is a statement about
our effort and not about either system, and it is the kind of reason this paper asks other
work not to leave implicit, so it is written here for a reader to weigh. A reader who judges it
insufficient should read DeepCYP’s absence as unforced, and the same applies to GLORYx,
whose distribution was not sought. Metabolite-Gen S12 is a preprint with no repository we
could find. The list is closed by a rule: a comparator that could not be obtained is reported
as unavailable with its reason, is not dropped silently, and the list is not extended after the
first run.

results/four method 291.json defines the comparison population, gates the MetaTox
column and carries the deployed interactive arm from the pools the comparison itself is read
from, reproducing Table 2 in every cell of both. It also records, beside that arm, the one it
replaced: an early dump ranked by the product of the two component scores over a narrower
pool, which reached 0.2677 at k = 50. No number in this paper derives from that column,
and it is kept rather than deleted so a reader who meets it in an older copy can tell which
arm they are looking at. Table 2 is supplied by deployment table.json, which carries a
gate asserting the two agree on MetaTox.
107 artifacts are pinned. Most record the script that wrote them, the SHA-256 of that
script’s source, and the commit it ran at; a sweep recomputes the hash of each producer and
refuses the artifact if that producer has changed. 106 of the 107 are current.
A pinned producer is not the same as a pinned model. Candidate pools record both
checkpoints that scored them with their digests; the pool sets frozen before that field existed
carry no such record, and for those the run is established by reproduction: their generator
and filter scores are reproduced exactly by one run’s checkpoints and by no other pair held
here, so both stages of the released system come from one run. The analysis scripts default to
it, a test refuses any producer of a pinned artifact that names another, and the gate over the
artifacts the manuscript’s numbers come from fails on any that names a checkpoint outside
it, because a default that is not the deployed setting reproduces something nobody ran. That
test reads the shell scripts too, and the two ways a path is written in them, which is how it
now sees the one that built the standardised pools: it named the filter of a run this repository
no longer ships, so the sweep over how the substrate is drawn compared two models rather
than two drawings, and its figures here are from the rebuild.
The guarantee that matters is narrower than the pinned set and is checked by a gate
rather than asserted. The numbers in this paper come from 87 of those artifacts, established
by running the number generator with its artifact reader instrumented, so the count is of files
S60

actually opened and not of matches in the source. Of the 87, the sweep finds none unpinned
and none whose producer it cannot identify. Of the rest, six predate stamping and are verified
by recovering their producer’s source from the history rather than from a recorded digest,
which the sweep labels as inferred; that is a weaker check and is counted separately for that
reason. A further three are exempt with a stated reason, each an output of the checking
apparatus itself, which cannot verify its own inputs without circularity.
Which model produced an artifact is checked separately, because a producer whose source
has not moved can still have been pointed at a checkpoint nobody deployed, and one was:
the worked example was ranked by a filter from a neighbouring training run for as long as the
example existed, and its own artifact said so in plain text. 0 of the 87 now name a checkpoint
outside the deployed run, and the gate fails on any that do. That check has a blind spot
worth naming: an artifact that records no checkpoint passes it without being examined. For
one of the 87 the identity is on the record even though that file does not carry it, established
the way the deployed pair itself was: scoring the artifact’s own candidates with every trained
checkpoint held here and finding that one reproduces them exactly and no other reproduces
them at all. 7 remain in the blind spot, their producer loading a model and nothing recording
which; for those the assurance is weaker and is of a different kind, that every such producer’s
default is the deployed pair and a test refuses any that names another. The gate fails the
build if any of the first two counts is not zero, and unpinning one artifact makes it fail, which
is how we know it can.
The identification itself had a blind spot of a different kind, and it is the one that let
the standardised pools through. It enumerated pool directories by a filename pattern and
reported on whichever ones matched, saying nothing about those that did not, so four of
the thirteen a published number is read from were never examined – among them the pool
the interactive arm’s whole column comes from, and both standardised pools. A check that
reports on what it finds and is silent about what is missing cannot fail on the thing that is
missing. It now refuses when a declared directory yields no shard it can read, the readers

S61

of those pools refuse a shard that does not record the released pair, and the invariant that
exposed it is checked on every run: a substrate the standardiser does not move is the same
molecule in both arms, so the two must return the same list on it.
Bringing those artifacts inside the guarantee meant re-running their producers, which is a
rerun and not a relabel, so what moved is reported. One number moved. The train split’s
clean triple count is 345,028 where the committed artifact recorded three more: three records
of train.sdf that the RDKit version used here no longer parses, which the loader reports
and which affect no substrate, no annotated pair and no figure in this paper. Every other
field of every re-run artifact is byte-identical to what was committed, including the leakage
audit’s overlap counts, the contamination audit and the population-defining comparison.
That is the evidence that the unpinned state was harmless, and it is worth having in a form
stronger than an assurance.
Two limits of the apparatus are ours to state. First, and materially: the stamp digests the
producer script’s own bytes and nothing else, so a change anywhere in the package (the code
that computes the tautomer keys, the matcher, the recall) invalidates no artifact and trips
no gate. The split manifest pins the data and the sweep pins the producer; between them
sits the library, and it is pinned by neither. Second, and narrowly: most stamped artifacts
record a dirty working tree at write time. Verification resolves by the recorded source digest,
which is independent of the commit, so the producer is identified regardless; what the flag
says is that the surrounding tree was not clean, not that the producer is unknown.
The guarantee also covers the pinned set and not the directory: of 508 files under results/
and everything below it, 283 carry no stamp and 38 were written by a producer that has
since changed. None of those is among the 87 the numbers come from, which the gate is
what establishes. The sweep reaches below the top level because most of what a published
number is actually made of lives there: the candidate pools are written one shard at a time
into their own directories, and a sweep that did not enter them would leave the files a curve
is assembled from outside every check it makes. An unstamped file there is not therefore

S62

unaccounted for. A producer records the files it read by path and digest, so the guarantee
extends one hop past the pinned set: 65 of the files below the top level are named as an input
by a pinned artifact, and a change to any of them makes that artifact fail rather than pass.
A split manifest pins by digest the triple files, the substrate sets, the evaluated test set,
the rule bank and every third-party artifact, and a verify mode recomputes and names any
leaf that has moved.
Numbers reach the page through macros generated from pinned artifacts: 387 distinct
macros in the manuscript and 542 here. The claim is not that every number does. 37
measurements are typed by hand, most of them in this document, and they are enumerated
in the checker itself rather than folded into a wildcard, so a number typed by hand that
is not already on that list still fails the check, and the list is the exact statement of what
remains to be wired. A checker refuses any other numeric literal in either document, refuses
any hand-typed pointer between the two documents in either direction, and writes the three
counts this paragraph quotes. Every such pointer resolves through xr against the other
document’s own labels, so a wrong one prints ?? and fails the build rather than compiling
silently. A hand-typed pointer cannot: nine of them here were wrong at once when the tables
were reordered, one badly enough to direct a search to the wrong table, which is why none is
permitted.
One file in the repository holds source structures rather than keys: the evaluated test
substrates, released as SMILES because reproducing the comparison means running a predictor
on one. Their annotated metabolites are not released that way. A single key would have fixed
the matching criterion at that key, since canonical equality, the stereochemistry-blind first
block and Tanimoto similarity each need the reference structure, and the sweep this paper is
largely about could then be reproduced under one criterion only. What is released instead
is the set of descriptors those criteria are decided by, one per reference, which is enough
for all 5 of them and is not the structure (Section S1). reproduce from descriptors.py
rebuilds the whole comparison from them under every criterion, reading no corpus structure

S63

at any point, and its default-criterion column matches the one the manuscript prints at every
budget. The substrates are the residue of the problem and the choice to ship them is stated
here rather than settled by the file’s existence.
The train and validation candidate pools are deposited at [ZENODO DOI]. Every
substrate and every annotated metabolite in them is replaced by its tautomer-canonical
InChIKey before the archive is built, so the deposit holds no source structure; the candidate
structures beside the keys are this work’s own output and are released as they are. The
transform is part of the deposit tool and its counts are recorded in the manifest (how many
substrates went in, how many came out, how many could not be keyed and how many keys
collided) because a silent drop there would be a silent loss of population. The manifest also
pins the SHA-256 of both the archive and the file inside it together with the record counts, so
a download can be verified against the version used here. The tool refuses to build an archive
from a pool whose substrates were not keyed, and refuses to publish one at all without a
licence: a Zenodo record cannot be withdrawn once published, so the last gate before one
exists is that it carries nothing that cannot be redistributed.
